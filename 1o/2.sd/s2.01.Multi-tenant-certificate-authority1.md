**What this says in plain words**
You are answering as an engineer who owns one clearly-bordered piece of infrastructure end to end — here, the certificate service, not the whole identity platform. There are two tiers of answer in the source. `IC3 baseline` is the floor: say all of it or you have not answered. `IC4 stretch` is extra depth you add when the interviewer pushes. 3. **The stretch is additive.** An `IC4 stretch` answer restates the baseline and then adds the harder case. If your stretch contradicts your baseline, the interviewer concludes you did not have a position.
4. **Never cite the labels to the interviewer.** Saying "this is the IC4 answer" claims knowledge of their rubric that you do not have.

---

> - Why this is hard
>   - Naive: `openssl` on a box, certificates in a bucket, a wiki page saying "renew yearly", private keys on disk.

**What this says in plain words**
The obvious first design: one server with the `openssl` command-line tool, a CA private key sitting as a file on that server's disk, issued certificates dropped into a storage bucket, and a note on a wiki reminding someone to renew them once a year.

**Why it matters**
This is the design the interviewer expects you to name in 20 seconds and then attack. Naming it first earns you the right to say "and here are the three ways it breaks". Most real companies ran exactly this at some point, so the interviewer has probably lived the breaks.

---

> - First break: expiry is a time bomb with `100%` probability
>   - A forgotten renewal is a hard outage of every dependent service (canonical pattern).

**What this says in plain words**
A certificate carries an end date. When that date passes, every client checking it will refuse to connect. Unlike a disk failure or a network partition, this failure is not probabilistic: it will happen, on a known date, unless someone acts before then.

**Why it matters**

- Worked example (illustrative)
  - An internal gateway certificate was issued on 2025-03-01 with a 1-year lifetime.
  - The engineer who set the wiki reminder left the company in August.
  - On 2026-03-01 at 00:00 UTC, 40 services that call the gateway over TLS start failing every request at once.
  - Nothing was deployed, nothing crashed, no alarm about CPU or memory fires. The on-call engineer spends the first 25 minutes looking for a code change that does not exist.
- "Canonical pattern" means this exact story has happened publicly many times (Microsoft Teams in 2020, Spotify in 2016, and others). The interviewer knows it. Your design has to make it structurally impossible, not just "less likely".

---

> - Second break: key custody
>   - Any copy of a CA private key outside an HSM is a compromise story, and a leaked CA key means re-issuing the world.

**What this says in plain words**
The CA's private key is the one secret that everything depends on. If it exists as a file, as bytes in a process's memory, or in a backup, it can be copied. Once copied, an attacker can sign certificates for any name, and the only fix is to stop trusting that key everywhere and issue new certificates for every service under it.

**Why it matters**

- Worked example (illustrative)
  - The CA key lives on disk on the `openssl` box. A backup of that box goes to a shared backup bucket.
  - A contractor with bucket read access copies the backup. Nobody notices.
  - Six months later, a forged certificate for `payments.internal` is seen in the wild.
  - Remediation: create a new CA, push its public key to every trust store on 10,000 hosts, re-issue 1,000,000 leaf certificates, and confirm every consumer reloaded — with no way to know how many other forged certificates exist.
- An HSM changes this. The key is generated inside the device and can never be read out. The service asks the HSM to *sign*, and gets back a signature, never the key. Custody stops being a matter of discipline and becomes a matter of hardware.

---

> - Third break: the service cannot force consumers to switch certificates
>   - Rotation only works if two versions overlap and consumers hot-reload.

**What this says in plain words**
When you issue a new certificate, the services that use it (load balancers, sidecars, gateways) have their own copies. The CA service cannot reach into their memory and swap it. It can only publish a new version and wait. So for the switch to be safe, the old and new versions must both be valid at the same time for a while, and the consumers must be able to pick up a new file without restarting.

**Why it matters**

- Worked example (illustrative)
  - You issue version 2 of a certificate and revoke version 1 the same minute.
  - Of 500 consumer processes, 480 reload within a minute; 20 are on a host whose file watcher crashed.
  - Those 20 now present a revoked certificate. Every client that checks revocation rejects them. That is a 4% outage caused by the rotation itself.
- With an overlap of 7 days, those 20 keep serving fine on version 1 while your telemetry shows "old serial still in use", and you fix the watcher before deprecating.

---

> - Minimum primitives
>   - HSM-held CA keys
>     - Sign inside the boundary.
>     - Never export plaintext CA private keys.
>   - Versioned certificates with overlapping validity
>   - Consumers that cache and hot-reload
>   - An append-only audit trail.

**What this says in plain words**
Four building blocks, and each one answers one of the breaks above:

1. **HSM-held CA keys.** The private key lives inside a tamper-resistant device. All signing happens inside it. The plaintext key never comes out. This answers the custody break.
2. **Versioned certificates with overlapping validity.** A "certificate" is a logical name; each renewal creates a new numbered version, and consecutive versions are both valid for a window. This answers the forced-switch break.
3. **Consumers that cache and hot-reload.** Every consumer keeps a local copy on disk and can swap to a new one without restarting. This answers both the forced-switch break and the "what if the CA is down" question that comes later.
4. **An append-only audit trail.** Every issue, rotate, and revoke writes a record that can never be edited or deleted. This is the compliance requirement and the forensic tool after a compromise.

**What breaks without it**
Drop any one and a break above reappears. No versioning ⇒ atomic swap ⇒ rotation outage. No hot-reload ⇒ restart storms. No audit ⇒ after a compromise you cannot say which certificates were legitimately issued.

---

> - What the user wants: "TLS just works
>   - Rotation never wakes me
>   - Compromise is containable."

**What this says in plain words**
The user is a service owner. Their three wishes: connections succeed without them thinking about certificates; renewal happens with no page at 3 a.m.; and if a key leaks, the damage stops at one tenant or one service rather than the whole company.

**Why it matters**
These three sentences are the acceptance test for every later decision. When you propose the overlap window, you can point at "rotation never wakes me". When you propose per-tenant subordinate CAs, you point at "compromise is containable". Tie decisions to these and the design sounds motivated instead of memorised.

---

> - 🗣 "This is a control-plane correctness problem with a data-plane survival constraint: low QPS, but every mistake is either an outage-by-timer or a security incident."

**What this says in plain words**
The quotable one-liner. The *control plane* is the part that issues and manages certificates; it handles very few requests per second (QPS) but must never be wrong. The *data plane* is the millions of TLS handshakes per second that use the certificates; it must keep working even when the control plane is down. And the two ways to fail are: a certificate expires on schedule (outage-by-timer), or a key leaks (security incident).

**Why it matters**
It tells the interviewer in one breath that you will *not* spend the hour on sharding and throughput, and that you know where the real risk is. Interviewers who hear "control plane vs data plane" in the first minute relax; it is the framing they use internally.

## Requirements — IC3 baseline

This section holds three things: the clarifying questions with assumed answers, the functional scope, and the numeric targets. Then it states the invariants (what must never be false) and the non-goals (what you refuse to build).

---

> - Numerical targets are interview assumptions unless an adjacent primary source states otherwise.

**What this says in plain words**
Every number below (1M certificates, 90-day lifetime, p99 under 2 seconds) is something you *assume* to make the design concrete. None of it is a measured Oracle figure unless a reference next to it says so.

**Why it matters**
Say "I'll assume" out loud before each number. The interviewer may change it ("make it 100M"), and you want to have shown that the design tolerates that change rather than having claimed the number was a fact.

---

> | Question | Assumed answer | Why it changes the design |
> |---|---|---|
> | Internal mTLS or public trust? | Internal-first (service identities); public endpoints via an external CA later | Private CA means no external rate limits and custom policy; public trust would add CA/Browser Forum rules and ACME |
> | Scale? | `1M` leaf certificates, `10k` consuming services, leaf TTL `90 d`, CA TTL `5 y` | Fixes renewal rate and shows this is not a throughput problem |
> | Who consumes the certificates? | Load balancers, gateways, sidecars; pull-and-cache model | Consumers must cache locally so handshakes never depend on this service |
> | Is revocation required? | Yes; CRL primary, OCSP later | Adds a signed-artifact publisher and a freshness SLO |
> | Compliance and audit? | Every issue, revoke, rotate audited; keys in FIPS 140-2 Level 3 HSMs | Forces append-only audit and HSM-only custody |
> | Who holds the leaf private key? | Requester (CSR flow) by default; service-managed key in the tenant's vault as an option | Decides whether the service ever touches private material |
> | Tenancy model? | OCI-style compartments; one tenant never sees another's resources | Drives per-tenant CAs, quotas, and `404`-not-`403` isolation |

**Row: Internal mTLS or public trust?**

- The question: will browsers on the public internet need to trust these certificates, or only your own services talking to each other with mutual TLS (mTLS, where both sides present a certificate)?
- The assumed answer: internal only, for now.
- Why it changes the design:
  1. A private CA answers only to you. No external issuer can rate-limit you, and you decide what names and lifetimes are allowed.
  2. Public trust would mean obeying the CA/Browser Forum baseline requirements (a rulebook browsers enforce: maximum lifetimes, mandatory revocation, audit logging to public transparency logs) and probably speaking ACME (the automated protocol Let's Encrypt uses). That is a different, much larger product.
- Worked example: with a private CA you can issue a certificate for the name `orders.svc.cluster.local` with a 90-day lifetime. A public CA would refuse that name outright because it is not a public DNS name.

**Row: Scale?**

- The assumed numbers: 1,000,000 leaf certificates, 10,000 services fetching them, each leaf lives 90 days, each CA lives 5 years.
- Why it changes the design: 1M leaves over 90 days gives the renewal rate (about 16,700 per day, computed in the next section). That number is small, which is the whole reason the interview goes to rotation instead of sharding.

**Row: Who consumes the certificates?**

- The consumers are the programs that terminate TLS: load balancers, API gateways, service-mesh sidecars.
- "Pull-and-cache" means the consumer fetches the bundle and keeps a copy; the service never pushes into the consumer's memory.
- Why it changes the design: a TLS handshake must never wait on this service. If it did, a control-plane outage would become a data-plane outage across 10,000 services.

**Row: Is revocation required?**

- Yes: you must be able to declare a certificate untrustworthy before its end date.
- CRL first (a signed list file), OCSP (an online status protocol) as a later addition.
- Why it changes the design: you now need a component that builds and signs the list, a place to publish it, and a target for how stale it may be (the freshness SLO — service level objective).

**Row: Compliance and audit?**

- Every mutation is recorded, and keys live in HSMs certified to FIPS 140-2 Level 3 (a U.S. government standard; Level 3 means the device resists physical tampering and does not release keys).
- Why it changes the design: the audit table must be append-only, and the service must store only a handle to the key, never the key.

**Row: Who holds the leaf private key?**

- Default: the requester generates its own key pair and sends a CSR (certificate signing request — the public key plus the requested names, signed with the private key as proof of possession). The service never sees the private key.
- Option: the service generates the key and stores it in the tenant's vault.
- Why it changes the design: in the default flow the service is never a custodian of any private material, which removes a whole class of risk. The managed option needs its own custody review.

**Row: Tenancy model?**

- Every resource belongs to a compartment (OCI's term for an isolation boundary inside a tenancy). Tenant A must not be able to learn that tenant B's resources exist.
- Why it changes the design: one subordinate CA per tenant, per-tenant quotas, and the rule that a request for another tenant's id returns `404` (not found) rather than `403` (forbidden), because `403` would confirm the id exists.

---

> - Functional scope
>   - Create a CA (root or subordinate) whose key is generated inside an HSM.
>   - Issue a leaf certificate from a CSR or a managed key specification.
>   - Renew and rotate with versioning and an overlap window.
>   - Revoke a version and publish a signed CRL.
>   - Serve the certificate, chain, and trust bundle by version with an ETag.
>   - List certificates expiring before a date for operational sweeps.

**What this says in plain words**
Six operations, and that is the whole product:

1. **Create a CA.** Either a root (self-signed, the top of the trust chain) or a subordinate (signed by a parent CA). The key pair is born inside the HSM.
2. **Issue a leaf.** Either from a CSR the caller supplies, or from a specification ("give me a P-256 key") that makes the service generate the key.
3. **Renew and rotate.** Every renewal creates a new version; the old version stays valid for an overlap window.
4. **Revoke and publish.** Mark a version untrusted and produce a new signed CRL that lists it.
5. **Serve the bundle.** Return the certificate plus its chain (the CA certificates up to the root) plus the trust bundle (the set of roots to trust), addressable by version, with an ETag (an HTTP header that lets a client ask "has this changed since I last fetched?").
6. **List expiring.** "Show me everything that expires before date X" so operators can sweep for stragglers.

**Why it matters**
Everything not in this list is a non-goal. When the interviewer asks "what about code signing?" the answer is "not in the first release, and here is why" rather than an improvised design.

---

> | Requirement | Target | Enforced by |
> |---|---|---|
> | Issue latency | p99 < `2 s` end to end; HSM sign `10–50 ms` (assumed) | Async work request, queue, HSM connection pool |
> | Read (get bundle) | p99 < `50 ms`, `99.99%` | Read replicas, ETag, CDN-able bundle endpoint |
> | Control-plane availability | `99.9%` for issue/rotate/revoke | Stateless API fleet across 3 fault domains; replicated metadata store |
> | Data-plane resilience | Zero handshake failures during a `24 h` control-plane outage | Consumers cache bundle on disk; renewal happens at `2/3` of lifetime |
> | Rotation | Zero downtime; overlap ≥ `7 d` between versions | Version state machine; deprecation only after adoption check |
> | Consistency | 1 committed current version per certificate | Expected-base CAS, current attempt fence, and issuer-scoped serial uniqueness |
> | Durability | Certificates and audit immutable for `7 y` | Append-only audit table; WORM archive in object storage |
> | Security | 0 plaintext CA private-key exports; 100% durable mutation audit intent | HSM custody for CA keys; separately scoped managed leaf-key delivery |
> | CRL freshness | `nextUpdate` ≤ `24 h`; rebuild within `15 min` of a revoke | CRL builder on revoke event plus daily timer; age alarm |

**Row: Issue latency.**

- Target: 99% of issuance requests complete (from `POST` to the version being `CURRENT`) within 2 seconds. The HSM signature itself is assumed to take 10–50 milliseconds.
- Enforced by: the request is accepted immediately and queued; a worker with a pool of open HSM connections does the signing.
- Worked example: 0.2 requests per second average, 40 ms per signature, one worker ⇒ HSM utilisation is 0.8%. Even a burst of 100 per second on one worker with 10 pooled connections stays under 2 seconds of queueing.

**Row: Read (get bundle).**

- Target: 99% of bundle fetches under 50 ms, available 99.99% of the time (about 52 minutes of downtime per year).
- Enforced by: read replicas of the database, ETags so unchanged bundles cost nothing, and an endpoint whose responses can be served by a CDN (content delivery network — a cache fleet near the clients).

**Row: Control-plane availability.**

- Target: 99.9% (about 8.8 hours of downtime per year) for the write operations. Lower than reads on purpose — writes can wait, handshakes cannot.
- Enforced by: API servers with no local state, spread across 3 fault domains (independent racks/power/network inside a data center), and a database that is replicated.

**Row: Data-plane resilience.**

- Target: if the control plane is completely down for 24 hours, not one TLS handshake fails.
- Enforced by: consumers keep the bundle on disk, and renewal happens at two-thirds of lifetime so there is always slack.
- Worked example: a 90-day certificate renews on day 60. If the control plane dies on day 60, the certificate is valid until day 90. That is 30 days of slack against a 24-hour requirement.

**Row: Rotation.**

- Target: zero downtime, and at least 7 days during which both versions validate.
- Enforced by: the version state machine (next-but-one section) and the rule that the old version is deprecated only after telemetry shows it no longer serves traffic.

**Row: Consistency.**

- Target: for each certificate, exactly one committed `CURRENT` version at any time.
- Enforced by three mechanisms, each explained in the deep dive:
  1. Expected-base CAS (compare-and-swap): "flip to version 5 only if the current version is still 4".
  2. Current attempt fence: a counter that marks which signing attempt is allowed to publish.
  3. Issuer-scoped serial uniqueness: no two certificates from one CA share a serial number.

**Row: Durability.**

- Target: certificates and audit rows cannot be changed or lost for 7 years.
- Enforced by: an append-only table plus a WORM (write once, read many) archive in object storage, which refuses overwrites at the storage layer.

**Row: Security.**

- Target: zero plaintext exports of a CA private key, ever; and every mutation's audit intent is durable.
- Enforced by: HSM custody, and a separately reviewed path for the one case where the service does hold private material (managed leaf keys).

**Row: CRL freshness.**

- Target: the CRL's `nextUpdate` field (the date by which a new list is promised) is never more than 24 hours out, and a revoke produces a new list within 15 minutes.
- Enforced by: the builder runs on every revoke event and also on a daily timer, and an alarm fires when the published list's age approaches the limit.

---

> - Invariants
>   - A private CA key exists only inside an HSM
>   - The service stores a key handle, never key bytes.

**What this says in plain words**
The first invariant. The database holds a reference like `ocid1.key.oc1..abc` — a name the HSM understands — and nothing else. There is no code path that could ever read the key.

**What breaks without it**
A database dump, a log line, or a heap snapshot becomes a CA compromise.

> - Every issued version names exactly one issuing CA version and one serial unique within that CA.

**What this says in plain words**
Each certificate version records which CA key version signed it and carries a serial number no other certificate from that CA has.

**What breaks without it**
Revocation works by serial. Two certificates with the same serial under one CA means revoking one revokes both — or neither, depending on the verifier.

> - A version that is `CURRENT` has at least one overlapping valid predecessor until adoption is confirmed.

**What this says in plain words**
While consumers may still be on the old version, the old version must still validate.

**What breaks without it**
The rotation outage from the framing section: any consumer that has not reloaded presents an invalid certificate.

> - A revoked version never returns to `CURRENT`.

**What this says in plain words**
Revocation is one-way. There is no "un-revoke".

**What breaks without it**
A verifier that cached the CRL entry would keep rejecting the certificate while another verifier accepts it. The world would disagree about whether the certificate is good.

> - Every mutating operation writes an audit row in the same transaction as the state change.

**What this says in plain words**
The state change and its audit record commit together or not at all.

**What breaks without it**
If the audit write is a separate step, a crash between the two leaves a state change with no record. After a compromise you can no longer prove which changes were legitimate.

> - Tenant A's credentials cannot observe the existence of tenant B's resources.

**What this says in plain words**
Not "cannot read", but "cannot even learn they exist". Every lookup is keyed by tenant first.

**What breaks without it**
An attacker enumerates certificate ids and learns which internal service names another tenant runs.

---

> - Non-goals
>   - Public WebPKI trust and ACME
>   - Buy that from a public CA.
>   - Code-signing and end-user client certificates in the first release.
>   - Building workload identity
>   - Reuse the platform's resource principal or SPIFFE identity.
>   - Designing the HSM/KMS service itself
>   - That is [s2.04](s2.04.HSM-key-management-and-signing.md).

**What this says in plain words**
Four things you explicitly refuse to build, each with a reason:

1. **Public web trust and ACME.** Being trusted by browsers means audits, transparency logs, and a rulebook. A public CA already does this; buy it.
2. **Code-signing and end-user client certificates.** Different lifetimes, different policy, different consumers. Later release.
3. **Workload identity.** Deciding *which* workload is allowed *which* name is a separate system. OCI has resource principals; the open standard is SPIFFE. This service consumes an identity; it does not mint one.
4. **The HSM / KMS (key management service) itself.** It is a separate question in this bank.

**Why it matters**
Naming non-goals is listed as an IC4 signal in the Level signal section. It shows you can scope a product, not just design everything you can think of.

## Capacity and consequence — IC3 baseline

This section does the arithmetic. Every estimate ends in a "Decision:" line, which is the point: a number that does not change a decision is a slide, not an estimate.

---

> - Writes (issuance and renewal)
>   - `1M` leaves with a `90 d` TTL renewed at `2/3` of life means each leaf renews every `60 d`.
>   - `1M / 60 d ≈ 16.7k renewals/day ≈ 0.2/s` average
>   - Tiny.

**What this says in plain words**
Each certificate lives 90 days but is renewed at day 60 (two-thirds of the way through). So each certificate is renewed once every 60 days. One million certificates divided by 60 days is about 16,700 renewals per day, which is 16,700 / 86,400 seconds ≈ 0.19 per second. Round to 0.2.

**Why it matters**
A single database on a laptop handles 0.2 writes per second without noticing. Saying "tiny" out loud, with the arithmetic shown, closes the throughput conversation and buys time for the hard parts.

---

> - Bursts happen when a CA rotates or a fleet is re-imaged
>   - Fan-out can be `10k+` in an hour.
>   - Decision: issuance is queued and rate-capped per CA, never synchronous, so a burst becomes a backlog rather than a timeout.

**What this says in plain words**
The average hides spikes. Two causes:

1. A CA key rotation may make many operators request re-issue at once.
2. Re-imaging a fleet (rebuilding 10,000 hosts from a fresh image) means every host comes up with no certificate and requests one immediately.

Ten thousand requests in one hour is about 2.8 per second — 14× the average. The decision: accept every request instantly into a queue, cap how fast each CA's queue drains, and never make the caller wait for the signature.

**Worked example**

- 10,000 hosts re-image over 20 minutes ⇒ 8.3 requests per second at the peak.
- Synchronous design: each request holds an HTTP connection while waiting for the HSM. With HSM throttled at 100 per second and a 2-second client timeout, the queue clears fine — but if the HSM hiccups for 5 seconds, 40 requests time out, the hosts retry, and the retry storm doubles the load.
- Async design: all 10,000 requests are accepted in milliseconds with a `202`. The worker drains at whatever rate the HSM allows. A 5-second HSM hiccup means the backlog grows by 40 and drains a second later. No host sees an error.

---

> - HSM signing budget
>   - Asymmetric `Sign` on a managed HSM is `order of magnitude` hundreds per second per region (check the current quota page).
>   - Forcing re-issue of `1M` leaves after a CA rotation at `100/s` takes `10,000 s ≈ 2.8 h` of saturated HSM.

**What this says in plain words**
A cloud HSM does roughly a few hundred signatures per second per region — the exact number is a published quota you should look up. If a CA rotation forced every one of the 1M leaves to be re-signed, at 100 per second that would take 10,000 seconds, nearly 3 hours, during which the HSM can do nothing else.

**Why it matters**
This is the one place where a number actually bites. The HSM is the only component with a hard ceiling, and 2.8 hours of saturation means every normal renewal during that window is blocked.

---

> - Decision: CA rotation does not re-issue leaves
>   - The new CA version signs at the next scheduled renewal and the old chain stays valid through the overlap.
> - Decision: renewal times carry jitter so fleets do not renew in lockstep.

**What this says in plain words**
Two decisions that follow from the HSM budget:

1. **Rotating the CA does not trigger a mass re-issue.** Existing leaves keep their old chain, which stays valid. Each leaf picks up the new CA when its own renewal comes due anyway. The 2.8-hour storm never happens; the load spreads over 60 days.
2. **Renewals are jittered.** If 10,000 hosts were issued certificates in the same minute (after a re-image), they would all come due for renewal in the same minute 60 days later. Adding a random offset (say, ±3 days) spreads that.

**Worked example of jitter**

- 10,000 certificates issued at 14:00 on day 0, all with `2/3` renewal ⇒ all due at 14:00 on day 60.
- Without jitter: 10,000 renewals in one scheduler tick. At 100 signatures per second, 100 seconds of HSM saturation, plus a `CertRotated` event storm that makes 10,000 consumers refetch at once.
- With ±3 days of jitter: 10,000 spread over 6 days ⇒ about 1,700 per day ⇒ 0.02 per second extra. Invisible.

---

> - Reads (bundle fetch)
>   - `10k` services fetching once per hour is `10,000 / 3,600 ≈ 3/s`
>   - A fleet restart can spike to thousands per second.
>   - Decision: the bundle endpoint is immutable per version with an ETag and long cache headers, so spikes hit the cache, not the metadata store.

**What this says in plain words**
Steady state: 10,000 consumers polling hourly is about 3 fetches per second. But if a whole fleet restarts, every consumer fetches on boot at once — thousands per second for a minute. The decision: make each version's bundle a fixed, never-changing response. Then a CDN or any HTTP cache can serve it, and the database sees one fetch per version, not one per consumer.

**Worked example**

- 5,000 sidecars restart within 30 seconds after a bad deploy rollback ⇒ 167 fetches per second.
- The bundle for `cert-42` version 7 is identical for every requester and carries `Cache-Control: max-age=86400, immutable`.
- The CDN answers 4,999 of them from cache. The database gets 1 query.
- Without immutability (say, the response included a "fetched at" timestamp), the cache would be useless and the database would take all 5,000.

---

> - CRL size
>   - Revoked is `≪ 1%`
>   - `10k` revoked serials at roughly `40 B` each is about `400 KB` signed.
>   - Decision: publish the CRL to object storage behind a CDN and re-sign it on revoke and at least every `24 h`
>     - Verifiers never fetch it from the API fleet.

**What this says in plain words**
Far fewer than 1% of certificates are ever revoked. Even a generous 10,000 revoked serials at about 40 bytes each (serial plus date plus reason) is a 400 KB file. That is small enough to put in a bucket and serve through a CDN. The decision: verifiers (the clients checking certificates) download the CRL from object storage, never from your API servers, and the file is rebuilt on every revoke and at least daily.

**Why it matters**
Verifiers may number in the tens of thousands and may fetch on every handshake if their cache is cold. Pointing them at the API fleet would make the CRL a denial-of-service vector against the control plane.

---

> - Storage
>   - `1M` certificates with about `3` retained versions and `6 KB` of PEM chain each is about `18 GB`
>   - Small.
>   - Audit at roughly `20k` events/day and `1 KB` per event for `7 y` is about `50 GB`.
>   - Decision: keep hot metadata in one relational store per region
>     - Archive audit to WORM object storage after `90 d`.

**What this says in plain words**
1M certificates × 3 versions kept × 6 KB of PEM (the text encoding of a certificate chain) = 18 GB. Audit: about 20,000 events per day (16,700 renewals plus revocations, reads of managed keys, and so on) × 1 KB × 365 × 7 ≈ 51 GB. Both fit in a single database. The decision: one relational database per region holds the live rows; audit rows older than 90 days move to WORM object storage.

**Why it matters**
This kills the "should we use a NoSQL store?" tangent. A relational store gives you transactions, which the consistency invariants need, and 70 GB is nothing for one.

---

> - So what
>   - This is not a scale problem
>   - Say so in the first two minutes.
>   - Spend the time on rotation, key custody, and failure containment.

**What this says in plain words**
The conclusion of all the arithmetic: throughput is irrelevant. State that early and explicitly, then move to the three topics that are actually hard.

**Why it matters**
Interviewers grade whether your estimates *changed* what you did. Here the estimates produced four decisions (async queue, no mass re-issue, jitter, immutable bundle) and one scoping call (skip scale). That is the "a number changes a decision" signal from the Level signal section.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| TTL | Time to live. Here, the validity period of a certificate. | Every rate is derived from the 90-day leaf TTL. |
| `2/3` renewal | Renewing at two-thirds of lifetime (day 60 of 90). | Sets the renewal interval and the slack. |
| Fan-out | One event causing many downstream requests. | A CA rotation or fleet re-image fans out to thousands of issuances. |
| Re-image | Rebuilding hosts from a fresh disk image, losing local state including cached certificates. | The main burst source for writes and reads. |
| Backlog | Queued work that has been accepted but not yet done. | What a burst becomes under the async design. |
| Asymmetric `Sign` | Producing a digital signature with a private key inside the HSM. | The HSM operation with a hard throughput ceiling. |
| Quota | A published per-region limit on an operation rate. | The HSM signing budget is a quota. |
| Jitter | A random offset added to a scheduled time so many events do not coincide. | Spreads renewals so fleets do not renew in lockstep. |
| Immutable response | An HTTP response that will never change for a given URL, so it may be cached indefinitely. | The bundle-per-version endpoint. |
| PEM | A text encoding of certificates and keys, the familiar `-----BEGIN CERTIFICATE-----` format. | Unit of the storage estimate. |
| Verifier / relying party | The client that checks a presented certificate and its revocation status. | Downloads the CRL from the CDN. |
| WORM | Write once, read many. Storage that refuses overwrites. | Where old audit rows go. |
| Relational store | A SQL database with transactions. | Chosen because the invariants need cross-row atomicity. |

## Architecture and flow — IC3 baseline

An ASCII diagram, seven components each with what it buys and what it costs, then one request traced end to end.

---

> ```text
> [control plane]
> Client/CI/Terraform -> API gateway (authn, compartment policy, quota)
>                           -> Certificate API service -> metadata store (CAs, CA versions,
>                                  |                        certificates, versions, work requests, audit)
>                                  | issue/rotate/revoke = work request (202 + work_request_id)
>                                  v
> [async]   Issuance worker -> Vault/HSM: Sign(key_ref, TBS) -> store version PENDING -> CURRENT -> event
> [async]   Rotation scheduler -> finds versions at 2/3 lifetime -> enqueue renew (idempotent per version)
> [async]   CRL builder -> re-sign CRL on revoke + every 24 h -> object storage bucket -> CDN
>
> [data plane / hot path]
> Consumers (LB, gateway, sidecar) -> GET bundle by version (ETag, long cache) -> disk cache
>    TLS handshakes use the cached chain; they never call the control plane
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The diagram is split into three labelled zones, and the split is the whole architectural idea.

1. **Control plane.** Where certificates are asked for, made, and recorded. Slow, transactional, allowed to be unavailable.
2. **Async workers.** Three background processes that do the parts of the job nobody waits on synchronously.
3. **Data plane / hot path.** Where certificates are actually *used*, on every TLS handshake. Fast, and deliberately unable to reach the control plane at all.

**Why the three-zone split is the design**
Because the failure requirement in the requirements section was that a control-plane outage must not break existing TLS.

- If a TLS handshake called the control plane to fetch a chain, the control plane's availability would become the availability of every service in the company.
- Worked example of that failure: the certificate API is down for 45 minutes during a deploy. If handshakes depended on it, every service-to-service connection in the fleet fails for 45 minutes. That is not a PKI outage, that is a total outage.
- The diagram's last line — "TLS handshakes use the cached chain; they never call the control plane" — is the sentence that prevents this. Everything else in the architecture exists to make that sentence true.

**Reading the arrows, zone by zone**

- **Control plane, top to bottom.**
  - `Client/CI/Terraform` — three kinds of caller. A human running a command, a build pipeline asking for a certificate as part of a deploy, and an infrastructure-as-code tool declaring that a certificate should exist. All three hit the same API.
  - `-> API gateway (authn, compartment policy, quota)` — the first hop authenticates the caller, checks whether the caller is allowed to touch this compartment, and checks whether the tenant is over its rate limit.
  - `-> Certificate API service` — the service that understands certificates as opposed to HTTP.
  - `-> metadata store` — one relational database holding six kinds of row, listed in the diagram: CAs, CA versions, certificates, certificate versions, work requests, audit.
  - `issue/rotate/revoke = work request (202 + work_request_id)` — the critical line. None of the three mutating operations completes synchronously. Each one returns HTTP `202 Accepted` and a ticket number.
    - Worked example of why: signing requires a round trip to a hardware security module, which can take 50–200 milliseconds and can be queued behind other work. Holding an HTTP connection open across that, and across a retry of that, makes the API's latency a function of the HSM's queue depth.
- **The three async workers.**
  - `Issuance worker -> Vault/HSM: Sign(key_ref, TBS) -> store version PENDING -> CURRENT -> event`
    - Reads the queued work, asks the HSM to sign, stores the result, flips the version's state from `PENDING` to `CURRENT`, and publishes an event.
    - `key_ref` is a *handle*, not a key. The worker holds a string like `ocid1.key.oc1..aaaa`, hands it to the HSM, and the HSM does the signing internally. The worker never sees private key bytes.
    - `TBS` is "to be signed" — the assembled certificate body before a signature is attached to it.
  - `Rotation scheduler -> finds versions at 2/3 lifetime -> enqueue renew (idempotent per version)`
    - A periodic sweep. Worked example at a 90-day lifetime: two thirds is day 60, so every certificate is queued for renewal with 30 days of validity still remaining.
    - "idempotent per version" means enqueueing the same renewal twice produces one renewal, which matters because a sweep that runs every hour will see the same due certificate many times.
  - `CRL builder -> re-sign CRL on revoke + every 24 h -> object storage bucket -> CDN`
    - Builds the certificate revocation list, signs it, and puts it in a bucket behind a content delivery network.
    - Two triggers: immediately when something is revoked, and unconditionally every 24 hours.
    - Worked example of why the unconditional rebuild exists: a CRL carries its own expiry (`nextUpdate`). If nothing is revoked for a week and the CRL is never rebuilt, it expires, and strict verifiers reject a *valid* certificate because the revocation list they fetched is stale. The daily rebuild keeps the artifact fresh even when its contents have not changed.
- **Data plane.**
  - `Consumers (LB, gateway, sidecar) -> GET bundle by version (ETag, long cache) -> disk cache`
  - The consumers are the things that terminate TLS: load balancers, API gateways, and service-mesh sidecars.
  - They fetch a bundle *by version*, which makes the response immutable and therefore cacheable forever.
  - `ETag` is an identifier the server attaches to a response; the client sends it back on the next request and gets `304 Not Modified` if nothing changed, which costs one round trip and zero bytes of body.
  - `disk cache` is the load-bearing word. Not memory — disk, so a process restart does not lose the chain.
    - Worked example: a sidecar restarts at 03:00 while the control plane is mid-deploy. With a memory cache it has no certificate and cannot serve TLS. With a disk cache it reads the chain it already had and comes up normally.

---

> - API gateway owns authentication, compartment policy, and quota.
>   - Mechanism bought: one policy check point (`allow group X to manage certificates in compartment Y`) and a per-tenant token bucket.
>   - Cost paid: every request pays a policy lookup
>     - Policy-service latency is on the control-plane path.

**What this says in plain words**
One component does three gatekeeping jobs, so no other component has to.

**Unpacking the mechanism**

- "One policy check point" is the benefit. Worked example of the alternative: if each of the nine API endpoints checked permissions itself, a new endpoint added next quarter could forget, and that single omission is a cross-tenant access bug.
- The example policy string `allow group X to manage certificates in compartment Y` is Oracle Cloud Infrastructure's actual policy syntax. A **compartment** is OCI's unit of resource grouping and access control — roughly, a labelled box of resources that a policy statement can name. Using that syntax in the answer signals familiarity with the platform the hiring team builds on.
- "Per-tenant token bucket" is the quota mechanism. A bucket holds tokens, requests consume them, and tokens refill at a fixed rate. Worked example: a bucket of 100 tokens refilling at 10 per second lets a tenant burst 100 requests instantly, then settle to 10 per second. That shape is right here because certificate requests genuinely arrive in bursts during a deploy.

**The cost, and why it is stated**

- Every request pays a policy lookup, which is a network call to a separate policy service before any certificate work starts.
- Worked example of the consequence: a policy service at the 99th percentile of 80 milliseconds puts 80 milliseconds on the floor of every control-plane operation, and a policy-service outage stops issuance completely even though the CA, the HSM, and the database are all healthy.
- Naming that cost unprompted is what separates describing an architecture from having thought about one.

---

> - Certificate API service owns validation and intent.
>   - Mechanism bought: validate synchronously, persist intent, return `202` with a work-request id ([s3.01](s3.01.Async-control-plane-resource-API.md)).
>   - Cost paid: every client needs a poll loop
>     - A fast operation still looks asynchronous.

**What this says in plain words**
The API does the cheap, fast part of the work in front of the caller, writes down what it intends to do, and hands back a ticket.

**Splitting "validation and intent"**

1. **Validation, synchronously.** Anything that can be rejected cheaply is rejected while the caller is still on the line.
   - Worked example: a request for the name `bank.example.com` from a tenant whose profile only permits `*.acme.internal` is a `400` in a few milliseconds. It would be much worse to accept it, return `202`, and have the caller discover the failure by polling 30 seconds later.
2. **Intent, persistently.** The *decision to issue* is written to the database before any signing happens.
   - This is the durable record that lets the operation be retried safely. A worker that picks up the work request finds a fully specified job: which CA, which name, which serial number, what the certificate body should be.
   - Worked example of why intent must precede signing: if the worker decided the certificate's contents itself, two workers retrying the same job could decide differently and produce two different certificates for one request.
3. **`202` with a work-request id.** The HTTP status meaning "accepted, not finished", plus the ticket number to ask about later.

**The cost, honestly stated**

- Every client needs a poll loop: submit, then repeatedly `GET /v1/work-requests/{id}` until it reports success or failure.
- "A fast operation still looks asynchronous" is the annoyance. Worked example: issuing from a warm HSM might take 120 milliseconds end to end, but the client still submits, gets a ticket, waits its poll interval of perhaps 1 second, and asks. The user experiences a one-second operation that took a tenth of a second.
- The trade is accepted because uniformity is worth more than best-case latency: every mutating operation behaves the same way, so client code has one shape rather than three.

---

> - Metadata store owns the source of truth for CAs, versions, and audit.
>   - Mechanism bought: one relational store per region gives transactions across version flip and audit row.
>   - Cost paid: single-region writes
>     - A region loss stops issuance until DR ([s2.06](s2.06.Regional-PKI-disaster-recovery.md)).

**What this says in plain words**
One transactional database per region holds the truth, because several facts must change together or not at all.

**Why "transactions across version flip and audit row" is the deciding requirement**

- A version flip means changing which certificate version is `CURRENT`. An audit row means recording who caused that change and when.
- These must be atomic. Worked example of them not being atomic: the flip succeeds and the audit write fails. The system now has a certificate that changed with no record of why, which in a PKI is a compliance finding, not a bug report.
- Worked example in the other order: the audit row is written and the flip fails. The audit log now asserts something that did not happen, which is worse — an audit log you cannot trust is not an audit log.
- A relational store gives this for free with `BEGIN ... COMMIT`. A key-value store without multi-key transactions would require inventing a two-phase protocol to get the same property.

**The cost**

- Writes go to one region.
- Worked example: the region holding the metadata store is lost. Certificates cannot be issued, renewed, or revoked anywhere until disaster recovery promotes a replica. Meanwhile, every already-issued certificate keeps working perfectly, because handshakes never touch this database.
- That second sentence is the consolation, and it is a direct dividend of the three-zone split: a total control-plane loss degrades the ability to *change* PKI state, not the ability to *use* it.

---

> - Issuance worker owns signing.
>   - Mechanism bought: `Sign` executes inside the HSM under an audited key handle
>     - The worker sees only public output.
>   - Cost paid: HSM latency and quota are on the issuance path, and an HSM signature cannot be rolled back by a database transaction.

**What this says in plain words**
The worker asks the hardware to sign; it never holds the key. And the last clause is the hardest sentence in the section.

**Mechanism: signing inside the HSM**

- An **HSM** (hardware security module) is a tamper-resistant device that generates and stores private keys and performs operations with them, and which is built so the key cannot be exported — not by an administrator, not by a compromised process, not by anyone.
- The worker sends `Sign(key_ref, SHA-256(TBS))` and receives a signature. It sends a *handle* and a *hash*, and gets back a signature.
- "The worker sees only public output" is the security property. Worked example of its value: the issuance worker's host is fully compromised. The attacker can request signatures for as long as they hold the host, and every one of those requests is logged by the HSM under an audited key handle. What the attacker cannot do is take the CA key and issue certificates for the next ten years from their own infrastructure. The blast radius is bounded in time, and it is visible.

**Cost, part one: latency and quota on the issuance path**

- HSMs are not fast and they have throughput limits, often expressed as signatures per second.
- Worked example: an HSM rated at 1,000 signatures per second is plenty for the steady state computed earlier, and is a hard ceiling during a mass reissuance after a compromise, where the queue drains at exactly that rate no matter how many workers exist.

**Cost, part two: the sentence to actually understand**

> an HSM signature cannot be rolled back by a database transaction

- A database transaction can be aborted, and everything it did disappears. An HSM signature is a real-world event that has already happened; there is no `ROLLBACK` for it.
- Worked example of the resulting hazard:
  1. The worker begins a transaction.
  2. The worker calls the HSM and gets a signature. A real, valid certificate now exists in the world.
  3. The worker tries to store it and the database connection dies.
  4. The transaction rolls back. The database has no record of the certificate.
  - The certificate is valid, signed by the CA, and completely unknown to the system that issued it. If it later needs revoking, there is no row to revoke, and the serial number it consumed may be handed out again.
- This single fact is why the design splits issuance into two transactions with a fence around them, which the rotation section takes apart in detail. Noticing it here, at the architecture stage, is the point of including it.

---

> - Rotation scheduler owns renewals.
>   - Mechanism bought: a periodic sweep at `2/3` of lifetime with idempotent enqueue.
>   - Cost paid: a scheduler bug is a fleet-wide expiry
>     - It needs its own "expiring soon" alarm as a backstop.

**What this says in plain words**
A timer-driven process renews certificates early, and its silent failure is the single most dangerous failure in the system.

**Mechanism: the two-thirds rule**

- Renew when two thirds of the lifetime has elapsed, leaving one third as margin.
- Worked example at 90 days: renewal is attempted at day 60, leaving 30 days. If the renewal fails, it can fail every day for a month and still be fixed before anything expires.
- Worked example of a tighter rule: renewing at day 85 leaves 5 days. A long weekend plus a failed alert, and certificates start expiring.
- "Idempotent enqueue" prevents the sweep from piling up duplicate work. Worked example: the sweep runs hourly and a certificate has been due since Tuesday. Without idempotency it accumulates 100+ renewal jobs; with it there is exactly one outstanding renewal per version.

**The cost, which is the real content of this bullet**

- "A scheduler bug is a fleet-wide expiry." Every certificate is renewed by this one process, so if it stops, everything expires — not at once, but continuously, starting 30 days later.
- Worked example with the numbers from the capacity section: 1,000,000 certificates on 90-day lifetimes means roughly 11,100 renewals per day. A scheduler that silently dies produces *no errors at all* for 30 days, because nothing is failing — nothing is happening. On day 31, about 11,000 certificates expire, and the same again every day after.
- Hence "it needs its own 'expiring soon' alarm as a backstop". The alarm does not watch the scheduler; it watches the *outcome*. Worked example of the difference: an alarm on "scheduler job failures" reports zero, because a dead scheduler has no failures. An alarm on "count of `CURRENT` certificates with `not_after` within 14 days" climbs from its normal value toward the full fleet, and fires.
- The general principle worth stating out loud in an interview: **alarm on the outcome you need, not on the health of the component that produces it.** A component that fails by doing nothing is invisible to its own health checks.

---

> - CRL builder owns revocation publication.
>   - Mechanism bought: a signed, versioned CRL artifact on object storage behind a CDN.
>   - Cost paid: revocation is only as fresh as the publish interval and the verifier's cache.

**What this says in plain words**
Revocation is published as a file, and a file has a publication delay.

**Mechanism**

- A **CRL** (certificate revocation list) is a list of revoked serial numbers, signed by the CA. Because it is signed, it can be served from anywhere untrusted — a bucket, a CDN edge — and a verifier can still check it is genuine.
- "Versioned artifact on object storage behind a CDN" means the file is written once per build and distributed by cache infrastructure designed for exactly this.
- Worked example of why this scales: 1,000,000 verifiers fetching a CRL every 6 hours is about 46 requests per second of origin load if nothing is cached, and effectively zero if the CDN serves it, because every verifier wants the identical bytes.

**The cost**

- Freshness is bounded by two independent delays, and they add.
- Worked example: the CRL is rebuilt within 1 minute of a revocation, the CDN caches for 1 hour, and a verifier caches its copy for 6 hours. A certificate revoked at 09:00 may be accepted by some verifier until roughly 16:00.
- This is the gap that OCSP and short-lived certificates exist to close, and it is the whole subject of [s2.03](s2.03.Certificate-revocation-CRL-OCSP.md). Naming the gap here, rather than claiming revocation is instant, is the honest position.

---

> - Consumers own the data plane.
>   - Mechanism bought: local disk cache plus hot-reload gives static stability during control-plane outages.
>   - Cost paid: the service cannot force a switch
>     - Adoption must be measured from consumer telemetry.

**What this says in plain words**
The things using certificates keep a local copy and can reload it without restarting — which means the CA can be down without anything breaking, and also means the CA cannot make anyone do anything.

**Mechanism: static stability**

- **Static stability** is the property that a system keeps working correctly on its last known good state when its control plane becomes unavailable. It does not degrade, it does not fail over — it simply continues, because it never depended on the control plane in the first place.
- Two parts make it work here:
  - **Local disk cache.** The chain is on disk, so it survives process restarts and host reboots.
  - **Hot-reload.** The consumer can pick up a new chain without restarting, so renewal does not require a deploy.
- Worked example: the entire certificate control plane is down for four hours. Every load balancer keeps terminating TLS with the chain it already has. New certificates cannot be issued during those four hours, and nothing that already exists notices.

**The cost, which is subtle and important**

- "The service cannot force a switch." Once a bundle is cached on ten thousand hosts, the CA has no way to reach into those hosts and make them use a new one.
- Worked example: a CA key is rotated and the new chain published at 10:00. At 14:00, how many consumers are actually presenting the new chain? The control plane does not know. It knows what it *published*; it does not know what is *deployed*.
- "Adoption must be measured from consumer telemetry" is the resolution: consumers report which version they are serving, and the control plane aggregates those reports.
- Worked example of why that matters concretely: retiring an old CA version is only safe once nothing is still using it. Without adoption telemetry, retirement is a guess, and a wrong guess breaks every consumer still on the old chain. This is exactly the check that gates the `PREVIOUS → DEPRECATED` transition in the rotation section.
- The general shape: **when you give up control for availability, you have to buy back observability.** Static stability is not free; its price is that you must now measure what you used to be able to command.

---

> → Client sends `POST /v1/certs` with `Idempotency-Key`, CSR, SANs, and TTL.
> → Gateway authenticates the caller and checks compartment policy and quota.
> → API verifies the CSR self-signature (proof of possession) and checks SANs and TTL against the tenant's profile.
> → API persists the certificate row, a `PENDING` version with a reserved serial, a work request, and an audit row in one transaction.
> → API returns `202` with `work_request_id`.
> → Issuance worker locks the certificate row, builds the TBS certificate, and calls `Sign(key_ref, SHA-256(TBS))` in the HSM.
> → Worker stores the signed PEM chain, flips the version `PENDING → CURRENT`, marks the prior version `PREVIOUS`, and writes the audit row.
> → Worker publishes `CertRotated`; consumers refresh on event or on their next poll and pick up the new ETag.
> → Client polls the work request until `SUCCEEDED`, then reads `GET /v1/certs/{id}/bundle`.

**What this is**
One issuance traced from the client's first call to the client reading the finished certificate. Nine steps. This is the narration to give in the interview when asked "walk me through a request".

**Step 1 — the client's request**

> Client sends `POST /v1/certs` with `Idempotency-Key`, CSR, SANs, and TTL.

- **`Idempotency-Key`** — a client-generated unique string. If the client retries because it never saw a response, the same key tells the server "this is the same request, not a new one".
  - Worked example of its absence: the client sends the request, the response is lost to a network blip, the client retries. Without the key the server issues a second certificate, consuming a second serial, and the client now has two certificates where it wanted one, one of which nobody will ever renew or revoke.
- **CSR** — certificate signing request. A small document containing the requester's *public* key and the name they want, signed by the matching *private* key.
- **SANs** — subject alternative names, the list of DNS names the certificate should be valid for. Modern verifiers read names from here, not from the legacy common-name field.
- **TTL** — how long the certificate should be valid.

**Step 2 — the gateway**

> Gateway authenticates the caller and checks compartment policy and quota.

- Three checks, in the cheapest useful order: who are you, are you allowed to do this here, and have you done too much of it lately.

**Step 3 — validation, including the one clever check**

> API verifies the CSR self-signature (proof of possession) and checks SANs and TTL against the tenant's profile.

- **Proof of possession** is the important idea. The CSR is signed by the private key corresponding to the public key inside it. Verifying that signature proves the requester actually holds the private key.
  - Worked example of what it prevents: an attacker takes a public key belonging to a service they do not control and asks the CA to certify it under a name they do control, or the reverse. Without proof of possession, the CA would happily bind a name to a key its requester cannot use, which produces certificates whose ownership does not match reality.
  - The check costs one signature verification — microseconds — and it is non-negotiable.
- **SANs and TTL against the tenant's profile** is the multi-tenancy check. The tenant profile says which name suffixes this tenant may request and the maximum lifetime they may ask for.
  - Worked example: tenant `acme` is constrained to `*.acme.internal`. A request for `payments.globex.internal` fails here, at validation, in milliseconds, and never reaches the signing path.

**Step 4 — the transaction, and what is inside it**

> API persists the certificate row, a `PENDING` version with a reserved serial, a work request, and an audit row in one transaction.

- Four rows, atomically. This is the transactionality the metadata-store bullet was justifying.
  1. **The certificate row** — the logical certificate, the thing that persists across renewals.
  2. **A `PENDING` version with a reserved serial** — a specific version of it, not yet signed, with its serial number already allocated so no other issuance can take it.
  3. **A work request** — the ticket the client will poll.
  4. **An audit row** — who asked for this, when.
- Worked example of why the serial is reserved *now* rather than at signing time: two concurrent issuances from the same CA must not receive the same serial number, because serial uniqueness within an issuer is an X.509 requirement and duplicate serials break revocation (revoking one serial would revoke two certificates). Allocating inside the transaction that creates the version makes the database's uniqueness constraint do the work.
- The row created here *is* the durable issuance intent mentioned in the API-service bullet. Everything the worker needs is now written down.

**Step 5 — the handoff**

> API returns `202` with `work_request_id`.

- The synchronous part is over. Everything that could be rejected cheaply has been.

**Step 6 — the worker signs**

> Issuance worker locks the certificate row, builds the TBS certificate, and calls `Sign(key_ref, SHA-256(TBS))` in the HSM.

- **Locks the certificate row** — takes a row-level lock so exactly one worker acts on this certificate at a time. This is the "single writer via row lock" invariant from the data model.
  - Worked example of its absence: two workers pick up duplicate messages from the queue for the same certificate, both sign, and both try to flip to `CURRENT`. One wins, and the loser's certificate is valid, signed, and orphaned.
- **Builds the TBS certificate** — assembles the body: serial, issuer, validity window, subject, SANs, extensions. Everything except the signature.
- **`Sign(key_ref, SHA-256(TBS))`** — hashes the body and sends the *hash* to the HSM, not the body.
  - Worked example of why the hash: an HSM signing operation works over a fixed-size digest; sending a hash means the request is 32 bytes regardless of certificate size, and the HSM's work is constant.

**Step 7 — the second transaction**

> Worker stores the signed PEM chain, flips the version `PENDING → CURRENT`, marks the prior version `PREVIOUS`, and writes the audit row.

- Four changes again, and again atomic. Note that this happens *after* the un-rollbackable HSM call, which is exactly the hazard named above and dissected in the rotation section.
- "Marks the prior version `PREVIOUS`" rather than deleting it. The old certificate stays valid and stays reachable, which is what makes rollback possible and what stops consumers who have not refreshed from breaking.

**Step 8 — telling the world**

> Worker publishes `CertRotated`; consumers refresh on event or on their next poll and pick up the new ETag.

- **Two paths on purpose**: an event for speed, a poll for reliability.
- Worked example of why both: the event bus drops a message. The consumer's periodic poll — say every 5 minutes — picks the change up anyway. The event makes the common case fast; the poll makes the worst case bounded.
- **The new ETag** is how the consumer knows something changed without downloading anything: the `If-None-Match` request returns `304` when the bundle is unchanged, and the full body only when it is not.

**Step 9 — the client finishes**

> Client polls the work request until `SUCCEEDED`, then reads `GET /v1/certs/{id}/bundle`.

- The poll loop the API-service bullet warned about, and the reason the work request exists at all.
- Worked example of the case it saves: the client never received the `202` and does not know its work-request id. It retries with the same `Idempotency-Key`, receives the *original* work request id, and polls that. No duplicate certificate, no ambiguity.

## API and data model — IC3 baseline

Ten endpoints in a table, a versioning policy, seven state tables, an ownership schema, reference DDL, and five schema notes.

---

> | Surface | Contract | Idempotency | Conflict/error behavior |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | `POST /v1/cas` | `{name, type: ROOT\|SUBORDINATE, parent_ca_id?, key_algo, ttl}`; returns `202 {ca_id, work_request_id}` | `Idempotency-Key`; same key and payload returns the same work request | Same key with different payload returns `409`; root creation needs a separate administrative policy |
> | `POST /v1/cas/{id}:rotate` | Creates a new CA signing version; returns `202 {work_request_id}` | Idempotent per current CA version | `409` if a rotation for this CA is already in flight |
> | `POST /v1/certs` | `{ca_id, csr or managed_key_spec, san[], ttl_days, auto_renew}`; returns `202 {cert_id, work_request_id}` | `Idempotency-Key` per tenant | `400` on bad CSR signature or disallowed SAN; `429` on tenant quota |
> | `GET /v1/certs/{id}` | `CURRENT` version metadata, issuer, `not_after`, state | Read | `404` for another tenant's id (no existence leak) |
> | `GET /v1/certs/{id}/bundle` | PEM chain of the current version; ETag; `?version=` for an explicit version | Read | `304` on `If-None-Match`; immutable per version |
> | `POST /v1/certs/{id}:renew` | Expected base version; returns `202` with work request | Same tenant/cert/base/token and payload returns the original operation | `409` only for conflicting payload or base version |
> | `POST /v1/certs/{id}:revoke` | `{version, reason}`; returns `202`; lands in the next CRL | Idempotent (already revoked returns the same result) | `409` if the version is `PENDING` and never issued; revoke of `CURRENT` requires an explicit flag |
> | `GET /v1/cas/{id}/crl` | Signed CRL for the CA version (also on CDN) | Read | Redirect to the object-storage CDP |
> | `GET /v1/certs?expiring_before=&after=` | Cursor page for operational sweeps | Read | Indexed on `(tenant_id, not_after)` |
> | `GET /v1/work-requests/{id}` | `ACCEPTED\|IN_PROGRESS\|SUCCEEDED\|FAILED`, percent complete, errors | Read | Authoritative answer after a lost `202` |

**What this says in plain words**
Ten surfaces. Four mutate and return `202`; six are reads. The third and fourth columns are where the design actually lives — how a retry is made safe, and what happens when two things collide.

**Reading the table row by row**

- **`POST /v1/cas` — create a certificate authority.**
  - `type: ROOT|SUBORDINATE` — a **root** CA signs itself and sits at the top of the trust chain; a **subordinate** CA is signed by a parent and does the day-to-day issuing. `parent_ca_id` is present for subordinates and absent for roots.
  - `key_algo` — which key algorithm, for example RSA-4096 or ECDSA P-384. This is a permanent choice: it is baked into every certificate the CA ever signs.
  - Conflict column: "same key with different payload returns `409`". Worked example of why that rule is right: a client retries with the same idempotency key but a changed name. The two possible behaviors are "silently ignore the change and return the old result" or "reject". Returning `409 Conflict` is the honest answer, because the client's two requests disagree and the server cannot know which one they meant.
  - "Root creation needs a separate administrative policy" — creating a root CA is not a normal tenant operation. Worked example of why: a root is a new trust anchor. Anything that trusts it trusts everything it ever signs. That is an organizational decision, not an API call a build pipeline should be able to make.
- **`POST /v1/cas/{id}:rotate` — give the CA a new signing key.**
  - "Idempotent per current CA version" — the operation is keyed by which version is current, so two retries of "rotate away from version 4" produce one rotation.
  - "`409` if a rotation for this CA is already in flight". Worked example: two operators both notice the CA is old and both click rotate. Allowing both would create two new CA versions, and the question "which one is current?" would be decided by timing. Refusing the second makes it a question rather than a mess.
- **`POST /v1/certs` — issue a certificate.**
  - `csr or managed_key_spec` — two modes. Either the caller generated the key themselves and sends a CSR, or they ask the service to generate and hold the key for them.
    - Worked example of the tradeoff: with a CSR the private key never leaves the caller's machine, which is stronger, and the caller is responsible for it. With a managed key the service handles generation and delivery, which is easier and means the key exists in one more place.
  - `400` on a bad CSR signature (proof of possession failed) or a disallowed SAN (outside the tenant's permitted names). Both are client errors, detectable immediately.
  - `429` on tenant quota — too many requests, come back later. Distinct from `400` because the request was *valid*, just rate-limited.
- **`GET /v1/certs/{id}` — read metadata.**
  - "`404` for another tenant's id (no existence leak)" is a security decision worth explaining. The intuitive response to "you may not see this" is `403 Forbidden`. But `403` confirms the resource exists.
    - Worked example of the leak: an attacker in tenant A enumerates certificate ids. A `403` means "real, belongs to someone else"; a `404` means "not real". They cannot read the certificate either way, but they can now count how many certificates other tenants hold and watch that number grow, which tells them when a competitor is deploying.
    - Returning `404` for both cases makes the two indistinguishable. The cost is a slightly less helpful error for a user who genuinely mistyped their own id.
- **`GET /v1/certs/{id}/bundle` — fetch the actual PEM chain.**
  - This is the endpoint the data plane calls. `?version=` allows pinning to a specific version instead of following `CURRENT`.
  - "immutable per version" is what makes aggressive caching safe: bundle version 7 is the same bytes forever, so it can be cached indefinitely.
  - `304` on `If-None-Match` is the cheap poll: the consumer sends the ETag it holds, and an unchanged bundle costs a round trip and an empty body.
  - Worked example of the saving: 10,000 consumers polling every 5 minutes is about 33 requests per second. If each transferred a 6-kibibyte chain that is 200 kibibytes per second forever, for data that changes four times a year per certificate. With `304`s it is essentially zero.
- **`POST /v1/certs/{id}:renew` — issue the next version.**
  - "Expected base version" is the key idea: the caller says which version they are renewing *from*.
  - Worked example of what that prevents: the scheduler queues a renewal of version 7. Meanwhile an operator manually renews, producing version 8. The queued job arrives carrying "base version 7", the current version is 8, and it is rejected — correctly, because the renewal it wanted to perform has already effectively happened. Without the base version, it would produce a redundant version 9.
  - "`409` only for conflicting payload or base version" — the word *only* matters. An identical retry is not a conflict; it returns the original operation.
- **`POST /v1/certs/{id}:revoke` — mark a version as no longer trustworthy.**
  - `{version, reason}` — revocation is per version, not per certificate. Version 6 can be revoked while version 7 remains good. The `reason` is a standard X.509 code such as key compromise or superseded, and verifiers can treat them differently.
  - "Idempotent (already revoked returns the same result)" — revoking twice is not an error. Worked example of why this matters operationally: during an incident, three people all revoke the same compromised certificate. All three should get a success, not two errors that make them wonder whether it worked.
  - "`409` if the version is `PENDING` and never issued" — you cannot revoke something that was never signed. There is no serial in circulation to put on a list.
  - "Revoke of `CURRENT` requires an explicit flag" — this is a guardrail. Worked example: revoking the current certificate of a production load balancer takes it offline as soon as verifiers notice. Requiring a deliberate extra flag means it cannot happen through a mistyped id in a script.
- **`GET /v1/cas/{id}/crl` — the revocation list.**
  - "Redirect to the object-storage CDP." The **CDP** is the CRL distribution point, the URL embedded in every certificate telling verifiers where to fetch the list. The API redirects rather than serving the bytes itself, so the CDN absorbs the load.
- **`GET /v1/certs?expiring_before=&after=` — the operational sweep.**
  - A cursor-paged query over expiry. `after=` is the pagination cursor, not a date filter on the other side.
  - "Indexed on `(tenant_id, not_after)`" — the index that makes this query cheap. Worked example: without it, finding certificates expiring in the next 14 days across 1,000,000 rows is a full scan every time the scheduler sweeps. With it, the database walks exactly the matching range.
- **`GET /v1/work-requests/{id}` — the outcome.**
  - "Authoritative answer after a lost `202`" is its reason for existing. The client that lost the response retries with the same idempotency key, receives the original work-request id, and asks here what actually happened.

> | Idempotency record | `(tenant_id, idempotency_key)` | Request hash and result id; retained ≥ the client retry horizon |

**What this says in plain words**
Seven kinds of row. The structural observation is that both CAs and certificates are split into a *thing* and its *versions* — the same shape twice.

**The thing-versus-version split, which is the whole data model**

- A **CA** is a long-lived identity. A **CA signing version** is one key pair and one validity window inside that identity.
- A **certificate** is a long-lived logical certificate — "the certificate for `api.acme.internal`". A **certificate version** is one actual signed X.509 document.
- Worked example of why they must be separate: over three years, `api.acme.internal` will have roughly twelve certificates issued to it. If the row were overwritten each time, you could not revoke a previous one, could not roll back to it, and could not answer "what was serving on March 3rd". The logical row is stable; the version rows accumulate.

**Row by row**

- **CA.** `(tenant_id, ca_id)` — tenant is part of every key, so the tenant boundary is structural rather than a filter someone might forget.
  - `CREATING|ACTIVE|DISABLED|DELETED` — four states. `DISABLED` means existing certificates stay valid but nothing new is issued, which is the right response to a suspected problem before you have decided it is a compromise.
  - "optimistic version" — a counter incremented on every write. A writer reads it, makes a change, and writes only if the counter is unchanged. Worked example: two workers both try to change the CA's current signing version; the first increments the counter from 4 to 5, the second's conditional write sees 5 where it expected 4, fails, and retries against fresh state. No lock is held, and no update is lost.
- **CA signing version.** Holds `key_ref` — the HSM handle — and the *public* chain.
  - Five states, listed here and taken apart properly in the rotation section.
- **Certificate.** "Single writer via row lock" is the invariant, and it is the one that stops duplicate issuance.
- **Certificate version.** "Serial unique per CA" and "PEM chain (public only)".
  - "public only" is an explicit statement that this database never stores private key material. Worked example of its value in an interview: it means a full dump of this database is embarrassing but not catastrophic, because everything in it is material that is published in certificates anyway.
- **Work request.** Holds the idempotency key alongside the operation, which is what lets a retry find its original.
- **Audit.** "append-only; no `UPDATE`/`DELETE` grants" — enforced by database permissions, not by application discipline.
  - Worked example of why permissions rather than discipline: an application-level rule against modifying audit rows is one bug away from being violated, and the violation is invisible. A missing `UPDATE` grant means the statement fails even if an attacker gets to run arbitrary SQL as the service.
- **Idempotency record.** `(tenant_id, idempotency_key)` holding the request hash and the result id.
  - The **request hash** is how "same key, different payload" is detected: the stored hash is compared against the new request's.
  - "retained ≥ the client retry horizon" — the record must outlive the longest period a client might retry. Worked example: a client retries for up to 24 hours, so a 1-hour retention means a retry at hour 2 finds no record and creates a duplicate certificate. The retention window is a correctness parameter, not a cleanup preference.

> - Schema constraints
>   - Use composite tenant ownership keys for every parent/child relationship.
>   - Persist the complete immutable issuance intent before the first signature request.

**What this says in plain words**
Two rules. The first makes cross-tenant mistakes impossible in the database; the second makes retried signing safe.

1. **Composite tenant ownership keys everywhere.** Every foreign key includes `tenant_id`, so a child row can only ever reference a parent belonging to the same tenant.
   - Worked example of what this forbids: a certificate row in tenant A referencing a CA in tenant B. With a plain `ca_id` foreign key, that row is perfectly legal and one bad query away. With `(tenant_id, ca_id)`, the database rejects it — the referenced pair does not exist.
   - The strength of this is that it needs no correct code. It is a constraint, so it holds against bugs, against ad-hoc SQL, and against a future maintainer who has never heard of it.
2. **Persist the complete immutable issuance intent before the first signature request.**
   - Everything that determines the certificate's contents is written down *before* the HSM is asked to sign anything.
   - Worked example of why "complete" and "immutable" both matter: a retry after a crash must produce the identical certificate body. If any part of the body were recomputed at retry time — a timestamp, a validity window derived from `now()` — the retry would produce a *different* certificate, and the system could not tell whether the first attempt had already succeeded.

**What this is**
The ownership and uniqueness rules expressed as pseudo-schema. Read it for the *constraints*, not the column list.

**The five things this block is actually asserting**

1. **Every key starts with `tenant_id`.** Not one table is keyed without it. That is the tenant isolation guarantee, written into the shape of the data.
2. **`cert_versions` carries two foreign keys, and the second is the interesting one.**
   - `(tenant_id, cert_id, ca_id) -> certificates` ties the version to its certificate *and* pins which CA that certificate belongs to.
   - `(tenant_id, ca_id, issuer_version) -> ca_versions` ties the version to the exact CA signing version that signed it.
   - Worked example of why the second matters: a CA rotates from version 4 to version 5. Certificates signed under version 4 must remain attributable to version 4 forever, because verifying their chain requires version 4's certificate. Recording only "signed by CA X" would lose that.
3. **`UNIQUE (tenant_id, ca_id, serial)`.** Serial numbers are unique per issuer, which is the X.509 rule, and the database enforces it.
4. **The `intent` / `result` split inside `cert_versions`.** One row, two halves.
   - **Intent** is written before signing: which version this renews from, the reserved serial, the issuer version, the full to-be-signed body (`TBS_DER`) and its hash, the policy generation, the state, an `attempt_fence`, and a `lease_until`.
     - `TBS_DER` is the certificate body in its exact binary encoding. Storing the *bytes*, not the inputs that would regenerate them, is what makes the intent truly immutable.
     - `attempt_fence` is a counter that increments per attempt; it is the mechanism that stops a stale worker from writing a result. Taken apart fully in the rotation section.
     - `lease_until` is when the current worker's claim on this row expires, so a crashed worker's work becomes available again after a bounded wait rather than forever.
   - **Result** is written after signing: the signed certificate, the chain hash, the validity window, and the revocation timestamp.
   - Worked example of why one row rather than two tables: the state transition from "intent recorded" to "result recorded" is then a single-row update, which is atomic in any relational database with no coordination at all.
5. **`outbox PRIMARY KEY (operation_id, transition_version)` — "inserted in the same transaction as the corresponding state transition".**
   - The **transactional outbox** pattern. Events to be published are inserted into a database table inside the same transaction as the state change they describe, and a separate process reads that table and publishes them.
   - Worked example of the problem it solves: the worker flips a version to `CURRENT` and then publishes `CertRotated` to the message bus. If it crashes between the two, the state changed and no event was ever sent, so consumers never learn. Conversely, publishing first and then committing can announce a change that then rolls back.
   - With an outbox, the state change and the intent to publish commit atomically. Publication may be delayed, and it may happen more than once, but it cannot be lost and it cannot describe something that did not happen.
   - The key `(operation_id, transition_version)` means one row per transition, so a retried transition does not enqueue a second event.

**What to notice, rather than what it says**

- `parent_ca_id uuid` is nullable, and the comment says `NULL = root`. One table holds both roots and subordinates, and the hierarchy is a self-reference.
- `key_ref text NOT NULL, -- HSM key handle, NEVER key material`. The comment is shouting on purpose. Worked example of the rule it encodes: no migration, no debugging session, and no "temporary" column ever puts a private key in this table. The handle is useless without the HSM, so a leaked database row gives an attacker a string they cannot use.
- `FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, parent_ca_id) REFERENCES cas (tenant_id, ca_id)` — the composite ownership rule applied to the hierarchy itself. A subordinate CA cannot have a parent in another tenant, structurally.
- `version bigint ... -- optimistic lock; serializes issuance per CA`. Worked example of what this serializes: two issuances from the same CA both increment this counter. One succeeds, the other's conditional write fails and retries. The effect is that issuance under a given CA is ordered without any explicit locking.

>   not_before   timestamptz,
>   not_after    timestamptz,
>   revoked_at   timestamptz,
>   revocation_reason text,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, cert_id, version_num),
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, cert_id) REFERENCES certificates (tenant_id, cert_id)
> );
> CREATE UNIQUE INDEX cv_serial ON cert_versions (tenant_id, cert_id, serial);
> CREATE INDEX cv_expiring ON cert_versions (tenant_id, not_after) WHERE state = 'CURRENT';
> ```

**The four columns worth stopping on**

1. **`serial numeric(49) ... -- X.509 serial; positive, <= 20 octets, enforced in code`.**
   - X.509 serials may be up to 20 bytes, which is a 160-bit number — far larger than a 64-bit integer. `numeric(49)` holds up to 49 decimal digits, which covers it.
   - "enforced in code" is the admission that the type alone is not the constraint: `numeric(49)` would happily accept a negative number or one requiring 21 octets. The real rule lives in the issuer.
   - Worked example of why the rule exists at all: serials are conventionally generated with substantial randomness, both to make them unguessable and because a predictable serial has historically been part of certificate forgery attacks.
2. **`tbs_hash bytea NOT NULL -- pinned issuance intent`.**
   - A hash of the exact bytes that will be signed, stored before signing.
   - Worked example of its use: a worker resuming an interrupted issuance recomputes the body, hashes it, and compares. Equal means this is genuinely the same issuance and it is safe to proceed or to accept an existing signature. Different means something changed, and the attempt must not continue.
3. **`attempt_fence bigint NOT NULL DEFAULT 0`.**
   - A **fencing token**: a number that only increases, used to reject work from a process that has been superseded.
   - Worked example of the failure it prevents: worker A claims the row at fence 7, stalls for two minutes on a garbage-collection pause, and its lease expires. Worker B claims the row, bumps the fence to 8, and completes the issuance. Worker A wakes up and tries to write its result. Its write is conditional on fence 7, the row is at 8, and the write is rejected. Without the fence, worker A would overwrite a completed issuance with its own stale result.
4. **`pem_chain text -- public material only; NULL while PENDING`.**
   - Nullable by design: the row exists as intent before any signature exists. The comment restates the no-private-material rule.

**The two indexes**

- `cv_serial` — unique serial, scoped per certificate here. The schema notes below explain why that scope is deliberately simplified.
- `cv_expiring ON (tenant_id, not_after) WHERE state = 'CURRENT'` — a **partial index**, one that covers only rows matching a condition.
  - Worked example of the saving: over three years the table holds perhaps twelve versions per certificate, of which exactly one is `CURRENT`. The partial index is therefore roughly one twelfth the size of a full one, and every entry in it is a row the expiry sweep actually wants. Superseded and revoked versions have expiry dates too, and no sweep ever cares about them.

**What this says in plain words**
The DDL above is deliberately weaker than the real rule, and the real rule is the one to say out loud.

**The difference, and why it matters**

- The DDL enforces uniqueness within one certificate: certificate `X` cannot have two versions with the same serial.
- The correct rule enforces uniqueness within one issuer: no two certificates signed by CA `Y` may share a serial, ever.
- Worked example of what the weaker rule permits: certificate `X` version 3 and certificate `Z` version 1, both signed by CA `Y`, both carrying serial `0x4A2F...`. Now CA `Y` revokes serial `0x4A2F...`. Every verifier checking the CRL sees that serial listed and rejects *both* certificates, because a CRL identifies certificates by serial and nothing else. One revocation silently killed an unrelated service.
- This is why the file flags it: the shortcut is fine in a code sample and wrong as a stated invariant.

**What this says in plain words**
Eight notes. The first four are one argument about serial numbers; the rest are restatements of points already made.

**The serial-number argument, in order**

1. **Uniqueness follows the issuer *name*, not the issuer object.**
   - X.509 identifies a certificate globally by the pair (issuer name, serial). So the namespace a serial must be unique within is defined by the *name* printed in the issuer field.
   - Worked example of why that is a stricter rule than it sounds: two separate CA objects in the database could be configured with the same issuer name. The database sees two distinct rows; X.509 sees one issuer. Serial collisions between them are real collisions.
2. **This design shares one allocation space across all versions of a tenant's logical CA.** Conservative, meaning it allocates from a single pool even where a narrower rule might be defensible.
3. **CA key rotation preserves that allocation space.**
   - Worked example of why this is necessary: CA `Y` rotates from signing version 4 to version 5, keeping the same issuer name. If version 5 started allocating serials from zero, it would immediately reissue serials that version 4 already used, and revoking one would affect the other. Rotation changes the key, not the namespace.
4. **Independent CA objects reusing an issuer name need a shared allocator or a prohibition.**
   - The honest statement of the remaining gap. If two genuinely separate CA objects carry the same name, either they must draw serials from one shared counter, or the system must forbid the name reuse. There is no third option, and pretending the problem does not exist is how the collision above happens.

**The remaining notes**

- **"The X.509 rule is enforced in the issuer code; SQL precision alone is insufficient."** Restates the `numeric(49)` point: the column type bounds the magnitude, it does not enforce positivity or the octet limit.
- **"`pem_chain` is nullable so a `PENDING` row can exist before the HSM signs; that row is the durable issuance intent."** The nullability is not laziness — it is what allows intent to precede signature, which is the foundation of safe retry.
- **"Work-request rows follow the [s3.01](s3.01.Async-control-plane-resource-API.md) shape and are omitted here."** Work requests are a general control-plane pattern, specified once in the async-API question and reused rather than redefined.

## Rotation state machine and static stability — IC3 baseline

The longest section in the source. A state machine, renewal timing, the adoption check, CA rotation, a documented-versus-proposed contract table, the scheduler, the issuance flow with its two transactions around one HSM call, and static stability.

---

> ```text
> issue v(n+1):  PENDING --verified sign + fenced publication--> CURRENT       (v(n) -> PREVIOUS)
>                PENDING --HSM sign fails after retries--> FAILED
> overlap window (>= 7 d): v(n+1) CURRENT and v(n) PREVIOUS both validate
> deprecate:     PREVIOUS --after window AND adoption check--> DEPRECATED (still parseable; alarm if seen)
> revoke:        CURRENT|PREVIOUS --revoke--> REVOKED (terminal; lands in next CRL)
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
Five lines describing every state a certificate version can be in and every way it can move between them.

**The states, in the order a version meets them**

1. **`PENDING`** — the intent exists, the serial is reserved, nothing is signed.
2. **`CURRENT`** — signed, published, and the version consumers get when they ask without pinning.
3. **`PREVIOUS`** — superseded but still completely valid. It has not expired and it has not been revoked.
4. **`DEPRECATED`** — past the overlap window and confirmed unused. Still parseable, but nothing should be presenting it.
5. **`REVOKED`** — terminal. On the CRL.
6. **`FAILED`** — signing did not succeed after retries. A dead end for that attempt.

**Line by line**

- **`PENDING --verified sign + fenced publication--> CURRENT (v(n) -> PREVIOUS)`**
  - Two conditions guard the transition, and both words are deliberate.
    - **"verified sign"** — after the HSM returns a signature, the worker verifies it against the issuer's public key before doing anything else. Worked example of why: an HSM misconfiguration, a wrong `key_ref`, or a corrupted response would otherwise produce a certificate that nothing in the world can validate, published as `CURRENT`, breaking every consumer that picks it up. Verifying costs microseconds and catches all of it.
    - **"fenced publication"** — the write that flips the state is conditional on the attempt fence, so only the attempt that is still the live one may publish.
  - The parenthetical `(v(n) -> PREVIOUS)` says the predecessor moves in the same transaction. Both changes commit together, so there is never an instant with two `CURRENT` versions or none.
- **`PENDING --HSM sign fails after retries--> FAILED`**
  - "after retries", not on first failure. Worked example: a transient HSM timeout should be retried, because the certificate is still wanted. Only persistent failure — a disabled key, a revoked authorization, an exhausted quota — moves the version to `FAILED`.
- **`overlap window (>= 7 d): v(n+1) CURRENT and v(n) PREVIOUS both validate`**
  - For at least seven days, both certificates work. This is the entire safety margin of rotation.
  - Worked example of what the window buys: 3,000 consumers cache the chain. The new version publishes on Monday. By Wednesday 2,900 have picked it up, and 100 are on hosts that have not refreshed. With a seven-day overlap those 100 keep working while someone finds out why. Without an overlap they broke on Monday.
- **`deprecate: PREVIOUS --after window AND adoption check--> DEPRECATED`**
  - Two conditions joined by `AND`, and the capitalization is doing work: time alone is not enough.
  - Worked example of why time alone fails: seven days have passed and 40 hosts are still serving the old serial because their refresh job has been broken for a month. Deprecating on the calendar breaks all 40. The adoption check is what turns "probably safe" into "observed safe".
  - `(still parseable; alarm if seen)` — a deprecated certificate is not destroyed. It can still be read and, technically, still validates. But if telemetry reports a handshake using it, that is an alarm: something is running configuration it should not have.
- **`revoke: CURRENT|PREVIOUS --revoke--> REVOKED (terminal; lands in next CRL)`**
  - Either a current or a previous version can be revoked, and revocation is one-way. There is no un-revoke.
  - Worked example of why terminal is right: un-revoking would mean a verifier that saw the certificate on a CRL and cached that fact must somehow learn it was a mistake. Nothing in the CRL model supports that. The correct recovery from a mistaken revocation is to issue a new certificate, not to reverse the revocation.

> - Renewal timing
>   - Renew at `2/3` of lifetime: a `90 d` certificate renews on day `60`.
>   - That leaves `30 d` to detect and fix consumers that did not switch.
>   - The mechanism reference ([s5.01](s5.01.PKI-KMS-mechanisms.md) C.5) uses `50%` for `24 h` certificates
>   - The fraction is a knob, the principle is "renew well before expiry, with jitter".
>   - The OCI rule fields are renewal_interval and advance_renewal_period.
>     - P90D and P30D are this design's assumed values.
>     - Imported certificates and externally managed private-key flows require the supported manual renewal path.

**What this says in plain words**
Renew early. The specific fraction is arguable; renewing early is not.

1. **Two thirds of a 90-day lifetime is day 60, leaving 30 days.**
   - That 30 days is not slack, it is the incident-response budget. Worked example of consuming it: renewal runs on day 60 and succeeds. Telemetry shows 12 hosts still on the old serial on day 63. Someone investigates on day 65, finds a stuck config-management agent, and fixes it on day 67. Twenty-three days still remain. Nothing was ever at risk.
2. **The reference design uses 50% for 24-hour certificates.**
   - Short-lived certificates renew at the halfway point — every 12 hours. The fraction is smaller because the absolute window is tiny: two thirds of 24 hours leaves 8 hours of margin, which is less than one overnight.
   - The general rule: the margin must be long enough for a *human* to notice and act. For a 90-day certificate, 30 days is generous. For a 24-hour certificate, 12 hours is about the minimum that survives a weekend.
3. **"The fraction is a knob, the principle is 'renew well before expiry, with jitter'."**
   - **Jitter** means randomizing each certificate's renewal time by a small amount instead of renewing everything at exactly two thirds.
   - Worked example of what jitter prevents: 1,000,000 certificates all issued during a single migration weekend would all reach day 60 on the same day, producing 1,000,000 renewals in 24 hours instead of the steady 11,100. That burst hits the HSM's signature-per-second ceiling and turns a routine sweep into an incident. Spreading each renewal randomly across a window flattens it.
   - This is the same reasoning as jitter in retry backoff: independent clients that agree on a schedule stop being independent.
4. **The OCI knobs: `renewal_interval` and `advance_renewal_period`.**
   - These are the actual field names in Oracle's certificate service, expressed as ISO 8601 durations. `P90D` is ninety days; `P30D` is thirty days.
   - `renewal_interval` = how long a certificate lives. `advance_renewal_period` = how far before expiry renewal begins. The two-thirds rule is `P90D` with `P30D`, which is the same statement in the platform's vocabulary.
   - Using the platform's own field names in an interview is a small, cheap signal that you have read its documentation rather than generalized from elsewhere.
5. **"Imported certificates and externally managed private-key flows require the supported manual renewal path."**
   - Automatic renewal requires the service to be able to produce the next certificate, which requires it to control the key or to receive a fresh CSR.
   - Worked example: a certificate issued by a public CA and imported into the service cannot be auto-renewed, because this service is not its issuer and has no authority to make another. Someone must obtain the new certificate externally and import it.
   - Naming this exception matters because "everything auto-renews" is a claim that will be tested, and the honest answer is "everything we issue auto-renews".

> - Adoption check before deprecating
>   - Data-plane telemetry answers "which certificate serial did handshakes use in the last `24 h`?"
>   - Do not deprecate `PREVIOUS` while its serial still serves traffic.
>   - Without telemetry, deprecation is a guess
>   - That guess is the classic rotation outage.

**What this says in plain words**
Before retiring an old certificate, look at whether anything is still using it. The way you look is by asking the data plane what serial numbers it has seen.

**Why the question is phrased as it is**

- "Which certificate *serial* did handshakes use in the last 24 hours" — the serial, because that identifies a specific version rather than the logical certificate.
- "Handshakes", because that is ground truth. The control plane's record of what it published is not evidence of what is deployed.
- "In the last 24 hours", because absence over a long enough window is the signal. Worked example of a window that is too short: a batch job runs weekly and presents the old certificate every Sunday. A 1-hour window on a Tuesday sees nothing and declares the old version unused; deprecating it breaks the job five days later.

**"That guess is the classic rotation outage"**

- The failure this describes is common enough to have a shape. It goes: rotation completes, the calendar says the window is over, the old version is retired, and some population of consumers that never refreshed immediately breaks.
- Worked example with numbers: 3,000 consumers, 2,980 refreshed within a day, 20 on hosts whose refresh agent has been failing silently. Nobody noticed, because 20 hosts serving a valid old certificate look exactly like 20 healthy hosts. The moment the old version is retired, those 20 fail, all at once, with an error about an unknown certificate — and the change that caused it happened days after the rotation everyone was watching.
- The adoption check converts this from a timing gamble into a measurement. The general principle, already stated in the architecture section: when you give up the ability to *force* a change, you must buy the ability to *observe* it.

> - CA rotation is the same machine one level up, plus cross-signing
>   - Create a new HSM key and a new CA signing version (`PENDING`).
>   - Cross-sign: the old CA version signs a certificate for the new CA's public key.
>   - Cross-signing can preserve a path to the old anchor only when client path builders and certificate constraints accept that path.
>   - Distribute the new chain to trust stores first
>   - Switch the signer only after coverage is high enough.
>   - Restrict the old key to required CRL signing after issuer cutover.
>   - Retain that capability through the required final post-expiry scheduled CRL publication.
>   - Full treatment: [s2.02](s2.02.Certificate-renewal-and-CA-rollover.md).

**What this says in plain words**
Rotating the CA's own key uses the same `PENDING → CURRENT → PREVIOUS` machine, with one extra trick to stop everything breaking at the moment of the switch.

**Why CA rotation is harder than certificate rotation**

- Rotating a certificate affects the things presenting it — a handful of hosts.
- Rotating a CA affects everything that *verifies* certificates signed by it, which is every client in the fleet, including ones nobody maintains.
- Worked example of the naive failure: the CA switches to a new key on Tuesday. Every certificate issued from Tuesday chains to a CA certificate that is not in anyone's trust store. Every verification fails, fleet-wide, immediately.

**The steps, in order**

1. **Create a new HSM key and a new CA signing version in `PENDING`.** Same shape as a certificate version: it exists, it is not yet in use.
2. **Cross-sign: the old CA version signs a certificate for the new CA's public key.**
   - **Cross-signing** means the new CA's public key is certified twice: once by itself (or by its parent) and once by the *old* CA.
   - Worked example of what this buys: a client that only trusts the old CA is presented with a certificate signed by the new CA. Ordinarily it rejects it. With a cross-signed certificate available, the client can build a chain — new certificate, new CA certificate signed by old CA, old CA — and every link is verifiable using only the old anchor it already trusts. The same certificate works for both old and new clients.
3. **"Cross-signing can preserve a path to the old anchor only when client path builders and certificate constraints accept that path."**
   - The honest caveat, and the reason cross-signing is not a magic wand.
   - A **path builder** is the part of a TLS client that assembles a chain from the certificates it was sent plus the ones it holds. Implementations differ in how hard they try.
   - Worked example of a path builder that fails: some older libraries build exactly one chain — the first one they can construct — and if it does not terminate at a trusted root they give up, without backtracking to try the cross-signed alternative. The cross-signed path exists and the client never finds it.
   - Worked example of a certificate constraint that blocks it: the old CA certificate carries a `pathLenConstraint` of 0, meaning it may not sign another CA. Cross-signing the new CA would violate that constraint, and a correct verifier rejects the path.
   - So cross-signing works, when tested against the clients you actually have. It is not a property you can assume.
4. **"Distribute the new chain to trust stores first; switch the signer only after coverage is high enough."**
   - Order matters absolutely. Distribute, measure, *then* switch.
   - Worked example of the reversed order: switch the signer on Tuesday and start distributing the new root. Between Tuesday and whenever distribution completes, every client without the new root fails. The whole point is that distribution is slow and uneven, which is exactly why it must finish before anything depends on it.
   - "Coverage is high enough" is measured, again, from telemetry, not assumed from a rollout dashboard.
5. **"Restrict the old key to required CRL signing after issuer cutover."**
   - Once the new CA is signing, the old key still has one job: signing CRLs for the certificates it issued.
   - Worked example of why it cannot simply be destroyed: a certificate issued under the old CA last month is valid for another two months and might be compromised tomorrow. Revoking it means listing its serial on a CRL signed by the *old* CA — nothing else can sign that list, because verifiers check the CRL's signature against its issuer.
   - "Restrict" means reducing the key's permissions to exactly CRL signing and nothing else. It can no longer issue certificates, so a compromise of the old key is far less dangerous than it was.
6. **"Retain that capability through the required final post-expiry scheduled CRL publication."**
   - Keep the old key until one CRL has been published *after* the last certificate it issued has expired.
   - Worked example of the reasoning: the last old-CA certificate expires on 1 June. A verifier fetching a CRL on 2 June still wants a validly signed, unexpired CRL — an expired CRL is a hard failure for strict verifiers even though there is nothing left to revoke. Publishing one final CRL after that date, then retiring the key, closes the window cleanly.

> - OCI contract boundaries
>
> | Topic | Documented OCI behavior | Proposed design in this file |
> |---|---|---|
> | Version stages | `CURRENT`, `PENDING`, `LATEST`, `PREVIOUS`, `DEPRECATED`, `FAILED`, `PENDING_ACTIVATION` (externally managed CA) | Same names minus `LATEST` and `PENDING_ACTIVATION`; adds `REVOKED` as a terminal state |
> | Deprecated versions | A `DEPRECATED` version cannot be made current again | Same; `DEPRECATED` is one-way and only reached after the adoption check |
> | Revocation | Tracked as `revocation_status` on a version; revoking a CA version is supported for subordinate CAs only | `REVOKED` state plus `revoked_at` and reason; root revocation is a trust-store change, not an API call |
> | Renewal | Creates another version with new contents and validity; `renewal_interval` and `advance_renewal_period` are ISO 8601 durations | Scheduler sweep at `2/3` of lifetime with jitter; same two knobs |
> | Old versions | Not deleted automatically; service limits cap versions per certificate | Retain `PREVIOUS` through the overlap; garbage-collect `DEPRECATED` after audit retention |
> | CA key custody | Requires an HSM-protected asymmetric Vault key; software-protected keys are not supported; RSA `2048`/`4096` or ECDSA `NIST_P384` | Same; the service stores only `key_ref` |
> | Hierarchy | Path length constraint `0–10` for subordinate CAs; mixed key families in one chain are not supported | Two-tier by default; one key family per chain |
> | CRL | Published to a dedicated Object Storage bucket per CA; CDP URL may substitute `{issuing_ca_version_number}` | Same bucket-plus-CDN model; one CRL object per CA signing version |

**What this table is for, and why it is unusual**
Two columns: what Oracle's service actually documents, and what this design proposes. Keeping them separate is a discipline most answers skip.

**Why the separation is worth the space**

- It makes every claim in the answer checkable. A reader can see exactly which statements rest on documentation and which are design choices.
- In an Oracle interview it is a strong position: the design speaks the platform's vocabulary, cites its documented behavior, and states precisely where and why it diverges.
- Worked example of the alternative going wrong: an answer that blends the two and asserts "the service supports `REVOKED` as a version state". If the interviewer knows the documented state list and `REVOKED` is not on it, the candidate is now defending an error instead of explaining a design.

**Row by row**

- **Version stages.** The documented list has seven; this design uses five of them and adds one.
  - **Dropped `LATEST`.** Worked example of why it is confusing here: `LATEST` and `CURRENT` can differ — the newest version created is not necessarily the one in use. Two overlapping notions of "the newest one" is exactly the ambiguity this design's `PENDING → CURRENT` machine is trying to remove.
  - **Dropped `PENDING_ACTIVATION`.** It exists for externally managed CAs, which this design does not cover.
  - **Added `REVOKED`.** Because revocation is central to the question and deserves to be a state rather than a flag.
- **Deprecated versions: one-way in both columns.** The design adds the adoption check as an additional precondition, which is stricter than the platform requires, not looser.
- **Revocation.**
  - Documented: a `revocation_status` field, and CA-version revocation only for *subordinate* CAs.
  - Proposed: a state plus `revoked_at` and a reason.
  - "Root revocation is a trust-store change, not an API call" is the important line. Worked example of why a root cannot be revoked by API: revocation works by a CA publishing a signed statement about a certificate it issued. A root is self-signed, so the only entity that could revoke it is itself, and no verifier would have reason to consult that list. The only way to stop trusting a root is to remove it from every trust store — a distribution problem, not an API problem. This is also why root compromise is catastrophic in a way subordinate compromise is not.
- **Renewal.** Same two knobs, with jitter added on this design's side.
- **Old versions.** Documented: not deleted automatically, with a service limit on versions per certificate.
  - Proposed: retain `PREVIOUS` through the overlap, then garbage-collect `DEPRECATED` after audit retention has elapsed.
  - Worked example of why audit retention gates deletion rather than the overlap: an auditor in eleven months may ask what was serving on a given date. That requires the version row, long after it has stopped being useful operationally.
- **CA key custody.** Documented and matched exactly: HSM-protected keys only, software keys not supported, and a fixed algorithm list (RSA 2048 or 4096, or ECDSA NIST P-384).
  - "the service stores only `key_ref`" restates the custody rule one more time. It appears in the architecture, the data model, the DDL comment, and here — four times, because it is the security claim the whole design rests on.
- **Hierarchy.**
  - **`pathLenConstraint` 0–10.** The extension that limits how many further CAs may appear below this one in a chain. A value of 0 means this CA may sign certificates but no further CAs.
  - Worked example of what it prevents: without a path-length constraint, a tenant's subordinate CA could sign another subordinate CA, which could sign another, and the hierarchy grows in ways nobody authorized. Setting 0 on the tenant CA makes it a leaf issuer permanently.
  - **"Mixed key families in one chain are not supported."** An RSA CA cannot sit above an ECDSA subordinate. Worked example of the practical consequence: migrating a hierarchy from RSA to ECDSA cannot be done one level at a time; it requires a parallel hierarchy and a full rotation.
  - This design chooses **two-tier by default** — a root and one layer of tenant subordinates. Worked example of why not deeper: every extra tier adds a certificate to every chain sent in every handshake, and adds another key to protect and rotate, for no isolation benefit that a subordinate per tenant does not already provide.
- **CRL.** A dedicated bucket per CA, with the CDP URL able to substitute `{issuing_ca_version_number}`.
  - That substitution is what allows "one CRL object per CA signing version". Worked example of why per-version CRLs are right: a certificate issued under CA version 4 must be checked against a CRL signed by version 4. Certificates from version 5 have their own list, signed by version 5. One combined list could not be signed by both keys.

> - Renewal scheduling flow
>   - Select due certificates through the expiry index with bounded batches.
>   - Under the certificate lock, compare the expected current version.
>   - Upsert one durable renewal intent for that base version and its dispatch outbox.
>   - Resume an existing incomplete intent instead of treating every existing row as complete.
>   - A queue deduplication key reduces redundant delivery but is not the correctness boundary.

**What this says in plain words**
Five rules for the sweep. The last one is the one people get wrong.

1. **Select through the expiry index with bounded batches.**
   - The index is `cv_expiring ON (tenant_id, not_after) WHERE state = 'CURRENT'`. Bounded batches means `LIMIT 1000`, not "everything due".
   - Worked example of why the bound matters: after a scheduler outage, 200,000 certificates are overdue. An unbounded sweep loads 200,000 rows into memory, holds one enormous transaction, and floods the queue in a single burst. Bounded batches of 1,000 make the recovery incremental, restartable, and paced.
2. **Under the certificate lock, compare the expected current version.**
   - The row was selected outside the lock, so by the time it is processed the state may have changed. Re-checking under the lock is what makes the decision sound.
   - Worked example: the query returns certificate `X` at version 7. Before the sweep processes it, an operator renews manually and it becomes version 8. Under the lock the sweep sees 8, not the 7 it expected, and skips — correctly, because the renewal it wanted has already happened.
3. **Upsert one durable renewal intent for that base version, and its dispatch outbox, together.**
   - **Upsert** means insert if absent, read the existing row if present. Combined with `UNIQUE (tenant_id, cert_id, base_version)`, this makes the intent exactly-once per base version, enforced by the database.
   - The outbox row goes in the same transaction, so an intent can never exist without its dispatch being recorded.
4. **"Resume an existing incomplete intent instead of treating every existing row as complete."**
   - The subtle bug this forbids: the upsert finds an existing intent row, the code concludes "already handled", and moves on.
   - Worked example of the resulting silence: an intent was created last Tuesday and its worker crashed before signing. Every subsequent sweep finds the row, assumes it is done, and skips. The certificate is never renewed and no error is ever logged, because from the sweep's point of view everything is fine. It expires 60 days later.
   - The correct behavior: an existing row is checked for *state*. Complete means skip; incomplete means resume.
5. **"A queue deduplication key reduces redundant delivery but is not the correctness boundary."**
   - The distinction to state explicitly in an interview.
   - Queue deduplication is best-effort and time-bounded. Worked example: a deduplication window of 5 minutes drops a duplicate sent at minute 3 and passes one sent at minute 7. It also does nothing at all if the queue fails over, or if the message is redelivered after an acknowledgement timeout.
   - The real boundary is the `UNIQUE (tenant_id, cert_id, base_version)` constraint. Two messages that both arrive produce one intent, because the second insert fails. The database is the thing that cannot be wrong; the queue is an optimization on top of it.
   - The general principle: **correctness lives in the durable store; the queue only affects how much work is wasted.**

---

> ```text
> SCHEDULE_RENEWAL(tenant, certificate, expectedBase):
>     transaction:
>         lock certificate; require current_version == expectedBase
>         intent = insert_or_read_unique(tenant, certificate, expectedBase)
>         if intent newly inserted: insert dispatch outbox
>         return intent.operation_id
> ```

**What this is**
The five rules above compressed into four lines of pseudocode. Everything is inside one transaction.

- `lock certificate; require current_version == expectedBase` — rule 2, the lock and the recheck, in one line.
- `insert_or_read_unique(...)` — rule 3's upsert. The word `unique` names the constraint that makes it safe.
- `if intent newly inserted: insert dispatch outbox` — the outbox is inserted only on creation, so re-running the scheduler does not enqueue a second dispatch for an existing intent.
- `return intent.operation_id` — the caller gets the same operation id whether the intent was created now or found from an earlier run, which is what makes the whole procedure idempotent from the outside.

---

> ```python
> def tick_renewals(db, queue, now):
>     """Rotation scheduler sweep: idempotent per (cert, base version)."""
>     rows = db.query("""
>         SELECT c.tenant_id, c.cert_id, cv.version_num, cv.not_before, cv.not_after
>         FROM certificates c JOIN cert_versions cv
>           ON cv.tenant_id = c.tenant_id AND cv.cert_id = c.cert_id
>          AND cv.version_num = c.current_version
>         WHERE c.auto_renew AND cv.state = 'CURRENT'
>           AND %s >= cv.not_before + (cv.not_after - cv.not_before) * 2 / 3
>         ORDER BY cv.not_after LIMIT 1000""", (now,))
>     for r in rows:
>         with db.transaction():
>             db.lock_certificate(r.tenant_id, r.cert_id)
>             if db.current_version(r.tenant_id, r.cert_id) != r.version_num:
>                 continue                                    # someone already flipped it
>             intent, created = db.upsert_renewal_intent(r.tenant_id, r.cert_id, r.version_num)
>             if created:
>                 db.insert_outbox(intent.operation_id)       # same transaction as the intent
>         queue.send(dedup_id=f"renew:{r.cert_id}:{r.version_num}",
>                    body={"operation_id": intent.operation_id})
> # Correctness boundary: the UNIQUE (tenant_id, cert_id, base_version) intent row.
> # The queue dedup_id only reduces duplicate delivery.
> ```

**Reading the query**

- The join pins `cv.version_num = c.current_version`, so exactly one version row per certificate — the current one. Without that, every historical version would appear.
- `WHERE c.auto_renew AND cv.state = 'CURRENT'` — two filters. Certificates with auto-renewal off are excluded entirely, which is the imported-certificate case.
- `%s >= cv.not_before + (cv.not_after - cv.not_before) * 2 / 3` — the two-thirds rule as arithmetic.
  - Worked example: `not_before` is 1 January, `not_after` is 1 April (90 days). The lifetime is 90 days, two thirds is 60 days, so the threshold is 2 March. Any sweep on or after 2 March selects this row.
  - Computing the threshold from the row's own dates rather than from a global constant means certificates with different lifetimes are all handled by one query.
- `ORDER BY cv.not_after LIMIT 1000` — most urgent first, bounded batch. Worked example of why the ordering matters under backlog: with 200,000 overdue certificates and 1,000 per sweep, ordering by expiry means the ones closest to expiring are renewed first. Unordered, the sweep might spend its first hours on certificates with 29 days left while others expire.

**Reading the loop**

- `with db.transaction():` opens per certificate, not per batch. Worked example of why: one transaction around 1,000 certificates holds 1,000 row locks for its whole duration and loses all its work on any failure. Per-certificate transactions are short, and a failure costs one certificate.
- `if ... != r.version_num: continue  # someone already flipped it` — the recheck under the lock, with the comment naming the race.
- `db.upsert_renewal_intent(...)` returns `(intent, created)`. The `created` flag is what makes the outbox insert conditional.
- `db.insert_outbox(...)  # same transaction as the intent` — the outbox pattern, with the comment stating the property that makes it correct.
- `queue.send(...)` is **outside** the transaction, and that placement is deliberate.
  - Worked example of putting it inside: the queue send succeeds, the transaction then fails to commit. A message now references an intent that does not exist. Sending after commit means the message can only ever describe committed state.
  - The cost of sending after commit is that a crash between the two loses the message. That is exactly what the outbox row covers: a separate publisher finds the un-dispatched outbox row and sends it. Neither mechanism alone is sufficient; together they give at-least-once delivery of only-committed facts.
- `dedup_id=f"renew:{r.cert_id}:{r.version_num}"` — keyed by certificate and base version, matching the intent's uniqueness. The trailing comments then say plainly that this is an optimization, not the guarantee.

---

> - A failed intent remains visible with a retry or operator-resolution state.
>   - Do not let a failed successor silently exclude the certificate from every future sweep.

**What this says in plain words**
A renewal that failed must stay visible and must not make the certificate invisible to future sweeps.

**The trap, spelled out**

- The intent is unique per `(tenant_id, cert_id, base_version)`. A failed intent still occupies that key.
- Worked example of the silent exclusion: certificate `X` at version 7 gets a renewal intent that fails permanently — say the tenant's policy changed and the requested name is no longer allowed. The intent row sits there in a failed state. Every subsequent sweep upserts, finds the existing row, and moves on. The certificate is never renewed, never retried, and never reported. It expires.
- The rule has two halves:
  - **"Remains visible"** — a failed intent appears in operational views and alarms. It is a work item, not a tombstone.
  - **"With a retry or operator-resolution state"** — either the system will try again on a schedule, or a human must act. Both are acceptable; silence is not.
- This is the same failure mode as the dead scheduler from the architecture section, one level down: something that fails by producing nothing generates no errors, so the alarm must watch the outcome — certificates approaching expiry — rather than the process.

---

> - Issuance flow
>   - Reserve one immutable intent.
>   - Claim an attempt.
>   - Sign the pinned TBS.
>   - Verify and assemble the certificate.
>   - Publish conditionally with audit and outbox.

**What this says in plain words**
Five steps. Read them as: write down exactly what will happen, take ownership of doing it, do the irreversible part, check it, then commit — but only if you are still the owner.

- **Reserve one immutable intent.** The `PENDING` row with serial, issuer version, and `TBS_DER`. Immutable means it will not be recomputed.
- **Claim an attempt.** Increment the fence and take a lease. This is the "I am the one doing this, as of now" declaration.
- **Sign the pinned TBS.** The irreversible step, and the only one outside a transaction.
- **Verify and assemble.** Check the signature before building the certificate around it.
- **Publish conditionally.** The second transaction, which succeeds only if the claim is still valid.

---

> ```text
> ISSUE(intent_id):
>     transaction:
>         lock intent and certificate
>         if intent committed: return recorded result
>         require intent runnable and no live competing attempt
>         persist tenant, base_version, issuer_key_version, serial, TBS_DER,
>                 TBS_hash, validity, policy_generation before first sign
>         increment attempt_fence; persist lease_until
>     signer.authorize_and_sign(intent_id, pinned_key_version, TBS_DER, attempt_fence)
>     verify signature against pinned issuer public key
>     assemble X.509 Certificate(TBSCertificate, algorithmIdentifier, signatureValue)
>     transaction:
>         lock intent and certificate
>         require current attempt_fence and live lease
>         require certificate.current_version == intent.base_version
>         require intent still publishable and issuer/policy permit release
>         persist certificate DER and chain
>         mark old current PREVIOUS; mark new version CURRENT
>         commit result, audit intent, and notification outbox together
> ```

**The shape to notice first**
Two transactions with one HSM call between them. The whole design of this procedure is a response to the fact that the middle step cannot be undone.

**Transaction 1, line by line**

- `lock intent and certificate` — both rows, so no other worker can act on either.
- `if intent committed: return recorded result` — the idempotent replay. Worked example: the work request is redelivered after the issuance already finished. The worker finds a committed intent and returns the existing result without signing anything.
- `require intent runnable and no live competing attempt` — two preconditions. Runnable excludes cancelled and failed intents; "no live competing attempt" checks whether another worker holds an unexpired lease.
  - Worked example of what this prevents: two workers pick up the same message. The first takes the lease; the second, arriving 2 seconds later, sees a live lease and stops rather than making a second HSM call for the same certificate.
- `persist ... before first sign` — the list is the complete issuance intent, and the phrase "before first sign" is the ordering guarantee.
  - `policy_generation` is worth a note: it pins which version of the tenant's policy authorized this issuance. Worked example of its use: a certificate signed under policy generation 12, published under generation 13 which no longer permits that name. The second transaction can compare and refuse to release it.
- `increment attempt_fence; persist lease_until` — the claim. The fence goes up by one, and the lease says how long the claim lasts.

**The HSM call and its two companions**

- `signer.authorize_and_sign(intent_id, pinned_key_version, TBS_DER, attempt_fence)`
  - The fence is passed *to the signer*, not just kept in the database. That lets the signing broker refuse a signature request from a superseded attempt, which is a second, independent line of defence.
  - `pinned_key_version` means the exact CA signing version is named by the intent. Worked example of why: if the CA rotates between transaction 1 and the signature, an unpinned call would sign with the new key, producing a certificate whose recorded issuer version is wrong.
- `verify signature against pinned issuer public key` — before doing anything with the signature, check it.
- `assemble X.509 Certificate(TBSCertificate, algorithmIdentifier, signatureValue)` — the three-part structure of a real X.509 certificate: the body, the identifier of the algorithm used, and the signature itself.

**Transaction 2, line by line — four preconditions before any write**

- `lock intent and certificate` again, because the earlier lock was released at the first commit.
- `require current attempt_fence and live lease` — am I still the owner?
  - Worked example of failing this: the worker stalled for 90 seconds on a garbage-collection pause. Its 30-second lease expired, another worker claimed the intent at fence 8, and this worker is still at fence 7. Its publication is refused. The certificate it signed is real and is now an orphan — which the crash-after-signing bullet below addresses.
- `require certificate.current_version == intent.base_version` — is the world still where I expected?
  - Worked example of failing this: the intent renews from version 7, but version 8 is already current because another path renewed first. Publishing would move the certificate backwards.
- `require intent still publishable and issuer/policy permit release` — the late policy check.
  - Worked example: between transaction 1 and transaction 2 the tenant's certificate was revoked, or the CA was disabled, or the policy generation changed such that the name is no longer permitted. A signed certificate exists, and this check stops it from being released. Signing and publishing are separate decisions on purpose.
- `persist certificate DER and chain` / `mark old current PREVIOUS; mark new version CURRENT` — the flip, both halves in one transaction.
- `commit result, audit intent, and notification outbox together` — three things atomically: the certificate, the audit record, and the event to publish. Any of them missing would leave the system inconsistent in a way nothing would detect.

---

> ```java
> // Issuance worker (Java 17): two short transactions around one HSM call.
> public void issue(UUID tenantId, UUID intentId) {
>     Pinned p = tx.run(() -> {
>         Intent it = repo.lockIntentAndCertificate(tenantId, intentId);   // SELECT ... FOR UPDATE
>         if (it.committed()) return null;                                  // idempotent replay
>         it.requireRunnableAndNoLiveAttempt();
>         long fence = repo.incrementAttemptFence(it, Duration.ofSeconds(30)); // lease_until
>         return new Pinned(it.tbsDer(), it.issuerKeyVersion(), it.serial(), fence);
>     });
>     if (p == null) return;
>     byte[] sig = hsm.sign(p.issuerKeyVersion(), p.tbsDer(), p.fence());   // signs inside the HSM, audited
>     X509Certificate cert = X509.assembleAndVerify(p.tbsDer(), sig, issuerPublicKey(p));
>     tx.run(() -> {
>         Intent it = repo.lockIntentAndCertificate(tenantId, intentId);
>         it.requireFence(p.fence());                                       // stale attempt cannot publish
>         it.requireBaseVersionStillCurrent();
>         repo.persistVersion(it, cert);                                    // PENDING -> CURRENT, old -> PREVIOUS
>         audit.log("cert.issue", it, Map.of("serial", p.serial()));
>         outbox.enqueue(new CertRotated(it.certId(), it.newVersion()));     // consumers refresh on event or poll
>     });
> }
> // A crash after hsm.sign leaves a signed but unpublished certificate for a tracked serial;
> // the retry re-signs the same TBS and the fence decides which attempt publishes.
> ```

**What to notice beyond the pseudocode it mirrors**

- **`SELECT ... FOR UPDATE`** is named in a comment. That is the SQL that takes a row-level write lock and blocks other transactions from taking it, which is the concrete mechanism behind "lock intent and certificate".
- **`Pinned`** is a small immutable carrier holding exactly four things: the TBS bytes, the issuer key version, the serial, and the fence. Everything needed for signing and for the later conditional publish, and nothing else.
  - Worked example of why this shape is right: the second transaction re-reads the intent from the database rather than trusting a stale in-memory copy, and compares against `p.fence()`. The only thing carried across the HSM call is what must be identical on both sides.
- **`if (p == null) return;`** — the idempotent replay returns `null` from transaction 1 and the method exits before touching the HSM. The fast path for a redelivered message costs one short transaction.
- **`Duration.ofSeconds(30)`** — the lease. Worked example of choosing this number: it must exceed the expected HSM signing time plus the second transaction, with margin, and it must be short enough that a crashed worker's intent becomes available again quickly. Thirty seconds against a signing call of 50–200 milliseconds is roughly two orders of magnitude of headroom, and a crashed worker blocks progress for at most half a minute.
- **`hsm.sign(...)` sits outside both transactions**, which is the comment on the first line made concrete: "two short transactions around one HSM call". Holding a database transaction open across a network call to hardware would tie up a row lock for the HSM's queue depth.
- **`X509.assembleAndVerify(...)`** — verification and assembly in one call, so there is no path that assembles without verifying.
- **`it.requireFence(p.fence()); // stale attempt cannot publish`** — the fence check, with the comment stating exactly what it buys.
- **`audit.log(...)` and `outbox.enqueue(...)` are inside `tx.run`** — both participate in the transaction, which is what makes the three-way atomicity real rather than aspirational.
- **The closing comment is the honest summary**: a crash after `hsm.sign` leaves a signed but unpublished certificate for a tracked serial, the retry re-signs the same TBS, and the fence decides which attempt publishes. It names the residual problem instead of pretending the code eliminated it.

---

> - Crash after signing
>   - Resume the same serial and exact TBS bytes.
>   - Randomized signatures may differ while identifying the same tracked certificate intent.
>   - A superseded or cancelled signed intent is quarantined and revoked when required.
>   - A database fence prevents stale publication.
>   - The signing broker must independently enforce authorization and writer ownership before physical signing.
>   - A SQL rollback cannot undo a signature already produced.

**What this says in plain words**
Six statements about the one case that cannot be designed away: the signature happened and nothing recorded it.

1. **"Resume the same serial and exact TBS bytes."**
   - The retry does not build a new certificate. It reads the stored `TBS_DER` and signs those exact bytes again, under the same serial.
   - Worked example of the payoff: the first attempt produced a certificate with serial `0x4A2F`. The retry produces a certificate with serial `0x4A2F` and identical contents. Whichever one ends up in the world, the database has a row for serial `0x4A2F` describing it. Nothing untracked was minted.
2. **"Randomized signatures may differ while identifying the same tracked certificate intent."**
   - The subtlety that surprises people. Some signature schemes are randomized — ECDSA, and RSA-PSS — so signing identical bytes twice produces two *different* signature values, both valid.
   - Worked example of the consequence: two byte-for-byte different certificates now exist, with the same serial and the same contents, differing only in the signature. Both verify. Both are the same logical certificate as far as the database and the CRL are concerned, because revocation identifies certificates by serial.
   - This is why the identity that matters is the *intent* — serial plus TBS hash — and not the certificate's bytes. A design that deduplicated on certificate bytes would conclude these were two different certificates.
3. **"A superseded or cancelled signed intent is quarantined and revoked when required."**
   - The cleanup path for the orphan. If a signed certificate cannot be published — because the fence moved on, or the policy check failed — it is recorded as quarantined rather than forgotten.
   - Worked example of why revocation may be required: the certificate exists and is valid. If there is any chance its bytes escaped the worker — logged, cached, sent anywhere — then the safe response is to put its serial on the CRL. "When required" acknowledges that a signature that provably never left the process is a different risk from one that may have.
4. **"A database fence prevents stale publication."** Restates the `attempt_fence` guarantee: the superseded worker cannot write its result.
5. **"The signing broker must independently enforce authorization and writer ownership before physical signing."**
   - Defence in depth. The database checked the fence before the call; the broker checks it again before the hardware acts.
   - Worked example of why one check is not enough: the worker process is compromised, or has a bug, and calls `hsm.sign` without having taken the lease. If the only guard were the database check the worker skipped, the signature happens. With an independent broker check, the request is refused at the boundary that actually controls the key.
   - The general principle: the component that performs the irreversible action must enforce its own preconditions, because every check before it can be bypassed by the thing calling it.
6. **"A SQL rollback cannot undo a signature already produced."**
   - The sentence the entire two-transaction structure exists because of, stated plainly. Everything above is the response to this one fact.

---

> - Consistency boundary
>   - The `PENDING` row in transaction 1 is the durable intent
>   - The flip in transaction 2 is the commit.
>   - Signing the same TBS twice yields the same serial, so a retry after a crash cannot mint an untracked certificate.
>   - Keep database transactions short around the HSM call.
>   - No database transaction alone makes a physical signature atomic with metadata publication.

**What this says in plain words**
Where the line is drawn between "intending to do this" and "having done it".

- **Transaction 1 writes the intent; transaction 2 is the commit.** Between them the system is in a defined state: something was intended, and it may or may not have happened. That is a state the system can reason about, which is the point.
- **"Signing the same TBS twice yields the same serial, so a retry after a crash cannot mint an untracked certificate."**
  - The key property, and the reason the serial is pinned in the intent rather than allocated at signing time.
  - Worked example of the alternative: if the retry allocated a fresh serial, a crash-and-retry would produce two certificates with two serials, only one of which the database knows about. The unknown one is valid, in the world, and unrevocable — because revoking a serial you have no record of is not possible.
- **"Keep database transactions short around the HSM call."** Both transactions are small and neither spans the network call.
- **"No database transaction alone makes a physical signature atomic with metadata publication."**
  - The final honest statement. You cannot make these two things atomic. What you can do is make every intermediate state recoverable, which is what the intent, the fence, the lease, and the quarantine path together achieve.
  - This is the same shape as the two-systems problem in any design that touches an external world: the answer is never "make it atomic", it is always "make every partial state identifiable and resolvable".

---

> - Static stability: the data plane survives a control-plane outage
>   - Consumers cache the full chain and key locally on disk and refresh on interval or event with the ETag.
>   - Handshakes can continue without this service while cached keys, the full chain, and required revocation evidence remain valid.
>   - Corollary interviewers probe: cache on boot.
>     - A consumer that fetches on startup with no local copy dies during a control-plane outage.
>     - Ship last-known-good in the deploy artifact or persist the cache to disk.
>   - Alarm on "certificates expiring in < `14 d`"
>   - During a long outage this is the countdown clock.
>   - Chaos test: isolate consumers for `24 h` after verifying at least that much remaining certificate and status validity.
>   - Separately test near-expiry material and expect the profile's declared failure behavior.

**What this says in plain words**
The property, its three preconditions, the trap, the alarm, and the test that proves it.

**The property and its preconditions**

- Handshakes continue without this service **while three things remain valid**: the cached key, the full chain, and the required revocation evidence.
  - "The full chain" — not just the leaf certificate. Worked example of the failure: a consumer caches only its own certificate and relies on fetching the intermediate. During an outage it has a leaf it cannot build a chain for, and every handshake fails on an incomplete chain.
  - "Required revocation evidence" — for verifiers configured to require a fresh OCSP response or CRL, a stale one fails. Worked example: a strict verifier with a 24-hour freshness requirement and a 48-hour outage rejects a perfectly valid certificate, not because the certificate is bad but because it cannot prove it is not revoked. Static stability has to cover the revocation evidence too, or it only covers half the handshake.

**The trap: cache on boot**

- "Corollary interviewers probe" is the source flagging this as a question that gets asked.
- Worked example of the failure: everything is cached in memory and fetched at startup. The control plane goes down at 02:00. At 02:30 an autoscaling event or a host failure restarts a consumer. It has no local copy, cannot reach the control plane, and cannot serve TLS. The fleet was statically stable right up until anything restarted — which, in a large fleet, is continuously.
- The two fixes are both about having a copy that predates the outage:
  - **Ship last-known-good in the deploy artifact** — the container image contains a working chain, so a fresh instance can serve before it has talked to anything.
  - **Persist the cache to disk** — a restart reads what the previous process had.
- Either works; the anti-pattern is depending on a network fetch to reach a serving state.

**The alarm and what it is really for**

- "Certificates expiring in under 14 days" is the outcome alarm from the architecture section.
- "During a long outage this is the countdown clock" reframes it. In normal operation it catches stuck renewals. During a control-plane outage it answers the only question that matters: how long until this stops being survivable? Worked example: hour 6 of an outage, nothing has expired, and the alarm shows 40 certificates inside 14 days. That is the number that decides whether the outage is an inconvenience or an emergency.

**The chaos test**

- "Isolate consumers for 24 h after verifying at least that much remaining certificate and status validity."
  - Cut consumers off from the control plane for a full day and confirm they keep working — but first check they have at least 24 hours of validity left, so the test measures the thing it intends to.
  - Worked example of the check mattering: running the test against a consumer whose certificate expires in 6 hours proves nothing about static stability. It fails at hour 6 because the certificate expired, which is correct behavior, and the test result is uninterpretable.
- "Separately test near-expiry material and expect the profile's declared failure behavior."
  - The second test is the opposite case, run deliberately: what happens when the cached material *does* expire during an outage?
  - The point is that the behavior should be *declared* rather than discovered. Worked example: does the consumer fail closed, refusing connections, or fail open, accepting an expired certificate? Both are defensible in different contexts, and the unacceptable answer is "nobody knows until it happens".

---

> - Consumer hot-reload
>   - Watch a file or event, rebuild the `SSLContext`, and swap an `AtomicReference` read by a delegating `X509ExtendedKeyManager`.
>   - Envoy uses SDS pushes
>   - Nginx uses `reload`.
>   - Existing sessions keep the old certificate until they close
>   - New handshakes use the new one.
>   - Never require a process restart
>   - Restart storms are self-inflicted outages.

**What this says in plain words**
How a running process picks up a new certificate without stopping. One Java mechanism and two production examples.

**The Java mechanism, unpacked**

- An **`SSLContext`** is the object holding the certificate, the key, and the trust settings used to create TLS connections.
- An **`X509ExtendedKeyManager`** is the component the TLS stack asks "which certificate and key should I present?" on each handshake.
- A **delegating** key manager does not hold the certificate itself — it holds a reference to one and forwards every call.
- An **`AtomicReference`** is a holder whose value can be replaced atomically, so readers see either the old value or the new one and never anything in between.
- Put together: the key manager reads the `AtomicReference` on every handshake. Reloading means building a fresh `SSLContext` and swapping the reference in one atomic operation. In-flight handshakes complete against whichever value they read; the next one reads the new value.
- Worked example of why the indirection is necessary: the TLS stack caches the key manager it was configured with. Replacing the `SSLContext` wholesale would require reconfiguring everything that holds one. The delegating key manager is installed once and never replaced; only what it points at changes.

**The two production examples**

- **Envoy uses SDS pushes.** SDS is the Secret Discovery Service, part of Envoy's dynamic configuration: the control plane pushes new certificates to the proxy over a streaming API and it swaps them live. No file, no signal, no restart.
- **Nginx uses `reload`.** A signal that makes the master process start new workers with the new configuration while old workers finish their existing connections and exit.
- Both achieve the same property by different means, which is the point of listing them: hot-reload is a solved problem in every serious TLS terminator, so requiring restarts is a choice, not a constraint.

**"Existing sessions keep the old certificate until they close; new handshakes use the new one."**

- Worked example: a long-lived gRPC connection established under version 6 stays on version 6 for its entire life, possibly hours. Every new connection after the swap uses version 7. Both are valid during the overlap window, so this is harmless — and it is another reason the seven-day overlap exists.

**"Never require a process restart; restart storms are self-inflicted outages."**

- Worked example of the storm: 3,000 sidecars renew within the same hour and each restarts to load its new certificate. Three thousand processes drop their connections, reconnect, and re-establish TLS at roughly the same time. Connection pools empty, upstreams see a synchronized surge of new handshakes, and retries amplify it. The certificates were all perfectly fine; the outage came entirely from how they were loaded.
- "Self-inflicted" is the right word: nothing failed. The system broke itself carrying out routine maintenance, which is the most avoidable class of outage there is.

## Revocation, key custody, and tenancy — IC3 baseline

Four topics: how revocation is published and why it is weak, who holds which keys, how serial numbers are chosen, and how tenants are kept apart.

---

> - Revocation: CRL freshness versus availability
>   - Revoke is a state flip plus audit plus a CRL rebuild
>   - The CRL is signed, versioned, and carries `nextUpdate ≤ 24 h`.
>   - Relying-party policy chooses soft-fail or hard-fail for unavailable status.
>   - A valid signed revoked result is not an availability error and must not become soft-fail acceptance.
>   - Short certificate lifetimes bound residual exposure for clients that do not enforce fresh revocation status.
>   - Choose lifetime by the controlled workload's renewal capability, not a claimed universal public-web value.
>   - OCSP with stapling is the follow-up: the server staples a signed freshness proof, which removes the client fetch but adds responder availability as a dependency.
>   - Documented OCI behavior: the CRL is published to a dedicated Object Storage bucket configured on the CA, with an optional custom HTTPS CDP URL
>     - OCSP is not described in the overview.
>   - Full treatment: [s2.03](s2.03.Certificate-revocation-CRL-OCSP.md).

**What this says in plain words**
Revoking a certificate is easy. Making the whole world *know* it was revoked is the hard part, and it can never be made instantaneous.

1. **"Revoke is a state flip plus audit plus a CRL rebuild."** Three things: the version moves to `REVOKED`, the audit row records who did it, and the published list is regenerated.
2. **"Signed, versioned, and carries `nextUpdate ≤ 24 h`."**
   - **Signed** so it can be served from untrusted infrastructure. A CDN edge cannot forge a CRL it did not sign.
   - **Versioned** so caches can tell one build from another.
   - **`nextUpdate`** is a field inside the CRL saying when a newer one is expected. Capping it at 24 hours means a verifier knows its copy is at most a day stale, and knows when to come back.
   - Worked example of why the cap matters: without a bounded `nextUpdate`, a verifier has no basis for deciding its cached CRL is too old, and a CRL fetched once could be trusted indefinitely.
3. **"Relying-party policy chooses soft-fail or hard-fail for unavailable status."**
   - A **relying party** is whatever is verifying a certificate — the client end of a TLS connection.
   - **Soft-fail**: if revocation status cannot be fetched, accept the certificate anyway.
   - **Hard-fail**: if it cannot be fetched, reject.
   - Worked example of the tradeoff: with soft-fail, an attacker who can block access to the CRL makes revocation ineffective — every revoked certificate is accepted again. With hard-fail, a CDN outage takes down every connection in the fleet, even though nothing is actually revoked. Browsers overwhelmingly chose soft-fail, which is why CRL-based revocation is widely considered weak on the public web.
   - The design does not choose for the verifier; it notes that the verifier's choice determines what revocation is worth.
4. **"A valid signed revoked result is not an availability error and must not become soft-fail acceptance."**
   - The distinction that is easy to implement wrongly, and the reason this line is in the source at all.
   - There are two different negative outcomes: "I could not reach the revocation service" and "I reached it and it told me, with a valid signature, that this certificate is revoked."
   - Worked example of conflating them: a client's error handling catches every non-success from the revocation check and applies its soft-fail policy. A definitive, signed *revoked* answer is caught by the same handler and the certificate is accepted. Soft-fail was supposed to tolerate *unavailability*; instead it now ignores revocation entirely, and the bug is invisible because everything keeps working.
   - The rule: an unreachable responder is an availability failure. A signed "revoked" is an answer, and answers are never soft-failed.
5. **"Short certificate lifetimes bound residual exposure for clients that do not enforce fresh revocation status."**
   - The honest fallback. If you cannot rely on revocation reaching everyone, make certificates expire quickly instead.
   - Worked example: a compromised certificate with a 90-day lifetime and soft-fail verifiers may remain usable for the rest of its 90 days. The same compromise with a 24-hour lifetime is usable for at most a day, with no revocation infrastructure involved at all.
   - Expiry is the one revocation mechanism that cannot be blocked, cached around, or soft-failed.
6. **"Choose lifetime by the controlled workload's renewal capability, not a claimed universal public-web value."**
   - Do not copy a number from the public-certificate world. Ask what *your* consumers can actually do.
   - Worked example of the real constraint: a fleet whose config-management agent runs hourly and reloads reliably can support 24-hour certificates comfortably. A fleet containing appliances that require a manual certificate upload cannot support anything shorter than months, and choosing 24 hours for them guarantees outages.
   - The lifetime is a function of your renewal automation, not of an industry best practice.
7. **"OCSP with stapling is the follow-up."**
   - **OCSP** (Online Certificate Status Protocol) asks a responder about one specific certificate instead of downloading a whole list.
   - **Stapling** inverts who asks: the *server* periodically fetches a signed status for its own certificate and attaches ("staples") it to the handshake.
   - Worked example of what stapling buys: without it, every client makes its own OCSP request, which is slow, leaks to the responder which sites each client visits, and fails when the responder is unreachable. With stapling, the client gets a fresh signed proof in the handshake and makes no request at all.
   - "adds responder availability as a dependency" is the cost, and it moves rather than disappears: the *server* now needs the responder to be reachable when it refreshes its staple. That is a much better place for the dependency — one server refreshing every few hours instead of thousands of clients per connection — but it is still a dependency.
8. **Documented OCI behavior.** A dedicated bucket per CA, an optional custom HTTPS CDP URL, and OCSP is not described in the overview.
   - The last clause is the useful one: the platform's documented model is CRL-based. Claiming OCSP as an available feature would be asserting something the documentation does not support.

> - Key custody
>   - CA keys: HSM only, referenced by handle
>     - `Sign` is an HSM operation that returns a signature, never a key.
>   - Leaf keys are requester-held in the CSR flow.
>   - A managed exportable leaf key needs encrypted storage and an authenticated delivery boundary.
>   - A nonexportable leaf key instead requires a compatible remote-signing TLS integration.
>   - The CA signing key never leaves the HSM in plaintext.
>   - Managed leaf-key generation and delivery require a separate custody review.
>   - Documented OCI behavior: HSM-protected key versions cannot be exported
>     - The public key can be downloaded
>     - The HSMs are FIPS 140-2 Level 3.
>   - Proof of possession: verify the CSR self-signature before policy checks so denials do not leak which names exist.
>   - Possessing a key proves nothing about authorization for a DNS name
>   - SANs, key usage, and validity are authorized from policy independently of the CSR.

**What this says in plain words**
Who holds which private key, and two separate ideas about proof: proving you hold a key, and being allowed to use a name.

**CA keys**

- HSM only, referenced by handle. `Sign` returns a signature and never a key.
- "The CA signing key never leaves the HSM in plaintext" is the absolute statement. Even a backup of the key, if one exists, is wrapped by another HSM-held key.
- Worked example of the property this guarantees: an attacker with full administrative access to every server the service runs on can request signatures while they hold that access, and every request is logged against the key handle. They cannot walk away with the key. That distinction — bounded, observable abuse versus permanent, silent forgery capability — is the entire reason HSMs exist.

**Leaf keys: three modes, with rising complexity**

1. **Requester-held (the CSR flow).** The consumer generates its own key pair and sends a CSR. The private key never leaves the machine that will use it. Simplest and strongest, and the default.
2. **Managed and exportable.** The service generates the key and hands it over.
   - Needs "encrypted storage and an authenticated delivery boundary". Worked example of what that means concretely: the key sits encrypted at rest, and delivering it requires proving who the recipient is and encrypting it in transit to them specifically. Getting either half wrong turns the certificate service into a key-leak service.
3. **Managed and nonexportable.** The service generates the key, it never leaves, and the consumer never holds it.
   - "requires a compatible remote-signing TLS integration". Worked example of why: a TLS handshake requires a private-key operation. If the key cannot leave, that operation must happen wherever the key lives, so the TLS terminator must support calling out to a remote signer mid-handshake. Most do not, out of the box.
- "Managed leaf-key generation and delivery require a separate custody review" — the design deliberately does not hand-wave this. Moving from mode 1 to mode 2 changes the service's security posture enough that it is a separate decision with its own review.

**Documented OCI behavior**

- HSM-protected key versions cannot be exported; the public key can be downloaded; the HSMs are **FIPS 140-2 Level 3**.
- FIPS 140-2 is a US standard for cryptographic modules, and Level 3 adds physical tamper *resistance* with active response: the device is built to detect physical intrusion and zeroize its keys rather than merely show evidence of tampering.
- Worth citing because it turns "the key is safe" into a specific, externally certified claim.

**Proof of possession, and the ordering rule**

- "Verify the CSR self-signature **before** policy checks so denials do not leak which names exist."
- Worked example of the leak if the order is reversed: an attacker submits a CSR with a deliberately broken signature for the name `payments.acme.internal`. If policy is evaluated first, the error tells them whether that name is permitted, configured, or already taken — information they should not get from a request that was never valid. Checking the signature first means an invalid CSR is rejected identically regardless of the name in it.
- The general pattern: **validate authenticity before evaluating authorization**, so that authorization decisions are never revealed to unauthenticated input.

**The two ideas that must not be confused**

- "Possessing a key proves nothing about authorization for a DNS name."
- Worked example: an attacker generates a perfectly good key pair and submits a valid CSR for `login.acme.com`. Proof of possession *passes* — they genuinely hold that key. What they do not have is any right to that name.
- So the two checks answer different questions, and both are required:
  - **Proof of possession** answers "is this key really yours?"
  - **Policy** answers "may you have a certificate for this name, with these usages, for this long?"
- "SANs, key usage, and validity are authorized from policy independently of the CSR" — the CSR *requests*; the policy *decides*. A CSR asking for a 10-year certificate with code-signing usage gets whatever policy allows, not what it asked for.

> - Serial numbers
>   - Proposed serials use 128 random bits with a positive, at-most-20-octet encoding and an issuer-scoped uniqueness check.
>   - Random serials are the chosen design.
>   - Sequential serials are not universally forbidden by X.509
>   - Public-trust policy requirements are a separate scope.

**What this says in plain words**
Serial numbers are 128 random bits, and the file is careful about *why* rather than asserting a rule that does not exist.

1. **128 random bits, positive, at most 20 octets, unique per issuer.**
   - 128 bits of randomness makes collision negligible. Worked example: at 1,000,000 certificates the probability of any two sharing a serial is on the order of 10⁻²⁷ — far below the probability of the database losing a row.
   - The issuer-scoped uniqueness check is still there as a belt-and-braces constraint, because the consequence of a collision (one revocation killing two certificates) is severe enough not to leave to probability alone.
2. **Why random rather than sequential.**
   - Worked example of what sequential serials leak: serial 4,812 today and 4,915 tomorrow tells an observer the CA issued 103 certificates overnight. Across weeks that reveals deployment cadence, fleet growth, and incident-driven reissuance — all from the public field of a certificate anyone can fetch.
   - Randomness also adds entropy to the signed content, which has historically mattered against attacks that exploited predictable certificate structure together with weak hash functions.
3. **"Sequential serials are not universally forbidden by X.509."**
   - The precision worth imitating. X.509 requires serials to be unique per issuer and positive; it does not require randomness.
   - "Public-trust policy requirements are a separate scope" — the rules mandating entropy come from the CA/Browser Forum baseline requirements, which govern publicly trusted CAs. This is a private CA, so those rules do not apply to it, and it adopts the practice because the reasoning is good rather than because a rule compels it.
   - Worked example of why this matters in an interview: saying "X.509 requires random serials" is wrong and checkable. Saying "X.509 requires uniqueness; public-trust policy requires entropy; we adopt entropy anyway for these two reasons" is correct and demonstrates you know which rule comes from where.

> - Multi-tenancy and authorization (JD keyword)
>   - Every resource lives in a tenant compartment
>   - Policy is checked at the gateway with OCI-style statements.
>   - Quotas: certificates per tenant and issue rate per tenant through a token bucket ([s1.01](s1.01.Distributed-rate-limiter.md)).
>   - Cross-tenant isolation test: tenant A's token must get `404`, not `403`, on tenant B's certificate ids so existence never leaks.
>   - Defense in depth: one subordinate CA per tenant with X.509 Name Constraints for that tenant's names, so a policy bug in tenant A's rules still cannot produce a certificate for tenant B's names.
>   - Full treatment: [s2.05](s2.05.Certificate-and-key-authorization.md).

**What this says in plain words**
Four isolation mechanisms, and the last one is the one that makes the answer strong.

1. **Compartments and gateway policy.** Every resource belongs to a tenant compartment, and policy is evaluated per request at one place. Already covered in the architecture section.
2. **Quotas on two dimensions: certificates per tenant, and issue rate per tenant.**
   - Two limits because they fail differently. Worked example: one tenant could create 10,000,000 certificates slowly, exhausting storage and the metadata store, or 10,000 certificates in one minute, exhausting the shared HSM's signature budget. Capping only the count leaves the burst open; capping only the rate leaves unbounded accumulation.
   - The token bucket is the rate mechanism, shared with the rate-limiter question.
3. **The `404`-not-`403` test, stated as a *test*.**
   - Phrasing it as an isolation test rather than a design note is deliberate: it is something an interviewer can ask you to demonstrate, and something a test suite can assert.
4. **Defence in depth: one subordinate CA per tenant, with X.509 Name Constraints.**
   - **Name Constraints** is an X.509 extension placed on a CA certificate that limits which names that CA may issue for. A correct verifier checks it during path validation and rejects any certificate outside the permitted set — *regardless of what the CA intended*.
   - Worked example of exactly what it buys, which is the sentence in the source: a bug in the policy engine causes tenant A's request for `payments.globex.internal` to be approved. The issuance proceeds under tenant A's subordinate CA, whose Name Constraints permit only `*.acme.internal`. Every verifier that checks the chain rejects the resulting certificate, because the CA that signed it was not permitted to sign that name. The policy bug produced a useless artifact rather than a cross-tenant certificate.
   - This is what "defence in depth" actually means here: the application-layer check (policy) and the cryptographic-layer check (Name Constraints) are *independent*. A bug in one is caught by the other, because they are enforced by different systems — one by your code, one by every verifier in the world.
   - Worked example of the second benefit, blast radius: tenant A's subordinate CA key is compromised. The attacker can issue certificates — but only for `*.acme.internal`, because that is what the constraint on their CA certificate permits. The compromise is contained to one tenant by cryptography rather than by trust in the attacker.
   - The caveat worth knowing: Name Constraints enforcement has historically been uneven across verifier implementations, particularly for older clients. In a controlled internal fleet, where you know what your verifiers are, that is a checkable assumption. On the open internet it would not be.

## Failure, operations, and security

A ten-row failure table, what to observe, retry discipline, tenant isolation, rollout safety, and a three-line runbook.

---

> | Failure | Blast radius | Detection | Degraded behavior | Recovery |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Consumer never adopts the new version | One service; outage when `PREVIOUS` expires | Adoption telemetry shows old serial still serving; expiring-in-`14 d` alarm | `PREVIOUS` stays valid through the overlap | Fix the consumer's reload; extend overlap; never deprecate while the old serial serves traffic |
> | Control plane or metadata store down | All new issue/rotate/revoke in the region | Health checks; work-request backlog age | Handshakes continue on cached bundles; renewals queue | Fail over the store replica; scheduler catches up on the backlog; countdown is the nearest `not_after` |
> | HSM or Vault unavailable or throttled | New signatures fail; running services unaffected | HSM error and `429` rate; issuance success rate | Work requests stay `IN_PROGRESS`; retries with jitter; per-tenant `429` with `Retry-After` | Raise quota; spread across per-environment keys; renewals were scheduled with `30 d` of slack |
> | Crash between HSM sign and version flip | One certificate version | Reconciler finds `PENDING` row older than a threshold | Version stays `PENDING`; `CURRENT` unchanged | Re-sign the same TBS (same serial) or mark `FAILED` and CRL the serial |
> | Stale CRL (builder or publish failure) | Verifiers accept revoked certificates (soft-fail) or reject everything (hard-fail) | CRL age gauge; alarm when `nextUpdate − now` < 2× publish interval | Old CRL keeps being served from the CDN | Restart the builder; re-sign; short TTLs bound the exposure |
> | Subordinate CA key compromise | Its issued leaves and identities | Anomalous signing and custody evidence | Stop affected signing; strict verifiers may reject the chain | Revoke through the parent and reissue through an uncompromised issuer; verify client adoption |
> | Root CA key compromise | Entire trust hierarchy | Unexpected root signing or custody breach | Stop affected signing and treat the anchor as untrusted | Distribute a new independently trusted root out of band; do not rely on cross-signing by the compromised root |
> | Rotation scheduler stalls or double-runs | Expiry risk or duplicate delivery | Expiry inventory and intent age | Durable renewal identity prevents a second logical renewal | Resume incomplete intents and catch up within HSM quotas |
> | Audit write fails | Compliance gap, not a serving outage | Audit write error rate; page immediately | Mutating operations fail closed (audit is in the same transaction) | Restore the audit store; reads and handshakes never depended on it |
> | Clock skew on a consumer | One host reports "not yet valid" | `notBefore` rejections in client logs | Back-date `notBefore` by `5 min` | NTP fix; monitoring on drift |

**What this says in plain words**
Ten failures with radius, detection, degraded behavior, and recovery. Read the second column first: almost every row's radius is bounded, and the two that are not are both key compromises.

**Row by row**

- **Consumer never adopts the new version.**
  - The classic rotation outage, now as a table row. Radius: one service — but only because the overlap window holds. "Outage when `PREVIOUS` expires" names the deadline.
  - Two detections, deliberately: adoption telemetry (the direct signal) and the 14-day alarm (the backstop that fires even if telemetry is broken).
  - Recovery ends with "never deprecate while the old serial serves traffic", which is the rule restated as an incident instruction.
- **Control plane or metadata store down.**
  - Radius: all new mutations in the region. Not handshakes — that is the whole point of the design.
  - "Countdown is the nearest `not_after`" is the sentence that tells you how urgent the incident is. Worked example: the nearest expiry is 26 days away, so this is a serious outage with no deadline. If the nearest expiry were 4 hours away, it would be a different incident entirely.
  - Detection includes "work-request backlog age", which is better than a queue depth. Worked example: a backlog of 5,000 that is draining is fine; a backlog of 50 whose oldest item is 3 hours old means nothing is progressing.
- **HSM or Vault unavailable or throttled.**
  - "New signatures fail; running services unaffected" — the same shape, one layer down.
  - Degraded behavior includes "per-tenant `429` with `Retry-After`", which is how backpressure is expressed politely: a status code plus a header saying when to come back, so clients do not guess.
  - Recovery's last clause — "renewals were scheduled with `30 d` of slack" — is the two-thirds rule paying out. An HSM outage that would be an emergency in a just-in-time design is a scheduling problem here.
- **Crash between HSM sign and version flip.**
  - Radius: one certificate version. Detection is a **reconciler** — a background process that looks for `PENDING` rows older than a threshold and resolves them.
  - Worked example of the threshold: normal issuance completes in under a second, so a `PENDING` row older than 5 minutes is definitionally stuck.
  - Two recoveries, and the choice matters: re-sign the same TBS under the same serial, or give up, mark `FAILED`, and put the serial on the CRL.
    - Worked example of when the second is right: the intent is no longer publishable — policy changed, the certificate was cancelled — but a signature may exist. Revoking the serial covers the possibility that the signed certificate escaped.
- **Stale CRL.**
  - The blast-radius cell is unusual because it depends on the verifier: soft-fail clients accept revoked certificates, hard-fail clients reject everything. The same failure has opposite consequences.
  - "Alarm when `nextUpdate − now` < 2× publish interval" is a well-chosen threshold. Worked example: publishing every 24 hours means alarming when under 48 hours of validity remain — enough warning for two full publish cycles to fail before anything expires.
  - "Old CRL keeps being served from the CDN" is the degraded behavior, and it is benign in the short term: a stale CRL is better than no CRL, because at least it validates.
- **Subordinate CA key compromise.**
  - Radius: its issued leaves. Contained, because of the per-tenant subordinate design.
  - "Strict verifiers may reject the chain" is the degraded behavior after revoking the subordinate, and it is honest — revoking a CA breaks everything under it, which is both the point and the cost.
  - "Verify client adoption" appears again in recovery, because reissuing under a new issuer is another rotation with the same adoption problem.
- **Root CA key compromise.**
  - The only unbounded radius: the entire trust hierarchy.
  - "Do not rely on cross-signing by the compromised root" is the critical instruction and the least obvious one. Worked example of the mistake: the natural instinct is to use the existing root to cross-sign a new root, so clients transition smoothly. But the compromised root's signature proves nothing — an attacker holding that key can sign anything, including their own "new root". A signature from a key in an attacker's hands carries no trust.
  - So the recovery is "distribute a new independently trusted root out of band", which means a manual, non-cryptographic distribution to every trust store. That is why root compromise is the disaster scenario in every PKI: the recovery cannot be automated, because automation would have to be authenticated by the thing that was compromised.
- **Rotation scheduler stalls or double-runs.**
  - Two failures in one row because the mitigations are related. "Durable renewal identity prevents a second logical renewal" is the unique intent row doing its job.
  - "Catch up within HSM quotas" is the constraint on recovery. Worked example: 200,000 overdue renewals against an HSM rated at 1,000 signatures per second is about 200 seconds of pure signing — fine. Against a 50-per-second quota it is over an hour, and that is the number that decides whether the backlog is recoverable before anything expires.
- **Audit write fails.**
  - "Compliance gap, not a serving outage" — the right framing. Nothing stops working.
  - "Mutating operations fail closed (audit is in the same transaction)" is the consequence of the atomicity decision made in the data model: if the audit row cannot be written, the transaction fails, so the operation does not happen.
  - Worked example of the alternative and why it is worse: an audit write that fails independently would let issuance continue with no record. The system would keep working and quietly accumulate unrecorded operations — discovered months later by an auditor, with no way to reconstruct what happened.
  - "Page immediately" because the failure mode is a total stop on mutations, and because an unrecorded window is not recoverable after the fact.
- **Clock skew on a consumer.**
  - Radius: one host. Detection: `notBefore` rejections in client logs.
  - Worked example of the failure: a certificate is issued at 10:00:00 with `notBefore` of 10:00:00. A consumer whose clock is 90 seconds slow believes it is 09:58:30 and rejects its own brand-new certificate as not yet valid.
  - The fix is elegantly cheap: back-date `notBefore` by 5 minutes. The certificate becomes valid slightly before it was issued, which absorbs any skew under 5 minutes with no coordination at all.
  - NTP and drift monitoring are the real fix; the back-dating is the mitigation that works without one.

> - Observe
>   - Issuance success rate and HSM `Sign` latency.
>   - Expiring-in-N-days count
>   - Page at `14 d`.
>   - CRL age and `nextUpdate − now`.
>   - Rotation adoption lag (time from `CURRENT` flip to last old-serial handshake).
>   - Work-request backlog age.
>   - Audit write failures (page immediately).
>   - Per-tenant quota rejections.

**What this says in plain words**
Seven measurements. Two of them page.

1. **Issuance success rate and HSM `Sign` latency.** The health of the write path, and the latency of its slowest component.
   - Worked example of why latency separately: success rate stays at 100% while `Sign` latency climbs from 80 milliseconds to 2 seconds. Nothing is failing yet, and the HSM is clearly saturating. This is the early warning the success rate will not give.
2. **Expiring-in-N-days count, paging at 14 days.** The outcome alarm. It fires whether the cause is a dead scheduler, a broken consumer, or an HSM outage — because it watches the thing that actually matters.
3. **CRL age and `nextUpdate − now`.** Two views of the same artifact: how old the published list is, and how much validity it has left.
4. **Rotation adoption lag: time from `CURRENT` flip to last old-serial handshake.**
   - The single most informative metric in this list, and the least obvious.
   - Worked example: a normal adoption lag of 4 hours means the fleet converges quickly and a 7-day overlap is generous. A lag that has crept to 3 days means the overlap is now only about twice the convergence time, and the margin is thinner than anyone thinks. Nothing is failing; the safety factor is quietly eroding.
5. **Work-request backlog age.** Age rather than depth, for the reason given above.
6. **Audit write failures, paging immediately.** Because mutations stop and the gap is unrecoverable.
7. **Per-tenant quota rejections.** Distinguishes a tenant hitting a limit — which might be abuse, might be a legitimate growth in need — from a system problem.

> - Retry discipline
>   - Exponential backoff with jitter on HSM and store transients.
>   - Bounded attempts and a total deadline per work request
>   - After that the version becomes `FAILED`, never silently retried forever.
>   - Retryable
>     - HSM `5xx` and throttling
>     - Store transients
>     - CRL upload.
>   - Terminal
>     - Policy violation
>     - Bad CSR signature
>     - Access denied
>     - Key disabled.
>   - Retries preserve the serial and exact TBS intent.
>   - They can produce multiple signature attempts, each attributable to that known intent.

**What this says in plain words**
Which failures are worth retrying, which are not, and what a retry must preserve.

- **Exponential backoff with jitter, bounded attempts, and a total deadline.** Both bounds, for the same reason as always: 10 attempts with exponential backoff can span hours, so a deadline is needed independently.
- **"After that the version becomes `FAILED`, never silently retried forever."**
  - The important word is *silently*. A `FAILED` version is visible and alarmable. A job retrying forever in the background is neither, and it consumes HSM quota permanently.
  - Worked example: a certificate whose CA has been deleted will never succeed. Retrying it every 30 seconds forever is 2,880 pointless HSM authorization attempts a day, generating errors nobody reads, while the underlying problem stays invisible.
- **The retryable list.** HSM `5xx` and throttling, store transients, CRL upload — all things that may succeed on their own later.
- **The terminal list, and why each is terminal.**
  - **Policy violation.** The request asks for something not permitted. Retrying sends the same request and gets the same answer.
  - **Bad CSR signature.** The CSR is malformed or fraudulent. It will not improve.
  - **Access denied.** The caller lacks permission. Fixing that is an administrative act, not a retry.
  - **Key disabled.** Someone deliberately disabled the signing key, often in response to an incident. Worked example of why retrying is actively harmful here: an operator disables a key because they suspect compromise. A retry loop hammers the disabled key with authorization attempts, generating exactly the anomalous-signing signal the security team is trying to read.
  - The general rule, same as everywhere: retry what may fix itself; escalate what requires a decision.
- **"Retries preserve the serial and exact TBS intent."** The immutability rule, restated in the retry context.
- **"They can produce multiple signature attempts, each attributable to that known intent."**
  - The honest consequence. Several signatures may exist for one logical certificate — the randomized-signature case from the rotation section.
  - "Attributable to that known intent" is the containment: every one of them shares the serial and the TBS hash recorded in the database, so none of them is untracked, and revoking the serial covers all of them at once.

> - Tenant isolation
>   - Every CA, certificate, and CRL bucket is scoped to a compartment
>   - Policy is evaluated per request at the gateway.
>   - Quotas cap certificates and issue rate per tenant so one tenant's burst cannot exhaust the shared HSM budget.
>   - A tenant's token receives `404` on another tenant's ids, not `403`, to avoid existence leaks.
>   - Per-tenant subordinate CAs with Name Constraints bound what a policy bug can sign.

**What this says in plain words**
The same four isolation mechanisms as the previous section, with one addition worth noticing.

- **"Every CA, certificate, and CRL bucket is scoped to a compartment."** The CRL bucket is the new item. Worked example of why it matters: a shared CRL bucket would let one tenant's revocation list be readable, or worse writable, in the same namespace as another's. Per-CA buckets keep even the published artifacts separated.
- **"so one tenant's burst cannot exhaust the shared HSM budget"** names the specific shared resource quotas are protecting. This is the noisy-neighbour problem, and the HSM is the contended resource because its throughput is a hard physical ceiling that cannot be scaled out by adding servers.
- The `404` rule and the Name Constraints rule are restated verbatim from the previous section, which is a signal about how much weight they carry: they are the two claims to make unprompted when asked about multi-tenancy.

---

> - Rollout and rollback safety
>   - Canary the API fleet by fault domain
>   - The data plane never depends on API health, so a bad deploy pauses issuance and nothing else.
>   - Schema changes follow expand, migrate, contract
>   - A new worker must accept `PENDING` rows written by the previous version.
>   - Version-state transitions are monotonic, so rolling back the worker cannot un-flip a `CURRENT` version.
>   - Chaos test: isolate consumers for `24 h` after verifying at least that much remaining certificate and status validity.
>   - Separately test near-expiry material and expect the profile's declared failure behavior.

**What this says in plain words**
Five rules for changing the system safely, plus the chaos test repeated from the rotation section.

1. **Canary by fault domain.** A **fault domain** is a group of hosts sharing a failure boundary — a rack, a power supply, a switch. Rolling out to one at a time means a bad deploy is contained to hosts that were already expected to fail together.
2. **"A bad deploy pauses issuance and nothing else."** The dividend of the three-zone split, stated as a deployment property. Worked example: a deploy takes the API fleet down for 20 minutes. No TLS handshake anywhere notices.
3. **Expand, migrate, contract.** Add the new column, move the data and the code, then remove the old column — three deploys, never one.
4. **"A new worker must accept `PENDING` rows written by the previous version."**
   - The cross-version compatibility rule, and here the window is short but real: a `PENDING` row written seconds before a deploy is processed seconds after it.
   - Worked example of the failure: version 5 changes how `TBS_DER` is encoded. A `PENDING` row written by version 4 is picked up by version 5, which cannot parse it. The intent is stuck, and the certificate is never issued — with a serial already reserved for it.
5. **"Version-state transitions are monotonic, so rolling back the worker cannot un-flip a `CURRENT` version."**
   - **Monotonic** means transitions only move forward: `PENDING → CURRENT → PREVIOUS → DEPRECATED`, never backwards.
   - Worked example of why this makes rollback safe: version 5 of the worker flips certificate `X` to version 8 and consumers pick it up. Version 5 is then rolled back to version 4. Because there is no transition that moves a version out of `CURRENT` back to `PENDING`, the old worker cannot undo the flip. It sees version 8 as current and continues from there.
   - Worked example of the alternative, if transitions were reversible: a rollback could revert the flip while consumers are already serving version 8. The database would say version 7 is current, the fleet would be serving version 8, and the adoption check would report something impossible.
   - The general principle: **a state machine whose transitions only go forward is safe to roll back the code around.** Reversibility in the data is what makes code rollback dangerous.

---

> - Runbook: expired certificate incident
>   - Issue an emergency short-TTL certificate from the subordinate CA.
>   - Hot-reload the consumer.
>   - Root-cause why the adoption alarm and the `14 d` alarm did not fire.

**What this says in plain words**
Three steps for the incident this whole design exists to prevent. Two to stop the bleeding, one to make sure it does not recur.

1. **Issue an emergency short-TTL certificate from the subordinate CA.**
   - Short TTL on purpose. Worked example: a 24-hour emergency certificate restores service now and forces the real fix within a day. A 90-day emergency certificate restores service and guarantees everyone forgets about it until it expires in three months, probably at 03:00.
   - From the subordinate, not the root. Even in an incident, the root stays off the issuance path.
2. **Hot-reload the consumer.** No restart, because a restart during an incident adds a second variable to a situation that already has one.
3. **"Root-cause why the adoption alarm and the `14 d` alarm did not fire."**
   - The step that distinguishes a runbook from a fix. An expired certificate means *two* independent alarms failed to do their job — the adoption check that should have caught a non-adopting consumer, and the 14-day countdown that should have caught the approaching expiry.
   - Worked example of what this investigation finds: the 14-day alarm was filtered to production and this certificate was tagged staging; adoption telemetry was never wired up for this consumer class at all. Both are one-line fixes that nobody would have looked for without this step.
   - The framing to take away: when a defended failure happens anyway, the defences are the incident. The certificate is just the symptom.

## Alternatives and IC4 stretch

A nine-row option table, three defeaters, four stretch scenarios, the weakest assumption, and two over-engineering warnings.

---

> | Option | What it is | Strength | Weakness | Choose when |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Two-tier hierarchy (chosen) | Root authorizes subordinate issuers | Separates root custody from routine signing | More path and lifecycle management | Baseline multi-tenant PKI with bounded issuer blast radius |
> | Single root | One CA issues everything | Simplest | Root key on the issuance path; compromise means re-issue the world | Dev and test only |
> | Automatic versioned renewal (chosen) | Early renewal with measured adoption | Reduces missed expiry | Requires key/CSR automation and telemetry | Managed keys and consumers capable of automated renewal |
> | CRL primary (chosen) | Signed list on object storage and CDN | No online dependency; cacheable | Freshness bounded by publish interval; soft-fail clients | Internal fleets and LB integrations |
> | OCSP plus stapling | Server presents a fresh signed status | Per-certificate freshness; no client fetch | Responder availability becomes a dependency | Browser-facing public certificates |
> | Async work request (chosen) | `202` plus poll for every mutation | Keeps HSM latency and serialization off the sync path; uniform for Terraform | Every client needs a poll loop | Control-plane resources |
> | Synchronous issuance | `201` with the certificate inline | Simple for interactive flows | Timeouts under HSM throttling; retries duplicate work | Add as a fast lane only for interactive developer flows |
> | Managed CA (OCI Certificates, AWS Private CA) | Buy the signer and versioning | Native compartment authz, audit, LB integration | Limited policy expressiveness; no ACME | The OCI fleet; say it is the team's own product |
> | SPIFFE/SPIRE workload identity | Per-node agents issue short-lived SVIDs | Identity and certificates together; rotation built in | Agent on every node; no long-lived certificates | Service-mesh mTLS at scale; pair with a managed CA upstream |

**What this says in plain words**
Nine options across five independent decisions: hierarchy shape, renewal style, revocation mechanism, API style, and build-versus-buy. Four are marked "(chosen)".

**Decision one: hierarchy shape**

- **Two-tier (chosen).** A root that signs subordinates, and subordinates that sign everything else.
  - Strength: "separates root custody from routine signing". Worked example of what that separation means physically: the root key can live in an HSM that is powered off, in a safe, and brought out for a ceremony twice a year. A key that signs 20,000 certificates a day cannot be treated that way.
  - Weakness: "more path and lifecycle management" — two levels of certificate to rotate, longer chains, more to get wrong.
- **Single root.** One CA issues everything.
  - "Root key on the issuance path" is the disqualifying property. The key that anchors all trust would be online, in use, thousands of times a day.
  - "Compromise means re-issue the world" — as the failure table said, root compromise has no automated recovery. Putting that key on the hot path maximizes the chance of the one failure you cannot recover from.
  - "Dev and test only" is the honest scope.

**Decision two: renewal style**

- **Automatic versioned renewal (chosen).** Early renewal plus adoption measurement.
  - "Requires key/CSR automation and telemetry" is the precondition, and it is real. Worked example of it not holding: a fleet of network appliances that need a certificate uploaded through a web form cannot participate. For those, manual renewal with a long lead time is the only option, and pretending otherwise creates outages.

**Decision three: revocation**

- **CRL primary (chosen).** "No online dependency; cacheable" is the strength — a file behind a CDN has no availability story to manage.
- **OCSP plus stapling.** "Per-certificate freshness; no client fetch."
  - "Choose when: browser-facing public certificates." Worked example of why the split falls there: browsers soft-fail on CRLs, so for public-facing certificates the CRL is close to worthless and stapling is the only mechanism that actually delivers freshness. In an internal fleet where you control the verifiers and can enforce CRL checking, the simpler mechanism is sufficient.

**Decision four: API style**

- **Async work request (chosen).** "Keeps HSM latency and serialization off the sync path; uniform for Terraform."
  - The Terraform note is specific and worth understanding: infrastructure-as-code tools are built around a submit-then-poll model, because everything they manage takes time to converge. A `202`-plus-poll API is the natural shape for them; a synchronous API that sometimes takes 4 seconds is the awkward one.
- **Synchronous issuance.** `201` with the certificate in the response.
  - "Retries duplicate work" is the specific weakness. Worked example: the HSM is slow, the client times out at 5 seconds and retries. The first request is still in flight and will complete. Without a work request to reconcile against, the system now has two issuances for one intent and the client has no way to find out.
  - "Add as a fast lane only for interactive developer flows" is the right compromise: a human at a terminal genuinely benefits from a synchronous response, and a human at a terminal is not the load that breaks the HSM.

**Decision five: build versus buy**

- **Managed CA (OCI Certificates, AWS Private CA).**
  - Strengths are all integration: native compartment authorization, audit, and load-balancer integration already wired up.
  - Weaknesses: "limited policy expressiveness; no ACME". **ACME** is the automated certificate-management protocol that Let's Encrypt popularized and that most modern certificate clients speak. Its absence means consumers cannot use off-the-shelf ACME clients and need platform-specific integration.
  - **"The OCI fleet; say it is the team's own product"** is the most tactically useful cell in the table. The interviewing team builds this service. Designing it from scratch without acknowledging that the managed product exists, and that it is *theirs*, reads as not knowing the landscape. The right move is to design the system while being explicit that in production on OCI you would use the platform's certificate service, and that the design explains what that service has to do internally.
- **SPIFFE/SPIRE workload identity.**
  - **SPIFFE** is a standard for giving workloads cryptographic identities; **SPIRE** is its reference implementation. An **SVID** (SPIFFE Verifiable Identity Document) is the short-lived certificate a workload receives.
  - Strength: "identity and certificates together; rotation built in". The workload does not request a certificate — it receives one continuously, as a property of being a recognized workload.
  - Weaknesses: an agent on every node, and no long-lived certificates. Worked example of why the second is a weakness rather than a feature: anything that cannot renew on an hourly cadence — an appliance, a partner integration, a client certificate embedded in a device — cannot use SVIDs at all.
  - "Pair with a managed CA upstream" is the realistic architecture: SPIRE issues workload identities, and its own signing authority chains up to the organization's CA.

---

> - Defeaters to volunteer
>
> → "Store the CA key encrypted in the database."
> ⇒ Encrypted-at-rest is not custody; the key exists in service memory during every signature.
> → Sign inside the HSM; the service holds a key handle, never key bytes.

**What "defeaters to volunteer" means**
Objections the interviewer is likely to raise, raised by you first, with the answer attached. Volunteering them shows you know where the design is attacked.

**Defeater one, unpacked**

- **The proposal:** encrypt the CA private key and store it in the database.
- **Why it fails:** "Encrypted-at-rest is not custody; the key exists in service memory during every signature."
  - Encryption at rest protects against someone who steals the storage — a stolen disk, a leaked backup, a dumped table.
  - It does nothing against someone who is inside the running process, because the process must decrypt the key to use it.
  - Worked example: the service signs 20,000 certificates a day. Twenty thousand times a day, the plaintext CA private key is in the process's memory. A memory-disclosure bug, a heap dump captured for debugging, a core file written on a crash, or an attacker with code execution on that host all yield the key itself — permanently, silently, and with no log entry anywhere.
  - Contrast with the HSM: the same attacker with the same access can request signatures while they hold the host, and every request is recorded. They never obtain the key. Revoking the compromised host's authorization ends the abuse; there is nothing for them to take home.
- **The answer:** sign inside the HSM, hold a handle.

---

> → "Rotation is an atomic swap of the certificate."
> ⇒ Consumers cache; without overlap every cached client breaks at once.
> → Two valid versions overlap for at least `7 d`; deprecate only after the adoption check.

**Defeater two, unpacked**

- **The proposal:** rotation is one instantaneous switch from old to new.
- **Why it fails:** there is no moment at which every consumer changes. Consumers hold cached copies with independent refresh schedules, and that is by design — it is the static stability the architecture deliberately bought.
  - Worked example: 3,000 consumers refresh on a 5-minute interval with jitter, so in the best case convergence takes minutes and in the realistic case it takes days, because some hosts are asleep, some agents are wedged, and some connections are long-lived. An atomic swap breaks every consumer that has not yet refreshed, at the instant of the swap.
- **The answer:** at least 7 days of overlap, and deprecate only after the adoption check confirms the old serial has stopped serving.
- The underlying idea is general: **any system with caching clients cannot have atomic configuration changes.** The change is a window, not an instant, and the design has to name the window's length and how you know it has closed.

---

> → "Rely on revocation to contain a compromise."
> ⇒ Verifiers soft-fail; a revoked certificate keeps working wherever the CRL is unreachable or cached.
> → Short TTL is the honest control; revocation is the backstop.

**Defeater three, unpacked**

- **The proposal:** a key is compromised, so revoke the certificate and the problem is contained.
- **Why it fails:** revocation is advisory, not enforceable. It works only where verifiers both check and enforce.
  - Worked example: a certificate is revoked at 09:00. A verifier with a 6-hour cached CRL accepts it until 15:00. A soft-fail verifier that cannot reach the CDN accepts it indefinitely. An attacker who can block CRL access from their target's network accepts it for as long as they like, deliberately.
- **The answer:** "Short TTL is the honest control; revocation is the backstop."
  - The word *honest* is doing the work. Expiry cannot be blocked, cached around, or soft-failed. A 24-hour certificate is contained within 24 hours by arithmetic; a revoked 90-day certificate is contained whenever verifiers cooperate.
  - Revocation is still built, and still matters — it closes the gap faster for the verifiers that do enforce. It is a backstop, not a guarantee.

---

> - IC4 stretch — stronger requirement: a certificate per pod
>   - Short-TTL (`24 h`) certificates, no reliance on revocation, SPIFFE-style identity.
>   - Issuance rises `100×`, so now it is a scale problem: HSM pool, per-CA sharding, or an intermediate signing tier.
>   - Delegate a short-lived, name-constrained sub-CA per node or cluster
>   - The HSM signs one sub-CA per node per day instead of one leaf per pod.
>   - Consumer-side renewal at `50%` of lifetime so the CA can be down for half a TTL before any expiry.

**What this says in plain words**
Change the requirement to one certificate per pod with a 24-hour lifetime, and the problem stops being a correctness problem and becomes a throughput problem.

1. **The new requirement.** 24-hour certificates, no reliance on revocation (because expiry does the job), SPIFFE-style workload identity.
2. **"Issuance rises `100×`."**
   - Worked example of where the factor comes from: 90-day certificates renewed once per quarter, against 24-hour certificates renewed daily, is roughly 90× more issuances per certificate. Add one certificate per pod rather than per service, with many pods per service, and 100× is conservative.
   - Three listed responses: an HSM pool, per-CA sharding, or an intermediate signing tier.
3. **The chosen answer: delegate a short-lived, name-constrained sub-CA per node.**
   - This is the idea worth understanding, because it changes the arithmetic rather than adding hardware.
   - Worked example with numbers: 1,000 nodes running 100 pods each is 100,000 certificates per day. Signing each one in the HSM is 100,000 HSM operations daily. Instead, the HSM signs *one* sub-CA certificate per node per day — 1,000 operations — and each node's agent issues its own pods' certificates using that sub-CA. HSM load drops by a factor of 100, and the node-local agent can issue in microseconds.
   - "Name-constrained" is what makes it safe: the node's sub-CA carries Name Constraints limiting it to that node's or cluster's namespace, so a compromised node can only mint identities it was already able to impersonate.
   - "Short-lived" is the second control: the sub-CA itself expires in 24 hours, so a stolen node sub-CA key is useless within a day.
   - The general pattern is **delegation of signing authority under constraints** — the same idea as the two-tier hierarchy, applied one more level down and with a much shorter lifetime.
4. **"Consumer-side renewal at `50%` of lifetime so the CA can be down for half a TTL before any expiry."**
   - The two-thirds rule recalculated for a 24-hour lifetime, and the sentence states exactly what the margin buys: with renewal at 12 hours, the CA can be completely unavailable for 12 hours before anything expires.
   - That framing — margin expressed as tolerable CA downtime — is the right way to choose the fraction at any lifetime.

---

> - IC4 stretch — 10× load
>   - Reads: bundle endpoint stays immutable per version behind the CDN
>     - `10×` reads never reach the store.
>   - Writes: shard the metadata store by tenant with the certificate row as the unit of serialization
>     - Avoid locking one CA row for every issuance by reserving serials per certificate row under the `(ca_id, serial)` constraint.
>   - HSM: partition tenants across HSM partitions or keys so one tenant's burst cannot starve another's renewals.

**What this says in plain words**
Three axes at ten times the load, and the write axis contains a genuine design change.

1. **Reads: nothing to do.** Because bundles are immutable per version, the CDN absorbs the increase entirely. Worked example: 10× the consumers polling with `If-None-Match` means 10× the `304` responses at the edge and the same near-zero origin load.
2. **Writes: shard by tenant, and move the serialization point.**
   - "Avoid locking one CA row for every issuance" is the problem. Worked example of the bottleneck: the CA row carries an optimistic version counter that every issuance under that CA increments. At 10× the load, a tenant with one CA has every issuance contending on one row, and throughput is capped by how fast that row can be updated — regardless of how many workers exist.
   - The fix: make the **certificate row** the unit of serialization instead, and reserve serials per certificate row under the `(ca_id, serial)` uniqueness constraint.
   - Worked example of why that is still correct: the invariant that must hold is "no two certificates from this CA share a serial". That is enforced by the unique constraint on `(tenant_id, ca_id, serial)`, not by the CA row lock. With 128-bit random serials, two concurrent issuances essentially never collide, and if they did, the constraint rejects one and it retries with a new serial. The lock was protecting an invariant the constraint already guarantees.
   - This is a good general move to recognize: **a lock that exists to protect an invariant can be removed if a constraint enforces the same invariant.** The lock serializes; the constraint only rejects the rare conflict.
3. **HSM: partition tenants across partitions or keys.**
   - The noisy-neighbour fix for the one resource that cannot be scaled by adding servers.
   - Worked example: one tenant's migration submits 50,000 issuances. On a shared HSM, every other tenant's routine renewals queue behind them. Across partitions, the burst saturates one partition and everyone else is unaffected.

---

> - IC4 stretch — cost and blast radius
>   - Cell the control plane per region and per availability-domain group
>   - A store failure strands one cell's issuance, not the region.
>   - Per-tenant subordinate CAs mean a CA compromise re-issues one tenant, not the fleet.
>   - Cost drivers are HSM operations and retained versions, not storage
>   - Garbage-collect `DEPRECATED` versions after the audit horizon.

**What this says in plain words**
Two containment mechanisms and one observation about where the money goes.

1. **Cell the control plane per region and per availability-domain group.**
   - A **cell** is a complete, independent copy of the stack serving a subset of traffic. An **availability domain** is a physically separate datacenter within a region.
   - Worked example: with one control plane per region, a metadata-store failure stops issuance across the whole region. With three cells per region, it stops issuance for one third of tenants, and the other two thirds never notice.
2. **Per-tenant subordinate CAs bound compromise recovery.** Worked example of the difference: one shared subordinate compromised means reissuing every certificate in the fleet and getting every consumer to adopt — weeks of work. A per-tenant subordinate compromised means reissuing one tenant's certificates.
3. **"Cost drivers are HSM operations and retained versions, not storage."**
   - The counterintuitive part, worth checking with numbers. Worked example: 1,000,000 certificates at roughly 6 kibibytes of PEM each is about 6 gigabytes — a rounding error in any storage budget. Meanwhile HSM operations are billed per signature or capped by a provisioned throughput you pay for, and retained versions multiply row counts and index sizes in the metadata store, which is the expensive database.
   - So the cost lever is **retention**, not compression or tiering.
4. **"Garbage-collect `DEPRECATED` versions after the audit horizon."** The retention policy that follows: keep versions as long as audit requires, then delete. The audit requirement sets the number, and the number sets the cost.

---

> - IC4 stretch — migration and BYOCA
>   - For an external issuer, generate the subordinate key in the selected HSM and submit its CSR for external signing.
>   - Import the resulting CA certificate chain through the supported externally issued CA workflow.
>   - Importing a root PEM alone does not give the service signing authority.
>   - Legacy consumers (an Oracle DB client that reads a wallet and trusts RSA only) get an `rsa-legacy` profile on its own subordinate with a sunset date, so its longer TTLs never widen the ECDSA fleet's blast radius.

**What this says in plain words**
**BYOCA** is "bring your own certificate authority" — the case where an organization already has a CA and wants this service to issue under it.

1. **The correct flow: generate the key here, get it signed there.**
   - Generate the subordinate's key pair *in this service's HSM*, produce a CSR, and send that CSR to the external CA for signing.
   - Worked example of why this order is the only safe one: the private key is created inside the HSM and never leaves. The external CA only ever sees a CSR — a public key and a name. The organization's existing trust hierarchy is extended to this service without any private key crossing an organizational boundary.
2. **Import the signed CA certificate chain back through the supported workflow.** The result is a subordinate CA that this service can use and that chains to the customer's existing root.
3. **"Importing a root PEM alone does not give the service signing authority."**
   - The misconception this bullet exists to correct, and it is a common one.
   - A root PEM is a *public* certificate. Importing it tells the service to trust that root; it does not confer any ability to sign on its behalf. Signing requires the private key, which stays with whoever holds it.
   - Worked example of the confusion: someone imports their company root PEM and expects issuance to start working. Nothing can be issued, because the service has a public certificate and no signing authority. The missing step is the CSR round trip above.
4. **The legacy-consumer pattern.**
   - The problem: an Oracle database client reads certificates from a **wallet** (Oracle's credential store) and supports RSA only, while the rest of the fleet has moved to ECDSA with short lifetimes.
   - The solution has three parts, and each one does distinct work:
     - **An `rsa-legacy` profile** — a named policy with the algorithm and lifetime this consumer class actually needs.
     - **On its own subordinate CA** — so the legacy material is under a separate issuer.
     - **With a sunset date** — so the exception is time-bounded and someone owns removing it.
   - "so its longer TTLs never widen the ECDSA fleet's blast radius" is the reason for the separate subordinate. Worked example: if legacy RSA certificates with 2-year lifetimes were issued under the same subordinate as the 90-day ECDSA fleet, compromising that subordinate would mean reissuing both, and the 2-year certificates would keep the exposure alive far longer. Separate issuers keep the legacy exception's risk inside the legacy exception.
   - The general pattern worth taking away: **an exception gets its own issuer and an expiry date.** That is how a temporary compatibility concession stays temporary and stays contained.

---

> - Weakest assumption
>   - Consumers reliably report which serial served each handshake
>   - Without that telemetry the adoption check is a guess.
>   - Mitigation: treat missing telemetry as "not adopted", extend the overlap, and alarm on the countdown to `PREVIOUS` expiry.

**What this says in plain words**
The whole safety argument for rotation depends on telemetry, and telemetry is the least reliable part of any fleet.

- **Why this is the weakest link:** the adoption check is what turns deprecation from a guess into a measurement. If the telemetry is incomplete, the check is measuring a subset and reporting it as the whole.
- Worked example of the silent failure: 3,000 consumers, of which 200 run an older agent that does not report serials. The adoption dashboard shows 100% adoption across the 2,800 that report. Deprecation proceeds, and 200 hosts break — and they were invisible precisely because they were old, which correlates with being the ones that had not refreshed.
- **The mitigation has three independent parts, and the first is the important one.**
  1. **Treat missing telemetry as "not adopted".** Fail safe. A consumer that does not report is assumed to still be on the old version, so deprecation waits. Worked example of the opposite default: treating silence as adoption makes every unmonitored consumer invisible, which is exactly backwards — the ones you cannot see are the ones most likely to be broken.
  2. **Extend the overlap.** More time for stragglers, at the cost of keeping more versions valid.
  3. **Alarm on the countdown to `PREVIOUS` expiry.** The backstop that does not depend on telemetry at all. Even with no adoption data, you know when the old version stops working, and that date is alarmable.
- The general principle: **when a safety check depends on a signal, decide what the absence of that signal means, and make the absence conservative.**

---

> - Over-engineering warning
>   - Do not build OCSP, multi-region active-active CAs, or a custom KMS for a `0.2/s` internal issuance service.
>   - Do not build workload identity
>   - Consume the platform's resource principal or SPIFFE identity.

**What this says in plain words**
Two warnings, and the first is anchored to a number.

1. **"Do not build OCSP, multi-region active-active CAs, or a custom KMS for a `0.2/s` internal issuance service."**
   - 0.2 issuances per second is the steady-state rate from the capacity section — about one certificate every five seconds.
   - Worked example of how absurd the over-build is at that rate: a multi-region active-active CA means solving consistent serial allocation across regions, cross-region replication of issuance state, and split-brain resolution for a key that must not sign twice — an enormous amount of difficult, high-risk work, to serve a load a single modest database handles without noticing.
   - Each item on the list is separately unjustified:
     - **OCSP** adds a highly available online responder as a new dependency, to improve revocation freshness for an internal fleet whose verifiers you control and whose certificates are short-lived anyway.
     - **Multi-region active-active** buys write availability during a region loss, for a control plane whose unavailability does not affect handshakes at all.
     - **A custom KMS** means writing your own key management for keys the platform's HSM already holds to FIPS 140-2 Level 3.
   - The discipline being demonstrated: quote the rate, then show that the proposed complexity does not earn its place against it.
2. **"Do not build workload identity; consume the platform's resource principal or SPIFFE identity."**
   - A **resource principal** is a platform-issued identity a workload gets automatically for being a recognized resource, with no credentials to distribute.
   - Worked example of what building it yourself means: bootstrapping identity for a brand-new instance with no credentials is the hardest unsolved-feeling problem in the area, and every platform has already solved it. Reimplementing it produces a worse version of something you get for free, and it is security-critical code.
   - The general rule: consume identity, issue certificates. They are different problems, and only one of them is this service's job.

**What this says in plain words**
A self-assessment grid. The second column is a checklist of content; the fourth column is what actually distinguishes a stronger answer from a merely complete one.

**Why the fourth column is the one to score**
In every row the difference is not more material. It is a different *kind* of claim about the same material.

- **Row: Requirements.**
  - Must land: internal versus public trust, who the consumers are, and revocation and audit as hard requirements rather than nice-to-haves.
  - Adds: naming the non-goals — WebPKI, workload identity, key management — and saying why buying each is cheaper than building it.
  - Separating signal: **"Scopes the PKI instead of designing all of PKI."**
  - Worked example of the failure this describes: PKI is enormous. A candidate who starts designing certificate transparency logs, ACME challenge validation, and a key management service has not misunderstood the technology — they have failed to notice that the question is about an internal issuance service at 0.2 requests per second. The strong move is to say what is out of scope and why, in the first two minutes.
- **Row: Estimates.**
  - Must land: 0.2 writes per second, 3 reads per second, a CRL measured in kilobytes.
  - Adds: letting the HSM signing budget force two conclusions — that CA rotation must not re-issue every leaf, and that renewals need jitter.
  - Separating signal: **"A number changes a decision, not just a slide."**
  - Worked example of passing it: "CA rotation must not re-issue leaves" is not a preference, it is arithmetic. One million leaf certificates against an HSM doing 1,000 signatures per second is about 17 minutes of continuous signing at full capacity, during which no routine issuance happens at all. So the design cross-signs instead, and the estimate is what proved it necessary.
  - Worked example of failing it: reciting the same numbers and then designing exactly what you would have designed without them.
- **Row: Architecture.**
  - Must land: the control-plane and data-plane split, async work requests, HSM handle only.
  - Adds: the cost of each component, and why the metadata store is single-region per cell.
  - Separating signal: **every box has a mechanism *and* a price.**
  - Worked example of the price most often omitted: the gateway's policy check buys one enforcement point and costs a policy-service call on every control-plane request, making that service's availability a dependency of issuance. Saying the second half unprompted is the signal.
- **Row: Deep dive.**
  - Must land: the version state machine with its overlap and adoption check, and static stability on cached bundles.
  - Adds: the two-transaction issuance around the HSM signature, the bound on orphan serials, and cross-signed CA rotation.
  - Separating signal: **"Reasons across the database and HSM boundary."**
  - Worked example of what that means concretely: recognizing that a SQL rollback cannot undo a signature, and then producing the response — pin the serial and TBS in transaction one, sign, publish conditionally on a fence in transaction two, and accept one revocable orphan serial as the residual. That is not a memorized pattern; it is reasoning about two systems whose consistency models do not compose.
- **Row: Operations.**
  - Must land: the failure table, the 14-day expiry alarm, CRL age, and `404`-not-`403`.
  - Adds: per-tenant subordinate CAs with Name Constraints, cells, and a rollback that cannot un-flip a version.
  - Separating signal: **"Volunteers containment before being asked."**
  - Worked example: describing per-tenant subordinate CAs with Name Constraints *as a response to policy bugs*, before anyone asks about multi-tenancy failures. It says you think about what happens when your own authorization layer is wrong, which is a different posture from designing an authorization layer.
- **Row: Wrap.**
  - Must land: the recap and the overlap tradeoff.
  - Adds: naming adoption telemetry as the weakest assumption, first.
  - Separating signal: finding the flaw before the interviewer does.
  - Worked example of why this specific admission is strong: adoption telemetry is genuinely the weakest link — the entire deprecation safety argument rests on it, and it is the part of any fleet most likely to be incomplete. Naming the real weakest assumption, rather than a safe-sounding one, is what makes it credible.

**How to use this table in preparation**
Rehearse the full answer, then score only the fourth column. Each entry is a yes-or-no observable rather than an impression, which makes it usable on yourself.

## Interview pushback and validation

Eight questions with a baseline and a stretch answer each, then twelve validation scenarios.

---

> - Q1: Where are the private keys?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - CA signing keys remain in the HSM.
>     - CSR leaf keys remain with their requester.
>     - Managed exportable leaf keys need a separate encrypted storage and authenticated delivery boundary.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Documented OCI behavior requires an HSM-protected Vault key for a CA and rejects software-protected keys
>     - Verify the CSR self-signature before policy so denials do not leak names.

**Why this question is asked first**
It is the fastest way to find out whether a candidate understands PKI or has only used it. There is exactly one right shape of answer, and it has three parts because there are three kinds of key.

**The baseline answer**

- **CA signing keys: in the HSM, always.** Not encrypted in a database, not in a secrets manager, not in memory.
- **CSR leaf keys: with the requester.** The consumer generated the key; it never travels.
- **Managed exportable leaf keys: a separate problem with its own boundary.** The honest third case, and refusing to hand-wave it is part of the answer.
- Worked example of the weak version of this answer: "the keys are stored securely." That is a non-answer. The strong version names a different custodian for each of the three key types.

**What the stretch adds**

1. **The documented platform constraint.** OCI requires an HSM-protected Vault key for a CA and rejects software-protected keys. Citing this turns a design preference into a platform requirement.
2. **The ordering rule.** Verify the CSR self-signature before evaluating policy, so a denial never reveals which names exist. Adding a second, unrelated insight to a question about key storage is what makes it a stretch answer rather than a longer one.

---

> - Q2: The root CA is compromised.
>   - Now what?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Root compromise requires a new independently trusted anchor and out-of-band trust-store distribution.
>     - Cross-signing by the compromised root cannot restore authenticity.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Alarm on any root `Sign` outside a ceremony window
>     - The failure is always a verifier you did not know existed, so build the trust-store inventory before the rotation, not during.

**Why this question separates people**
The instinct is to reach for the tools that solve every other PKI problem — revoke it, cross-sign a replacement, rotate. All three are wrong here, and knowing *why* is the answer.

**The baseline answer**

- **A new independently trusted anchor, distributed out of band.** "Out of band" means by means not authenticated by the compromised root — a configuration-management push, a new base image, a manual install.
- **"Cross-signing by the compromised root cannot restore authenticity."**
  - Worked example of the trap: the natural plan is to have the old root cross-sign the new one, so existing clients transition smoothly. But an attacker holding the old root's key can produce that exact signature for a root of *their* choosing. A signature made by a compromised key proves nothing to anyone who knows it is compromised, and clients who do not know are not safe either way.
  - The deeper point: authenticity flows downward from a trusted anchor. Once the anchor is untrusted, nothing it says can re-establish trust — including a statement about its own successor.

**What the stretch adds**

1. **"Alarm on any root `Sign` outside a ceremony window."**
   - The root signs perhaps twice a year, during a scheduled ceremony. Every other signature is, by definition, unexpected.
   - Worked example of why this is such a good alarm: a normal signing service is too busy for anomaly detection to work well. The root is the opposite — its expected rate is essentially zero, so *any* activity outside a known window is a high-confidence signal with no false-positive problem.
2. **"The failure is always a verifier you did not know existed, so build the trust-store inventory before the rotation, not during."**
   - The operational insight, and the hardest-won one.
   - Worked example: the rotation plan covers every service, every load balancer, and every container image. Then a partner's integration fails, or a hardware appliance nobody has logged into for three years, or a client library with a certificate bundle compiled in. These are found during the incident because nobody had a list.
   - "Before the rotation, not during" is the instruction: the inventory of everything that trusts this root is the *first* deliverable of a root rotation, not a thing discovered while failing.

---

> - Q3 (premise reversal): Why not use Let's Encrypt for everything and skip this service?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Public trust for public endpoints, yes
>     - Internal mTLS wants a private CA with no external rate limits, internal names, custom policy, and no dependency on an external issuer for service identity.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - The honest build case on OCI is policy and inventory on top of the managed Certificates service
>     - The signer and versioning should be bought, and saying so is the IC4 signal.

**What a premise reversal is**
The interviewer attacks the existence of the system rather than its design. The wrong response is to defend everything you have built; the right one is to concede what is genuinely true and then state the specific reasons the remainder still needs to exist.

**The baseline answer**

- **Concede immediately:** for public endpoints, yes, use a public CA. That is what it is for.
- **Then name four specific reasons internal mTLS needs a private CA:**
  1. **No external rate limits.** Worked example: public CAs impose issuance limits per domain per week. A fleet re-imaging thousands of hosts would exceed them, and the failure arrives as a refusal from an organization you cannot call.
  2. **Internal names.** A public CA will not issue for `payments.acme.internal`, because it cannot validate control of a name that does not exist in public DNS.
  3. **Custom policy.** Key usages, lifetimes, and extensions specific to internal use, which a public CA does not offer.
  4. **No external dependency for service identity.** Worked example of the risk: internal service-to-service authentication that depends on an external issuer means that issuer's outage or policy change becomes an internal incident. Making your own identity infrastructure depend on a third party is a strange thing to choose.

**What the stretch adds, and why it is counterintuitive**

- The stretch answer **concedes more**: buy the signer and the versioning from the platform's managed Certificates service, and build only policy and inventory on top.
- "Saying so is the IC4 signal" is the source telling you plainly that arguing for less building scores higher.
- Worked example of why: the interviewing team builds the managed service. A candidate who designs a bespoke CA without noticing that their employer sells one reads as unaware of the landscape. A candidate who says "the signing and version machinery is a solved product — what my team would actually own is tenant policy, issuance inventory, and adoption telemetry" has correctly identified where the remaining work is.
- The general lesson: seniority shows in knowing what *not* to build, and the design exercise is valuable as an explanation of what the bought component must do internally.

---

> - Q4: Now give every pod its own certificate.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Short-TTL (`24 h`) certificates with no revocation reliance and SPIFFE-style identity
>     - Issuance rises `100×`, so it becomes a scale problem for the HSM pool.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Delegated name-constrained sub-CAs per node move the HSM off the hot path
>     - The HSM signs one sub-CA per node per day.

**What this question is testing**
Whether you notice that the *character* of the problem changes. At 0.2 issuances per second the hard part is correctness. At 100× it is throughput, and the correctness machinery is already built.

- **The baseline** identifies the shift and names the bottleneck: the HSM pool.
- **The stretch** changes the arithmetic instead of buying more hardware: one name-constrained sub-CA per node per day, with each node issuing its own pods' certificates locally.
  - Worked example, repeated because it is the payoff: 1,000 nodes at 100 pods each is 100,000 certificates daily. Delegation reduces HSM operations to 1,000 — a factor of 100 — and the sub-CA's own 24-hour lifetime plus Name Constraints keep the delegation safe.
- "Move the HSM off the hot path" is the phrase to say. It is the same move as moving bytes off the API fleet in an upload design: identify the resource that cannot scale horizontally and arrange for it not to be on the per-request path.

---

> - Q5: How do consumers hot-reload without a restart?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Watch a file or event, rebuild the `SSLContext`, swap an `AtomicReference` behind a delegating `KeyManager`
>     - Envoy SDS or Nginx reload
>     - Existing sessions finish on the old certificate.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - The consumer reports the serving serial back as telemetry, which is what makes the adoption check possible.

**What this question is testing**
Whether the design's central safety property is connected to a real mechanism, or whether "consumers hot-reload" was an assumption nobody examined.

- **The baseline** is concrete: a specific Java mechanism and two production systems that do it.
- **The stretch** makes the connection the rest of the design depends on: the same consumer-side integration that reloads certificates is the one that reports which serial it is serving.
  - Worked example of why that matters for planning: hot-reload and adoption telemetry are usually treated as two separate work items, and telemetry is the one that gets cut. Recognizing that they live in the same component means they ship together — and since the deprecation safety argument rests entirely on the telemetry, shipping reload without it would leave the design's weakest assumption completely unmitigated.

---

> - Q6: How do you guarantee serial uniqueness across a CA key rotation?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - This design chooses 128 random serial bits with issuer-scoped uniqueness.
>     - Sequential serials are not universally prohibited by X.509.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - All signing versions of one logical CA share one serial namespace
>     - Enforce the positive, at-most-20-octet rule in the issuer because SQL precision alone does not.

**Why this is a sharp question**
It combines two things people know separately — serials must be unique, and CA keys rotate — and asks what happens at their intersection. The answer requires knowing that the namespace is defined by the issuer *name*, not by the key.

- **The baseline** gives the design choice (128 random bits, issuer-scoped uniqueness) and then the careful correction: sequential serials are not universally prohibited. Getting the second half right shows you know where the rule comes from.
- **The stretch** answers the actual question directly: all signing versions of one logical CA share one serial namespace.
  - Worked example of the failure it prevents: CA version 5 begins allocating serials independently of version 4. A serial issued under version 4 last month is reissued under version 5 today. Both certificates carry the same issuer name and the same serial, so they are the same certificate as far as X.509 identification and CRLs are concerned. Revoking one revokes both.
  - Rotation changes the key; it does not create a new issuer.
- The second stretch point is the enforcement detail: `numeric(49)` bounds the magnitude but permits a negative value or a 21-octet encoding, so the rule lives in the issuer code.

---

> - Q7: A database transaction cannot roll back an HSM signature.
>   - Where is your consistency boundary?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Reserve the intent (serial, validity, TBS hash) in one transaction, sign, then flip in a second transaction
>     - A crash between leaves a `PENDING` row whose serial is known and revocable.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Re-signing the same TBS yields the same serial, so retries never mint an untracked certificate
>     - The accepted residual is one revocable serial bounded by TTL, and you say that bound out loud.

**Why this is the hardest question in the set**
It is stated as a fact rather than a question, and then asks where your boundary is. There is no answer that makes the problem go away, so the only good answer is one that bounds it precisely.

**The baseline: the two-transaction structure**

- Reserve the intent, sign, flip. The key sentence is the second: **a crash between leaves a `PENDING` row whose serial is known and revocable.**
- Worked example of why "known" is the entire property: the bad outcome is not a failed issuance — those are easy. The bad outcome is a valid certificate the system has no record of, which can never be revoked because revocation identifies certificates by serial. Reserving the serial before signing means every signature that can possibly exist corresponds to a row you can find.

**The stretch: naming the residual and its bound**

- "Re-signing the same TBS yields the same serial, so retries never mint an untracked certificate." Retries are safe because the intent is immutable.
- **"The accepted residual is one revocable serial bounded by TTL, and you say that bound out loud."**
  - The residual: in the worst case, one signed certificate exists that was never published. It is attributable to a known serial, so it can be revoked. And even without revocation, it expires when its TTL runs out.
  - Worked example of the full bound: a 90-day certificate that is orphaned is at most 90 days of exposure, immediately reducible by revoking its serial. With 24-hour certificates the bound is a day.
  - "Say that bound out loud" is the interview instruction. A candidate who says "the two-transaction design handles it" has not answered. A candidate who says "the residual is one orphaned, tracked, revocable serial, bounded by the certificate TTL" has quantified exactly what cannot be eliminated — which is what the question asked for.
- The transferable idea: **when two systems cannot be made atomic, the answer is never a cleverer protocol. It is to make every intermediate state identifiable, and then state the worst case in one sentence.**

---

> - Q8: Disabling a CA races an issuance in flight.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Recheck the CA state at the signing boundary under the row lock
>     - Issuance after `DISABLED` fails the work request, and disabling issuance is not revoking existing certificates.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Document the cutoff explicitly (work requests accepted before the disable complete or fail, never half-sign), and avoid making the CA row a global hot row by reserving serials per certificate.

**What the race actually is**
An operator disables a CA — perhaps suspecting compromise — while an issuance is already in progress. The question is what happens to the in-flight request.

**The baseline: two statements, and the second matters more**

1. **Recheck the CA state at the signing boundary, under the row lock.** The state is checked when it can still change the outcome, not only at request admission.
   - Worked example: the CA is `ACTIVE` when the request is accepted at 10:00:00. It is disabled at 10:00:02. The worker begins signing at 10:00:03 and re-reads the state under the lock, sees `DISABLED`, and fails the work request instead of signing.
2. **"Disabling issuance is not revoking existing certificates."** The distinction that must be stated, because conflating the two is a serious operational error.
   - Worked example of the consequence: an operator disables a CA expecting to contain a suspected compromise. Every certificate that CA already issued keeps working perfectly — it is signed, valid, and unaffected. If the intent was to stop existing certificates, disabling did nothing at all, and the operator believes they have acted.
   - Disable stops *new* issuance. Revoke invalidates *existing* certificates. They are different buttons with different effects and different blast radii.

**What the stretch adds**

1. **"Document the cutoff explicitly: work requests accepted before the disable complete or fail, never half-sign."**
   - The point is that the cutoff must be a *stated policy*, not whatever the code happens to do.
   - Worked example of why: during an incident someone will ask "did anything get issued after we disabled it?" A documented cutoff answers that immediately. An undocumented one means reading the code and the logs under pressure.
   - "Never half-sign" is the invariant: there is no outcome where a certificate is partially issued. Either the work request completes fully, or it fails. Every in-flight request resolves to one of those two.
2. **"Avoid making the CA row a global hot row by reserving serials per certificate."**
   - The performance note attached to a correctness question, which is why it is a stretch item. The CA state check happens under the CA row lock, so if every issuance also incremented a counter on that row, the CA row would serialize all issuance under that CA.
   - Worked example: a tenant with one CA and 500 issuances per second would find every one of them queued behind a single row's lock. Reserving serials per certificate row moves the contention to rows that are naturally spread out, while the `(tenant_id, ca_id, serial)` constraint still enforces uniqueness.

> - Validation scenarios
>   - Renewal at day `60` with the old serial still serving on day `67`
>   - Deprecation must wait.
>   - Isolate consumers for 24 h with > 24 h remaining chain and required status validity.
>   - Verify declared failure behavior separately with near-expiry certificates and stale status.
>   - Consumer restarted with an empty disk cache during the outage.
>   - Worker crash between HSM sign and version flip
>   - Reconciler outcome.
>   - Double-run of the rotation scheduler
>   - No duplicate versions.
>   - Revoke followed by CRL builder failure
>   - CRL age alarm fires before `nextUpdate`.
>   - Tenant A token on tenant B certificate id returns `404`.
>   - Subordinate CA rotation with cross-sign
>   - Old-trust-store verifier still validates.
>   - HSM throttling during a fleet re-image
>   - Renewals back off without duplicating work.

**What this list is**
Twelve tests, each pairing a scenario with the specific outcome that must be observed. Every one maps to a claim made earlier in the file, which is the point: each design claim has a test that would falsify it.

1. **Renewal at day 60, old serial still serving on day 67 → deprecation must wait.**
   - Tests the adoption check against the calendar. Worked example of the failure it catches: an implementation that deprecates purely on the 7-day timer passes every other test and fails this one, which is exactly the classic rotation outage in a test harness.
2. **Isolate consumers for 24 hours with more than 24 hours of remaining chain and status validity.**
   - Tests static stability. The precondition — more than 24 hours remaining — is what makes the result interpretable.
3. **Verify declared failure behavior separately, with near-expiry certificates and stale status.**
   - The deliberately-fail test. Confirms the behavior at expiry is the *declared* one rather than whatever emerges.
4. **Consumer restarted with an empty disk cache during the outage.**
   - Tests the cache-on-boot trap directly. Worked example of what a passing implementation does: the consumer finds a last-known-good chain in its deploy artifact and serves TLS without reaching the control plane. A failing one comes up with no certificate.
5. **Worker crash between HSM sign and version flip → reconciler outcome.**
   - Tests the hardest path in the design. The assertion is not "no crash happens" — it is that the reconciler reaches a defined outcome: either the same TBS is re-signed under the same serial, or the version is marked `FAILED` and the serial is placed on the CRL.
6. **Double-run of the rotation scheduler → no duplicate versions.**
   - Tests the unique renewal-intent constraint. Worked example of how to run it: start two scheduler instances against the same database and confirm one intent per certificate and base version.
7. **Revoke followed by CRL builder failure → CRL age alarm fires before `nextUpdate`.**
   - Tests that the alarm has enough lead time to be useful. The alarm must fire *before* the published CRL expires, not when it already has.
8. **Tenant A token on tenant B certificate id returns `404`.**
   - The isolation test, as a literal assertion. It is a single line in a test suite and it catches the existence leak permanently.
9. **Subordinate CA rotation with cross-sign → old-trust-store verifier still validates.**
   - Tests the cross-signing claim against a real verifier holding only the old anchor. Worked example of why this must be tested rather than assumed: the rotation section already warned that cross-signing depends on the client's path builder finding the alternative path. This test is how you find out whether *your* clients do.
10. **HSM throttling during a fleet re-image → renewals back off without duplicating work.**
    - Tests retry discipline under the realistic burst. Two assertions in one: backoff happens, and the backed-off retries do not create duplicate issuances.

**The shape worth copying**
Every scenario names an observable outcome, not a vague "it should work". That is what makes the list a test plan rather than a wish list, and it is what lets you answer "how would you know?" for any claim in the design.

## Recall card

Five one-liners, five numbers, three failures, three defeaters, a readiness test, and a four-sentence closing script.

---

> - “Control-plane correctness with a data-plane survival constraint: low QPS, every mistake is an outage-by-timer or a security incident.”

**What this says in plain words**
The opening line, and it characterizes the whole problem in one sentence.

**Why it matters**
It names the two things that make this question unlike most system-design questions.

- **"Low QPS."** The problem is not throughput. Worked example: 0.2 issuances per second is a load any single database handles without effort, so any answer that spends its time on sharding and caching has misread the question.
- **"Outage-by-timer."** The characteristic failure is not a crash — it is a deadline. Worked example: a scheduler that silently stops produces no errors for 30 days and then expires 11,000 certificates a day. Nothing failed; time simply passed.
- **"Or a security incident."** The other failure class: a leaked key, a cross-tenant certificate, an unrevocable orphan. These are not outages at all, and they do not show up on latency dashboards.
- Saying this sentence early tells the interviewer you know which axis the difficulty is on.

---

> - “Two valid versions overlap; consumers switch; deprecate only after the adoption check.”

**What this says in plain words**
Rotation in three clauses, in order.

**Why it matters**
The third clause is the one most answers omit.

- Worked example of the first two alone: overlap for 7 days, let consumers switch, then deprecate on day 8. That is a calendar, not a check, and the 40 hosts whose refresh agent has been broken for a month all break on day 8.
- "Only after the adoption check" is what turns a hopeful schedule into a verified one. If you say one sentence about rotation, say this one.

---

> - “Issuance is an HSM signing operation against a key handle, never a key export.”

**What this says in plain words**
The service asks the hardware to sign; it never obtains the key.

**Why it matters**
"never a key export" is the phrase that does the work.

- Worked example of the property: an attacker with full control of every host can request signatures while they hold that access, and every request is logged against the handle. They cannot leave with the key.
- The contrast that makes it concrete: a key encrypted in a database is in process memory 20,000 times a day, and a single heap dump gives an attacker permanent, silent forgery capability with no log entry.

---

> - “Handshakes never call the control plane; cached bundles are the static stability.”

**What this says in plain words**
The data plane does not depend on the control plane, and the mechanism is a local cache.

**Why it matters**
It is the sentence that makes a control-plane outage survivable.

- Worked example: a four-hour control-plane outage. No new certificates are issued. Every TLS handshake in the fleet succeeds throughout.
- The corollary to have ready, because it is the standard follow-up: the cache must be on *disk*, or shipped in the deploy artifact, because a consumer that restarts mid-outage with only an in-memory cache cannot reach a serving state.

---

> - “Revocation enforcement depends on the relying party and the lifetime of cached evidence.”

**What this says in plain words**
Whether revocation works is decided by the verifier, not by you.

**Why it matters**
It is the honest framing, and it is what makes the short-TTL argument land.

- Worked example: a certificate revoked at 09:00 with a 6-hour verifier cache is still accepted until 15:00. A soft-fail verifier that cannot reach the CRL accepts it indefinitely, and an attacker who can block CRL access arranges exactly that.
- Which leads to the conclusion this sentence exists to support: expiry is the control that cannot be blocked, cached around, or soft-failed. Revocation is the backstop.

---

> - 5 numbers: `90 d` leaf TTL renewed at day `60`
>   - Overlap ≥ `7 d`
>   - CRL `nextUpdate` ≤ `24 h`
>   - Issue p99 `2 s`
>   - Page at `14 d` to expiry.

**What these are**
The five figures to have without thinking. Each one is a decision, not a measurement.

1. **90-day lifetime, renewed at day 60.** The two-thirds rule, leaving a 30-day incident-response budget.
2. **Overlap at least 7 days.** How long both versions stay valid — the window in which a non-adopting consumer can be found and fixed.
3. **CRL `nextUpdate` at most 24 hours.** The published staleness bound, which also forces the daily rebuild even when nothing is revoked.
4. **Issue p99 of 2 seconds.** The latency target for an asynchronous operation, dominated by HSM time and poll interval rather than by any computation.
5. **Page at 14 days to expiry.** The outcome alarm. Worked example of why 14 rather than 3: 14 days leaves two full working weeks to diagnose a stuck renewal, which covers a holiday period and a person being on leave.

**Why five numbers rather than a table**
Because they are the ones an interviewer asks for directly, and hesitating on them undercuts everything else. Note that four of the five are *durations*, which is the shape of this problem: almost every quantity that matters is a deadline.

---

> - Failures to rehearse
>   - Consumer non-adoption.
>   - Control-plane outage near expiry.
>   - Root compromise requiring an independent trust path.

**What these are**
Three failures chosen because they cover three different kinds of difficulty.

1. **Consumer non-adoption.** The operational failure. Caused by the design's own choice to give up control for availability, and answered by the adoption check.
2. **Control-plane outage near expiry.** The compound failure. Either alone is survivable; together they are an emergency, and the nearest `not_after` is the clock.
   - Worked example of the compounding: an outage with 26 days to the nearest expiry is a serious incident with no deadline. The same outage with 4 hours to the nearest expiry is a different incident entirely, and the response is to get an emergency short-TTL certificate issued by any means available.
3. **Root compromise requiring an independent trust path.** The catastrophic failure, and the one whose recovery cannot be automated — because automation would have to be authenticated by the thing that was compromised.

---

> - 3 defeaters: encrypted-DB key custody
>   - Atomic-swap rotation
>   - Revocation as the primary control.

**What these are**
The three objections to volunteer, in the order they are most likely to come up.

1. **Encrypted-DB key custody.** Encryption at rest is not custody; the key is in memory on every signature.
2. **Atomic-swap rotation.** Consumers cache, so there is no instant at which everyone switches. Change is a window.
3. **Revocation as the primary control.** Enforcement belongs to the verifier; short TTL is the control you actually own.

**Why volunteering them scores**
Each one is the *natural* first design a competent engineer would reach for. Naming them and explaining why they fail shows you considered and rejected them, which is a different thing from never having thought of them.

---

> - Done when: rotation state machine drawn from memory
>   - Static-stability answer in `30 s`
>   - Schema written cold.

**What this says in plain words**
Three readiness tests, and each one is pass-or-fail rather than a feeling.

1. **The rotation state machine drawn from memory.** Six states and every transition, including the two conditions on deprecation.
2. **The static-stability answer in 30 seconds.** Worked example of a passing answer: "Consumers cache the full chain and key on disk and hot-reload. Handshakes never call the control plane, so a control-plane outage stops new issuance and nothing else. The trap is caching on boot — a consumer that restarts mid-outage with no local copy cannot serve, so last-known-good ships in the deploy artifact."
3. **The schema written cold.** Tables, composite keys, the intent-and-result split, and the two indexes, with no prompting.

---

> - Wrap-up script
>   - “The design is an async control plane over HSM-held keys, versioned certificates with an overlap window, a CRL published to object storage, and consumers that cache bundles so the data plane survives a control-plane outage.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence one: the whole architecture in four clauses.

**Why it matters**
Each clause names a component *and* the property it provides, and the last clause is the one that ties them together.

- "Async control plane over HSM-held keys" — the write path and its custody model.
- "Versioned certificates with an overlap window" — the rotation model.
- "A CRL published to object storage" — revocation, with its distribution implied.
- "Consumers that cache bundles so the data plane survives a control-plane outage" — the availability property, stated as a consequence rather than a feature.

---

> -   - “The key invariant is that a version is never deprecated while its serial still serves traffic, and every issuance reserves a known serial before the HSM signs.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence two: two invariants, one about rotation and one about issuance.

**Why it matters**
They are the two properties everything else exists to protect.

- **"Never deprecated while its serial still serves traffic."** The rotation invariant. Worked example of violating it: deprecating on the calendar, and 40 hosts break.
- **"Every issuance reserves a known serial before the HSM signs."** The issuance invariant. Worked example of violating it: a crash after signing produces a valid certificate with a serial nobody recorded, which can never be revoked.
- Both are stated as invariants rather than as mechanisms, which is the right level: the fence, the lease, and the adoption telemetry are all implementations of these two sentences.

---

> -   - “The main tradeoff is async issuance with overlap: every client needs a poll loop and we retain two valid versions, in exchange for zero-downtime rotation and HSM latency off the request path.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence three: exactly what was paid and exactly what was bought.

- **Paid:** every client writes a poll loop, and two valid certificates exist at once for at least a week.
  - The second cost is worth stating plainly: two valid certificates means two things that could be compromised and two things to keep track of. It is a real security cost, not just bookkeeping.
- **Bought:** rotation with no downtime, and HSM latency off the request path.

**Why it matters**
Naming the cost in concrete terms — a poll loop, two valid versions — rather than as "some added complexity" is what makes the tradeoff sound examined.

---

> -   - “My weakest assumption is consumer adoption telemetry; without it the deprecation step is a guess, so I would validate that first and extend the overlap when data is missing.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence four: the flaw, its consequence, and two specific responses.

**Why it matters**
It names the genuine weakest link rather than a safe-sounding one.

- Worked example of the failure it admits: 200 of 3,000 consumers run an old agent that does not report serials. The dashboard shows 100% adoption among reporters. Deprecation proceeds and 200 hosts break — and they were invisible *because* they were old, which is the same reason they had not refreshed.
- The two responses are both concrete: validate the telemetry coverage first, and treat missing data as "not adopted" so the overlap extends rather than the deprecation proceeding.
- Ending on the fail-safe default is a good last impression: it says that when the design's weakest signal is absent, the system waits rather than guesses.

- That is exactly the caveat in the rotation section — "only when client path builders and certificate constraints accept that path" — demonstrated on the largest possible sample.
- And it is exactly the pushback Q2 insight: the failures were verifiers nobody had inventoried, discovered during the transition rather than before it.
- Worked example of how to use this in an interview: when asked about CA rotation, citing this event by name shows the answer comes from a real case with a known outcome rather than from a diagram. It is also a reminder that the organization involved is one of the most competent PKI operators in the world, planned the transition for years, and still broke a large number of clients.
