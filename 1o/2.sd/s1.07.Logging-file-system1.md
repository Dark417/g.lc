## Interview framing

The level, the evidence, why the problem is hard, and the sentences to open with.

---

> - Target: OCI IC3 ownership of a bounded platform component.
>   - `IC3 baseline` is what must land in every phase.
>   - `IC4 stretch` adds architecture changes under stronger constraints.
>   - These are preparation labels, not Oracle's official rubric.

**What this says in plain words**
You answer as an engineer who owns one clearly-bordered piece of infrastructure, including its failures and its pager. `IC3 baseline` is the floor; `IC4 stretch` is depth added when pushed. Both labels are a study device, not a company grading scale.

**Why it matters**

1. **Bounded controls scope.** Inside the box: accepting records, writing them to local files durably, serving time-range reads, and shipping sealed files onward. Outside: the central search index, alerting, and what applications choose to log.
2. **The baseline is a checklist.** Skip the acknowledgment modes and you dropped the item the whole design is organized around.
3. **The labels are yours.** Saying "this is the IC4 answer" claims knowledge of their rubric you do not have.

---

> - Evidence: retain the first-hand report attribution in [the bank](2.sd.md#1infra).
>   - The architecture below is a proposed interview answer.
>   - It does not claim to reproduce Oracle's implementation or an interviewer's accepted answer.

**What this says in plain words**
Someone reported being asked this in a real Oracle interview. The design here is this preparation's own answer, not a leaked design.

**Why it matters**
High confidence the question gets asked, no claim that this architecture is what the interviewer wanted.

---

> - Why this is hard
>   - Naive: every producer appends to one file under a lock and calls `flush()` after each line.

**What this says in plain words**
The obvious design: one shared file, a lock so concurrent threads do not interleave their bytes, and a flush after every line so nothing is lost.

**Why it matters**
Every part of it is reasonable in isolation, and together they cannot work at the stated rate. That is what makes it the right naive design to name.

- Worked example of it being fine: an application writing 20 lines per second. The lock is uncontended, 20 flushes per second is trivial, and nothing more is needed.

---

> -   - First break: at `10,000 records/s` the lock and per-record flush serialize producers, and `flush()` still does not mean the bytes reached disk.

**What this says in plain words**
Three failures in one line.

1. **The lock serializes producers.**
   - Worked example: every logging call from every thread must acquire one lock. At 10,000 records per second, with each holding the lock for the duration of a write, the lock becomes the application's bottleneck. Worse, the wait is inside the application's own request handling, so logging slows down the product.
2. **Per-record flush is not affordable.**
   - Worked example of the gap: 10,000 records per second means 10,000 synchronous flushes per second. A typical disk sustains perhaps a few hundred, because each one waits for the device to confirm. You are asking for an order of magnitude more than the hardware provides, continuously.
3. **`flush()` does not mean the bytes reached disk.**
   - The subtlest and most important of the three. A language-level flush pushes bytes from the program's buffer into the operating system. The operating system holds them in its own page cache and writes them out later.
   - Worked example: the process calls `flush()`, gets no error, and reports success. The machine loses power one second later. The bytes were in the operating system's memory, never on the device, and they are gone. The program did everything it was told and the guarantee was never real.
   - This distinction is the heart of the whole design, and the filesystem-boundary section returns to it in detail.

---

> -   - Second break: a crash leaves a torn frame, a query reads a half-written record, and retention deletes a file a reader is still scanning.

**What this says in plain words**
Three more failures, in the reading and cleanup paths.

1. **A crash leaves a torn frame.**
   - A frame is one record's bytes on disk, including its length and checksum. Torn means the write stopped partway.
   - Worked example: a 200-byte record is being written when the process dies after 80 bytes. The file now ends mid-record. A reader that trusts the length prefix will read 200 bytes and get 80 bytes of record plus 120 bytes of whatever was there before, or run off the end of the file.
2. **A query reads a half-written record.**
   - Worked example: the writer has written 150 of 200 bytes when a reader scans the file. Without a rule saying how far a reader may go, the reader parses a record that does not exist yet.
3. **Retention deletes a file a reader is still scanning.**
   - Worked example: a query is 40% of the way through a segment when the retention worker deletes it as too old. The query fails mid-way, or worse, silently returns partial results as though that were the complete answer.

---

> -   - Minimum primitives
> -     - A byte-bounded queue
> -     - One writer per active segment
> -     - Checksummed frames
> -     - A published durable prefix
> -     - A manifest that readers pin.

**What this says in plain words**
Five parts you cannot drop.

1. **A byte-bounded queue.** Bounded in *bytes*, not record count, so its memory use is actually limited.
2. **One writer per active segment.** A single thread owns the file, which removes the lock contention by removing the sharing.
3. **Checksummed frames.** Each record carries a checksum, so a torn or corrupt record is detectable rather than silently parsed.
4. **A published durable prefix.** A single number saying "everything up to here is genuinely on disk". Readers and acknowledgments both use it.
5. **A manifest that readers pin.** A list of which segments exist, which a reader can hold so retention cannot delete beneath it.

**Why it matters**
Each primitive answers one of the five failures above, in order. Losing your place in the interview, you can walk the five and reconstruct the design.

---

> - 🗣 “Acceptance, local durability, and remote durability are three different promises; the design is the state machine that upgrades a record from one to the next.”

**What this says in plain words**
A quotable one-liner; the 🗣 marker means say it roughly verbatim. Two claims:

1. There are three distinct promises, not one thing called "written".
2. The design's content is the machinery that moves a record from one promise to the next.

**Why it matters**
It reframes the question within ten seconds, and it is the reframing that makes everything else tractable.

- Worked example of the three promises for one record: at 14:00:00.000 it is accepted into memory, and a crash now loses it. At 14:00:00.050 it is flushed to local disk, and a process crash no longer loses it but a disk failure does. At 14:02:00 it is uploaded to object storage, and now the host can burn down.
- Most candidates treat "durable" as one property and then cannot answer what a given failure costs. Naming three promises turns an unanswerable question into three answerable ones.

---

> - Opening script
>   - “I will treat this as a host-local segmented log with a central upload path, not a search engine.”

**What this says in plain words**
The first sentence, and it is largely a scoping move: a local append-only log that ships files onward, explicitly not a search system.

**Why it matters**
"not a search engine" prevents a very expensive detour.

- Worked example of the detour: a candidate who hears "logging service" starts designing inverted indexes, tokenization, and query planning. That is a different and much larger system, and 45 minutes of it answers a question nobody asked.
- The honest framing: this component makes bytes durable and shippable. Something else makes them searchable.

---

> -   - “First I define the acknowledgment modes, because ‘written’ means something different for a debug line and an audit record.”

**What this says in plain words**
The first design step is defining what acceptance means, with the justification attached.

**Why it matters**
Worked example of the difference: a debug line about cache statistics is worth nothing if the process crashes before flushing, and blocking the application for 100 milliseconds to guarantee it would be absurd. An audit record of a financial transaction has the opposite profile entirely. One mechanism cannot serve both, and pretending it can produces a system that is either too slow or too lossy.

---

> -   - “Then I size the queue, segment, and flush batch from the record rate and the disk budget.”

**What this says in plain words**
Three sizes derived from two inputs: how fast records arrive and how much disk exists.

---

> -   - “The architecture is producers into a bounded queue, one writer per active segment, and a manifest that readers and the uploader consume.”

**What this says in plain words**
The whole architecture in one sentence: many producers, one queue, one writer, and a manifest that two different consumers read.

---

> -   - “The deep dive is group commit, rotation, and what crash recovery can and cannot trust.”

**What this says in plain words**
Three deep-dive topics, and the third is phrased unusually: what recovery *cannot* trust.

**Why it matters**
Worked example of the distinction: after a crash, recovery can trust a frame whose checksum validates. It cannot trust that a valid frame was acknowledged to anyone, because the crash may have happened between the disk write and the acknowledgment. Knowing the difference is what the deep dive is for.

---

> -   - “I will close with retention versus readers, failure modes, and where I would change the design for an audit contract.”

**What this says in plain words**
Announces the ending: the conflict between deleting old data and serving live readers, the failure table, and how an audit requirement changes everything.

**Why it matters**
"retention versus readers" is named as a conflict rather than a feature, which is accurate: the retention worker wants to delete and the reader wants the file to stay, and the design has to arbitrate.

## Requirements — IC3 baseline

The numbers warning, the clarifying table, functional scope, the acknowledgment modes, the numeric target table, invariants, and non-goals.

---

> - Numerical targets are interview assumptions unless an adjacent primary source states otherwise.

**What this says in plain words**
Every number here was chosen to make the design concrete. None came from Oracle unless a link says so.

**Why it matters**
Worked example: at 10,000 records per second, group commit is mandatory. At 100 records per second, flushing each one is fine and the entire deep dive is unnecessary.

---

> | Question | Assumed answer | Why it changes the design |
> |---|---|---|
> | Library, host daemon, or central service? | Host daemon that owns local files and uploads sealed segments | One writer per host; local disk is the first durability boundary |
> | What does “written” mean to the caller? | Three modes: best-effort, local-durable, replicated | Decides what the API returns and what a loss window can claim |
> | Query shape? | Time-range scan by ingest time; full-text search is a separate derived index | A sparse offset index suffices; no inverted index on the hot path |
> | Ordering key? | Writer-assigned ingest sequence; event time is a carried field | Late event time cannot break append order |
> | Retention? | `24 h` local within `200 GB`; longer retention lives centrally | Disk budget forces the upload and deletion policy |
> | Multi-tenant streams? | Yes; streams are tenant-scoped with per-stream quotas | Queue bytes, disk share, and authorization are per stream |
> | Record size bound? | `200 B` typical, `64 KB` maximum | Fixes frame layout and the memory bound per batch |

**What this says in plain words**
Seven questions, the assumed answer, and what each decides.

- **Row: Library, daemon, or central service?**
  - Three genuinely different shapes.
    - A library runs inside each application process, so every process is a writer and they contend for the same files.
    - A daemon is one process per host that owns the files, and applications send records to it.
    - A central service means every record crosses the network on the hot path.
  - The daemon is chosen, and the consequence is stated: one writer per host, and local disk is the first durability boundary.
  - Worked example of why the library fails here: 40 application processes on a host, each holding the file open and appending. Coordinating their offsets, their rotation, and their recovery requires exactly the locking the naive design failed on, multiplied across process boundaries.
- **Row: What does "written" mean?**
  - Three modes, which the next block defines fully.
  - "what a loss window can claim" is the sharp part: the mode determines what you are allowed to promise about how much data a given failure destroys.
- **Row: Query shape?**
  - Time-range scans, with full-text search explicitly elsewhere.
  - Worked example of the difference in cost: a time-range scan needs only a sparse index saying roughly where each time lands in each file. Full-text search needs an inverted index mapping every term to every record containing it, which can exceed the size of the data itself and must be maintained on write.
- **Row: Ordering key?**
  - A writer-assigned sequence number defines order. Event time is carried as data, not used for ordering.
  - Worked example of why: two records arrive with event times 14:00:05 and 14:00:03, in that order, because the second application was slow to report. Ordering by event time would require inserting the second record before the first, which an append-only file cannot do. The ingest sequence orders them as they arrived, and the event times are preserved as fields.
- **Row: Retention?**
  - 24 hours locally within 200 gigabytes, with longer retention central.
  - The two numbers together are the constraint, and the capacity section shows they conflict at higher rates.
- **Row: Multi-tenant streams?**
  - Streams are tenant-scoped with per-stream quotas on queue bytes, disk share, and authorization.
- **Row: Record size bound?**
  - 200 bytes typical, 64 kilobytes maximum.
  - Worked example of why the maximum matters: without it, a single record could be 500 megabytes, which must be held in memory to be framed and checksummed. The maximum bounds the memory any one record can demand.

---

> - Functional scope
>   - Accept structured records into a named stream with a chosen acknowledgment mode.
>   - Append records to immutable, checksummed, size-bounded local segments.
>   - Seal and publish segments to a manifest that readers and the uploader consume.
>   - Serve bounded time-range queries over local segments.
>   - Upload sealed segments to object storage and reclaim local disk under retention.

**What this says in plain words**
Five capabilities, and the second carries three adjectives that are each a decision.

1. **Accept records into a named stream with a chosen mode.** The caller picks the promise.
2. **Immutable, checksummed, size-bounded segments.**
   - *Immutable*: once written, bytes never change. Worked example of the benefit: a reader needs no locking against a writer for any sealed segment, because nothing can change underneath it.
   - *Checksummed*: corruption and torn writes are detectable.
   - *Size-bounded*: bounds recovery scan time and gives the uploader a sensible unit.
3. **Seal and publish to a manifest consumed by readers and the uploader.** One publication serving two consumers.
4. **Serve bounded time-range queries.** *Bounded* means a page limit, so no query can ask for everything.
5. **Upload and reclaim disk under retention.** The two halves are linked: you cannot reclaim until you have shipped, for streams that require it.

---

> - Acknowledgment modes
>   - `BEST_EFFORT`: accepted into bounded memory
>     - May be lost on process crash.
>   - `LOCAL_DURABLE`: acknowledged after the required local file flush succeeds.
>   - `REPLICATED`: acknowledged after the chosen remote durability boundary.
>   - A local durable append does not survive loss of the only disk or host.

**What this says in plain words**
The three promises, defined precisely, with a defeater on the second.

1. **`BEST_EFFORT`: in memory, may be lost on process crash.**
   - Worked example: a debug line is accepted in under a millisecond. The process is killed 10 milliseconds later. The line is gone, and that is the documented contract rather than a failure.
2. **`LOCAL_DURABLE`: acknowledged after the local flush succeeds.**
   - Worked example: the record survives the process being killed, because the bytes reached the device. Acknowledgment takes up to 100 milliseconds because it waits for the next group flush.
3. **`REPLICATED`: acknowledged after a remote durability boundary.**
   - Worked example: the record survives the entire host being destroyed, because a copy exists elsewhere. Acknowledgment now includes a network round trip.
4. **The defeater: local durable does not survive losing the disk or the host.**
   - Stated explicitly because "durable" invites over-reading. Worked example: a record is local-durable, the disk fails, and the record is gone. Nothing was violated; the promise was about process crashes, not hardware.

**Why it matters**
The three modes are a price list. Each buys a strictly larger set of survivable failures, and each costs strictly more latency. Making the caller choose is what lets one system serve both debug lines and audit records.

---

> | Requirement | Target | Enforced by |
> |---|---|---|
> | Ingest rate per host | `10,000 records/s` at `200 B/record` | Byte-bounded queue with explicit admission result |
> | Best-effort enqueue latency | p99 < `1 ms` under admitted load | Reserve-and-copy on the producer thread; no I/O on that thread |
> | Group flush trigger | `50 ms` or `1 MB`, whichever fires first | Writer timer plus byte trigger |
> | Local-durable acknowledgment | p99 < `100 ms` under healthy storage | One flush per batch; futures complete after the durable prefix advances |
> | Query latency | p99 < `1 s` for a bounded five-minute range | Sparse index plus pinned manifest; bounded page size |
> | Local retention | At most `24 h` within a `200 GB` budget | Retention worker deletes oldest unpinned sealed segments first |
> | Central freshness | Sealed segment uploaded within `5 min` under healthy network | Uploader with retry; oldest-unshipped-segment alarm |
> | Maximum record size | `64 KB` | Rejected at admission before queue reservation |
> | Data-plane resilience | Append survives a 1 h registry outage for preauthorized streams | Versioned cached configuration with a 1 h policy validity budget |

**What this says in plain words**
Nine promises, each with a number and a mechanism.

- **Row: Ingest rate, 10,000 records per second at 200 bytes.**
  - "with explicit admission result" is the key phrase: the caller is told whether the record was accepted, rather than the system silently dropping under load.
- **Row: Best-effort enqueue latency, p99 under 1 millisecond.**
  - Enforced by "reserve-and-copy on the producer thread; no I/O on that thread".
  - Worked example of why no input-output on the producer thread: the producer thread belongs to the application, serving a user request. Any disk operation there makes the application's latency depend on storage latency. Reserving space and copying bytes is pure memory work, measured in microseconds.
- **Row: Group flush trigger, 50 milliseconds or 1 megabyte.**
  - Two triggers because two different conditions matter. Worked example: at a low rate, the timer fires first and bounds latency. At a high rate, the byte trigger fires first and bounds batch size so a single flush does not become enormous.
- **Row: Local-durable acknowledgment, p99 under 100 milliseconds.**
  - Note it is double the 50-millisecond flush trigger, which is deliberate: a record arriving just after a flush waits nearly a full interval for the next one, plus the flush itself.
  - "under healthy storage" is the defeater, and the weakest assumption later in the file is exactly that this holds.
- **Row: Query latency, p99 under 1 second for a bounded five-minute range.**
  - Both qualifiers matter: bounded, and five minutes. A one-day range is a different request with a different expectation.
- **Row: Local retention, at most 24 hours within 200 gigabytes.**
  - "at most" and "within" together: whichever limit is reached first wins. The retention worker deletes oldest unpinned sealed segments, and every word of that is a condition.
- **Row: Central freshness, 5 minutes.**
  - With an "oldest-unshipped-segment alarm", which is the right metric: not how many segments are waiting, but how old the oldest one is.
- **Row: Maximum record size, 64 kilobytes, rejected before queue reservation.**
  - Worked example of the ordering: rejecting after reserving queue space would let an oversized record consume the queue before being refused. Checking first means the rejection costs nothing.
- **Row: Data-plane resilience, appends survive a 1-hour registry outage.**
  - Bounded by "a 1 h policy validity budget", so cached configuration expires rather than being trusted forever.

---

> - Invariants
>   - One writer owns each active segment's byte offset.
>   - Published durable offset never exceeds a successfully flushed prefix.
>   - A record is either fully framed and checksummed or rejected during recovery.
>   - Retention never deletes a segment still pinned by an active reader.
>   - A claimed loss window includes queue residence and disk stalls.

**What this says in plain words**
Five statements true at every instant.

1. **One writer owns each active segment's offset.** No two things decide where the next byte goes.
2. **Published durable offset never exceeds a flushed prefix.**
   - The central correctness property. Worked example of the violation: advancing the durable offset when the write call returns, before the flush. An acknowledgment is sent, the machine loses power, and the record was never on the device. The system promised something that was not true.
3. **A record is fully framed and checksummed, or rejected during recovery.** There is no third state. A partial record is not partially valid.
4. **Retention never deletes a pinned segment.**
5. **A claimed loss window includes queue residence and disk stalls.**
   - The most intellectually honest line in the file, and the over-engineering warning returns to it.
   - Worked example: the flush timer is 50 milliseconds, so it is tempting to say "at most 50 milliseconds of data can be lost". A record can sit in the queue for 30 seconds during a disk stall before a flush is even attempted. The real loss window is queue age plus flush latency, not the timer setting.

---

> - Non-goals
>   - Full-text or field search
>   - That is a derived index over uploaded segments.
>   - Global ordering across hosts
>   - Ordering is per stream per writer.
>   - Exactly-once delivery to downstream consumers.
>   - A tamper-evident audit ledger
>   - That is a stricter stream class with its own retention and permissions.

**What this says in plain words**
Four exclusions, each with its relocation.

1. **Search is a derived index over uploaded segments.** Not excluded from the product, excluded from this component.
2. **Global ordering across hosts is not provided.**
   - Worked example of what this means: two records written on different hosts at what appears to be the same instant have no defined relative order. Establishing one would require synchronized clocks or a coordination protocol on the hot path, and neither is worth it for logs.
3. **Exactly-once delivery downstream is not provided.** Consumers must tolerate duplicates.
4. **A tamper-evident audit ledger is a stricter stream class.**
   - Worked example of the difference: this design detects accidental corruption with checksums. It does not detect deliberate modification by someone with write access to the files, because they could recompute the checksum. Tamper evidence needs cryptographic chaining and separate permissions, which is a different product.

**Why it matters**
The fourth is the one that protects you. Worked example: an interviewer asks whether this could serve as the audit log for a regulated system. The prepared answer is that audit is a separate stream class with its own retention, permissions, and tamper-evidence requirements, not a mode of this one.

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Host daemon | One process per machine owning the log files, receiving records from applications. | Gives one writer per host without cross-process locking. |
| Acknowledgment mode | The caller's choice of what must be true before the call returns. | Best-effort, local-durable, or replicated. |
| Loss window | How much data a given failure destroys. | Determined by the mode, and larger than the flush timer suggests. |
| Inverted index | A search structure mapping terms to the records containing them. | Excluded; it can exceed the data in size. |

## Capacity and consequence — IC3 baseline

Five estimates. Each ends in the decision it forces.

---

> - Raw write volume
>   - `10,000 × 200 B = 2 MB/s`.
>   - Daily input is `172.8 GB` before framing/index overhead.
>   - Seven days would require about `1.21 TB` before compression.
>   - A `200 GB` local budget holds about `27 h` of raw input, so it cannot promise seven-day local retention at this load.
>   - Decision: cap local retention at `24 h`
>     - Anything longer lives in object storage, so upload is part of the durability story.

**What this says in plain words**
The arithmetic chain from records to a retention policy.

1. **2 megabytes per second.** 10,000 records × 200 bytes.
2. **172.8 gigabytes per day.** 2 megabytes per second × 86,400 seconds.
3. **About 1.21 terabytes for seven days.**
4. **200 gigabytes holds about 27 hours.** 200 ÷ 172.8 × 24 ≈ 27.8.
5. **So seven-day local retention is impossible at this rate.** Not difficult, arithmetically impossible: you would need six times the disk.

**Why it matters — the decision**
Cap local retention at 24 hours, and upload becomes part of the durability story rather than a background nicety.

- The estimate's real work is to close off a requirement. Worked example of the alternative conversation: someone asks for seven days of local logs. Without the arithmetic, this is a negotiation about priorities. With it, it is a statement that 200 gigabytes cannot hold 1.21 terabytes, and the question becomes how much disk to buy or how to compress.
- 24 hours rather than 27 leaves margin, because 172.8 gigabytes per day is the raw figure before framing and index overhead, and because a rate spike would otherwise fill the disk.

---

> - Segment size
>   - A `64 MB` segment fills in approximately `32 s`.
>   - This bounds recovery scan and upload unit size.
>   - Compression is performed on sealed segments and consumes CPU/I/O.
>   - Do not assume a fixed compression ratio.
>   - Decision: `64 MB` keeps the worst-case crash scan to one segment and gives the uploader a unit large enough to amortize per-object overhead.

**What this says in plain words**
Why 64 megabytes, from both directions.

1. **It fills in about 32 seconds.** 64 megabytes ÷ 2 megabytes per second.
2. **It bounds two different things.**
   - **Recovery scan.** Worked example: after a crash, only the active segment can have a torn tail. Scanning and validating 64 megabytes takes well under a second. If segments were 4 gigabytes, recovery would take a minute or more, during which the host logs nothing.
   - **Upload unit.** Worked example of too small: 1-megabyte segments at 2 megabytes per second means 2 uploads per second, each with request overhead, authentication, and a separate object to track. 64-megabyte segments means one upload every 32 seconds.
3. **Compression costs CPU and input-output, and its ratio is unknown.** Log text often compresses well, and binary payloads may not. It must be measured.

**Why it matters — the decision**
The size is a balance between two opposing pressures, and naming both is the point.

- Smaller segments mean faster recovery and more upload overhead.
- Larger segments mean slower recovery and better upload amortization.
- 64 megabytes sits where both are acceptable, and the justification names each.

---

> - Flush batch
>   - At `2 MB/s` a `50 ms` timer yields batches of about `100 KB`, roughly `500 records`.
>   - That is about `20 flushes/s` regardless of record rate.
>   - Per-record flushing would demand `10,000 flushes/s`, an order of magnitude beyond what a typical single disk sustains for synchronous flushes.
>   - Decision: group commit is mandatory for durable mode
>     - The flush count, not the record count, is the storage budget.

**What this says in plain words**
The most important estimate in the file.

1. **A 50-millisecond timer at 2 megabytes per second gives about 100-kilobyte batches, roughly 500 records.**
2. **That is 20 flushes per second, regardless of record rate.**
   - "regardless of record rate" is the crucial property. Worked example: if the rate doubles to 20,000 records per second, the timer still fires 20 times per second and each batch is simply larger, about 1,000 records. The storage cost is unchanged.
3. **Per-record flushing would demand 10,000 flushes per second**, an order of magnitude beyond a typical disk.

**Why it matters — the decision**
Two conclusions, and the second is the sentence to remember.

- **Group commit is mandatory**, not an optimization. Without it, durable mode cannot work at this rate on this hardware.
- **"The flush count, not the record count, is the storage budget."**
  - Worked example of the reframing: 10,000 records per second sounds like a heavy write load. As 20 flushes per second of 100 kilobytes each, it is a light one. The same workload is either impossible or trivial depending entirely on how the flushes are batched.
  - This is why the design's central mechanism is a batching one rather than a faster-disk one.

---

> - Buffering
>   - A `64 MB` queue holds about `32 s` of raw records at the example rate.
>   - With unlimited writer delay, a `50 ms` flush timer does not bound data age to `50 ms`.
>   - Decision: track oldest queued record age and distinguish the timer target from a hard durability SLA
>     - A disk stall turns the whole queue into the loss window.

**What this says in plain words**
The estimate that corrects a comfortable misconception.

1. **A 64-megabyte queue holds about 32 seconds of records.**
2. **A 50-millisecond flush timer does not bound data age to 50 milliseconds.**
   - The timer says how often a flush is *attempted*. It says nothing about how long a flush *takes*.
   - Worked example: the disk stalls for 20 seconds. The timer fires every 50 milliseconds and each flush blocks. Records continue arriving and filling the queue. After 20 seconds there are up to 40 megabytes of unflushed records. A crash now loses 20 seconds of data, not 50 milliseconds.

**Why it matters — the decision**
Two actions, and the second is a discipline rather than a mechanism.

- **Track oldest queued record age.** The direct measurement of the real loss window, which the timer setting cannot tell you.
- **Distinguish the timer target from a hard durability guarantee.**
  - Worked example of the claim that must not be made: "we flush every 50 milliseconds, so you can lose at most 50 milliseconds of logs". It is false whenever storage is slow, which is exactly when failures happen.
  - The honest version: the flush target is 50 milliseconds under healthy storage, the loss window equals the oldest queued record's age, and that age is measured and alarmed on.

---

> - Sparse index
>   - One entry per `64 KB` block gives `1,024 entries` per `64 MB` segment, about `16 KB` at `16 B` per entry.
>   - `200 GB / 64 MB ≈ 3,200` local segments imply about `51 MB` of index.
>   - Decision: the index fits in memory, so a range query costs one positioned read per candidate segment rather than a scan from the segment start.

**What this says in plain words**

1. **1,024 entries per segment.** One per 64-kilobyte block, and 64 megabytes ÷ 64 kilobytes = 1,024.
2. **About 16 kilobytes of index per segment.** 1,024 × 16 bytes.
3. **About 51 megabytes for all 3,200 local segments.**

**Why it matters — the decision**
51 megabytes is nothing, so the whole index stays in memory, and that changes what a query costs.

- Worked example of the difference: a query for records around 14:32. Without an index, the reader opens a candidate segment and scans from byte zero, potentially reading 64 megabytes to reach the right place. With the index in memory, the reader looks up which 64-kilobyte block covers 14:32 and reads directly from that offset.
- Per segment, that is 64 kilobytes read instead of up to 64 megabytes, a thousandfold reduction, bought with 51 megabytes of memory.
- "sparse" is what makes it small: one entry per block rather than per record. At 200 bytes per record there are roughly 320 records per block, so a dense index would be 320 times larger, about 16 gigabytes, which would not fit.

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Framing overhead | The extra bytes a record's length prefix, checksum, and header add. | Why 172.8 gigabytes is a floor, not the real figure. |
| Compression ratio | How much smaller data becomes when compressed. | Workload-dependent, so it is not assumed. |
| Recovery scan | Reading and validating the active segment after a crash. | Bounded by segment size, which is why 64 megabytes matters. |
| Per-object overhead | The fixed cost of each upload request, independent of size. | Amortized by larger segments. |

## Architecture and flow — IC3 baseline

Diagram first, then each component with the mechanism it buys and the price it charges, then one append traced end to end.

---

> ```text
> Stream registry (control plane) --cached config--> host daemon
>                                                      |
> Application producers -> byte-bounded queue -> single segment writer
>                                                 |
>                                       active segment + sparse index
>                                                 |
>                                       seal / manifest publication
>                                                 |
>                                 sealed files -> uploader -> object store
>
> Range query -> pinned segment manifest -> framed record scans
> Central search index <- durable segment/object references
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
A vertical write path, two read paths at the bottom, and configuration arriving from the side.

**Reading the write path, top to bottom**

1. **Application producers → byte-bounded queue.** Many threads, one queue, bounded in bytes.
2. **Queue → single segment writer.** One thread, which is what removes the lock contention.
3. **Writer → active segment plus sparse index.** Bytes to the file, and index entries built as it goes.
4. **→ seal / manifest publication.** When the segment is full or old, it is closed and published.
5. **→ sealed files → uploader → object store.** Asynchronously, off the write path.

**Reading the arrows that are not vertical**

- **Stream registry → cached config → host daemon.** One-way. The daemon polls and caches; nothing on the append path calls the registry.
- **Range query → pinned manifest → framed record scans.** The reader goes through the manifest, not directly to files, which is what makes pinning possible.
- **Central search index ← durable segment/object references.** The arrow points *backwards*, into the diagram. The search index pulls from what this system publishes; this system never pushes to it or waits for it.

**Why it matters**
Two structural properties are visible before a word is spoken: the append path touches no network and no control plane, and the two consumers of sealed data are both pull-based and therefore cannot block writing.

---

> - Stream registry (control plane) owns stream definitions, retention, and authorization.
>   - Mechanism: the daemon polls it and caches last-known-good config.
>   - Cost: a stale cache applies old quota or retention for one poll interval
>     - Static stability is bought with bounded staleness.

**What this says in plain words**
The registry defines streams, their retention, and who may write to them. The daemon polls and caches the last configuration that parsed correctly.

**Why it matters**
"last-known-good" is doing specific work: a configuration that fails to parse is not adopted, so a bad push cannot replace a working configuration with nothing.

- Worked example of the cost: retention is shortened at 14:00, and the daemon polls every 60 seconds. For up to 60 seconds it deletes according to the old policy. That is acceptable for a retention change and would not be for an authorization revocation, which is why the validity budget is bounded at one hour.
- "Static stability is bought with bounded staleness" is the general statement: the append path keeps working without the registry precisely because it is willing to act on slightly old information, and the bound is what keeps that safe.

---

> - Producer path owns admission.
>   - Mechanism: reserves queue bytes before copying the record
>     - Returns accepted, rejected, or a durability future by mode.
>   - Cost: rejection is a real outcome the caller must handle
>     - Best-effort loss is explicit, not silent.

**What this says in plain words**
The producer reserves space first, then copies. It returns one of three things depending on the mode: accepted, rejected, or a future that completes when durability is reached.

**Why it matters**
Reserve-before-copy is the correct order, and rejection being explicit is a design stance.

- Worked example of reserving first: two threads each want 200 bytes and 300 bytes remain. Both check "is there room?" and both see yes, then both copy, and the queue is 100 bytes over. Reserving atomically means one gets 200 and the other is told there are 100 left, which is not enough.
- Worked example of the cost: an application calls the logger and receives a rejection. It must decide what to do, which is real work the application author now has to think about. The alternative, silently dropping, means the application believes every log line was written and the operator has no idea data is missing.

---

> - Queue owns the memory bound.
>   - Mechanism: byte-counted batches with a per-stream share
>     - Oldest-record age is a first-class metric.
>   - Cost: overload drops or blocks
>     - The queue cannot be both lossless and bounded.

**What this says in plain words**
The queue is counted in bytes, divided per stream, and its oldest record's age is a primary metric rather than a debugging detail.

**Why it matters**
The cost line is a statement of impossibility, and it is worth saying aloud in an interview.

- **"The queue cannot be both lossless and bounded."** If input exceeds output and the queue has a size limit, the only choices are to discard data or to stop accepting it. There is no third option, and a design that appears to offer one has simply moved the unbounded growth somewhere else.
- Worked example of the hidden version: an "unbounded" queue does not avoid the choice. It defers it to the moment the process runs out of memory, at which point everything is lost at once rather than the least important records being dropped deliberately.

---

> - Writer owns the active segment and the durable prefix.
>   - Mechanism: drains by size or oldest-record deadline, handles partial writes, flushes before advancing the durable offset, serializes rotation with append.
>   - Cost: throughput is capped by one encode-and-write loop
>     - Scaling needs more partitions, not more writers per segment.

**What this says in plain words**
Four responsibilities in one thread, and a cost that follows directly from there being one thread.

1. **Drains by size or oldest-record deadline.** The two triggers.
2. **Handles partial writes.**
   - Worked example: a write call is asked to write 100 kilobytes and reports that it wrote 60. This is normal behavior, not an error. The writer must loop until every byte is written, and code that assumes a single call writes everything silently loses 40 kilobytes.
3. **Flushes before advancing the durable offset.** The ordering that makes the invariant true.
4. **Serializes rotation with append.** Rotation and appending cannot overlap, or a record could be written to a segment being sealed.

**Why it matters — the cost**
One thread means one thread's throughput, and the remedy is named precisely.

- Worked example: one encode-and-write loop handles perhaps 10,000 to 50,000 records per second depending on record size and encoding cost. Beyond that, adding writers to the same segment reintroduces exactly the coordination the design removed.
- "more partitions, not more writers per segment" is the scaling answer: split the stream into partitions, each with its own single writer and its own segments. Parallelism comes from having more independent things, not from sharing one thing harder.

---

> - Sealer and uploader own publication and central durability.
>   - Mechanism: seal on size or age, write a checksummed footer, publish the manifest generation, upload under a stable object identity.
>   - Cost: two durability states, local and central, and the operator must observe both.

**What this says in plain words**
Four steps and one operational cost.

1. **Seal on size or age.** Age matters for low-volume streams. Worked example: a stream producing 10 kilobytes per hour would never reach 64 megabytes, so without an age trigger its records would sit in an unsealed segment for weeks and never be uploaded.
2. **Write a checksummed footer.** Proof the segment is complete, which recovery uses to distinguish a sealed segment from one that was being written during a crash.
3. **Publish the manifest generation.** The segment becomes visible to readers and the uploader at one moment.
4. **Upload under a stable object identity.**
   - Worked example: the upload times out and is retried. With a stable identity derived from the segment, the retry writes to the same object name and overwrites. With a generated name per attempt, each retry creates a new object, and a flaky network produces five copies of one segment with no way to tell which is authoritative.

**Why it matters — the cost**
Two durability states means two things to watch, and watching only one gives a false sense of safety.

- Worked example: local durability looks perfect, with every flush succeeding. The uploader has been failing for six hours. Every record is on one host's disk and nowhere else, and losing that host loses six hours of logs. The local metrics would show nothing wrong.

---

> - Query owns visibility.
>   - Mechanism: pins a manifest generation and its segments, reads valid frames up to the visibility boundary, releases pins.
>   - Cost: pins delay retention
>     - A leaked pin is a disk leak.

**What this says in plain words**
A reader pins a manifest generation, reads up to a defined boundary, and releases. The cost is that pins hold segments alive.

**Why it matters**
"A leaked pin is a disk leak" is the crisp form of a real operational failure.

- Worked example: a query process crashes while holding pins on 40 segments. Those 2.5 gigabytes cannot be deleted. More queries crash the same way over a week, and the disk fills with segments nothing is reading and retention cannot touch.
- The fix appears later as a lease: a pin expires if not renewed, so a dead reader's pins release themselves.
- "reads valid frames up to the visibility boundary" is the other half: a reader may not read past the durable prefix of an active segment, because beyond it the bytes may be incomplete.

---

> → Reserve bounded queue space and assign a record identity.  
> → Enqueue the record or return an explicit admission failure.  
> → Writer appends a length-prefixed checksummed frame.  
> → Writer flushes the batch when the byte/time trigger fires.  
> → Advance the durable prefix only after successful flush.  
> → Complete durable acknowledgment futures for records in that prefix.  
> → Seal and publish segments for range queries and asynchronous upload.

**What this says in plain words**
One append traced end to end. Walked concretely:

An application logs a 200-byte record to stream `svc-api` in `LOCAL_DURABLE` mode at 14:00:00.000.

1. **Reserve queue space and assign an identity.**
   - 200 bytes plus framing overhead are reserved. The record gets writer epoch 7 and sequence 4,182,993.
2. **Enqueue, or return an explicit failure.**
   - There is room, so it is enqueued and a future is returned. The producer thread's work is finished, having done no disk operations, in well under a millisecond.
3. **Writer appends a length-prefixed checksummed frame.**
   - At 14:00:00.030 the writer drains the queue and encodes the record: length, checksum, epoch, sequence, times, severity, payload. The bytes go into the file, which means into the operating system's page cache.
4. **Writer flushes when the trigger fires.**
   - At 14:00:00.050 the 50-millisecond timer fires. The batch of about 500 records, roughly 100 kilobytes, is flushed to the device. The flush returns at 14:00:00.058.
5. **Advance the durable prefix only after successful flush.**
   - Now, and not before, the durable offset moves to cover this record.
6. **Complete durable acknowledgment futures in that prefix.**
   - The future returns. Total elapsed: 58 milliseconds, within the 100-millisecond target.
7. **Seal and publish.**
   - At 14:00:32 the segment reaches 64 megabytes, is sealed with a footer, published to a new manifest generation, and queued for upload.

**Why it matters**
The trace makes the three promises concrete on one record: accepted at 0.5 milliseconds, locally durable at 58 milliseconds, centrally durable a few minutes later.

| Control plane | The path managing configuration: stream definitions, retention, authorization. | Polled and cached; never on the append path. |

## API and data model — IC3 baseline

The interface table, the identity table, the reference SQL, what it omits, and two blocks on time and framing.

---

> | Surface | Contract | Conflict/error behavior |
> |---|---|---|
> | `append(stream, record, mode)` | Result or future with record sequence | Best-effort acceptance is distinct from durable acknowledgment; oversize or over-quota returns an explicit rejection |
> | `appendBatch(stream, producerEpoch, firstSeq, records)` | Idempotent batch keyed by producer epoch and sequence | Replay of an acknowledged `(epoch, seq)` returns the prior result; a stale epoch is rejected |
> | `flush(stream, throughSequence)` | Wait for a required durable prefix | Deadline/error is visible to the caller; never reports success on a failed flush |
> | `query(stream, from, to, cursor, limit)` | Bounded page with an ordering definition | Cursor pins segment/offset or snapshot generation; an expired pin returns a documented expiry, not silent gaps |
> | `close()` | Stop admission, drain, flush, seal | Bounded shutdown; reports the unflushed remainder |

**What this says in plain words**
Five operations: one append, one batch append, one durability wait, one query, one shutdown.

- **Row: `append(stream, record, mode)`.**
  - Returns either a result or a future, depending on the mode. Best-effort returns immediately; durable returns something to wait on.
  - Two explicit rejections: oversize and over-quota. Both are refusals with a reason, not silent drops.
- **Row: `appendBatch(stream, producerEpoch, firstSeq, records)`.**
  - Idempotent by producer epoch and sequence, which is what makes a retry safe.
  - Worked example: a producer sends records 5,000 to 5,099 under epoch 3. The acknowledgment is lost and it resends the same batch. The daemon sees epoch 3 sequence 5,000 was already acknowledged and returns the prior result, so no duplicates are written.
  - "a stale epoch is rejected" covers the restart case. Worked example: a producer restarts and comes back as epoch 4. A delayed batch from epoch 3 arrives afterwards and is refused, because epoch 3 no longer exists.
- **Row: `flush(stream, throughSequence)`.**
  - Waits until the durable prefix covers a given sequence.
  - "never reports success on a failed flush" is stated because the opposite is a real and tempting bug. Worked example: a flush fails, and the code treats it as best-effort and returns success anyway, so the caller believes an audit record is durable when it is not.
- **Row: `query(stream, from, to, cursor, limit)`.**
  - Bounded pages with a defined ordering, and a cursor that pins either a segment and offset or a snapshot generation.
  - "an expired pin returns a documented expiry, not silent gaps" is the important half. Worked example: a reader paginates slowly, and its pin expires between pages. Continuing would return page 4 from a different set of segments than page 3, with records missing and nothing indicating it. An explicit expiry error is far better than a quietly incomplete answer.
- **Row: `close()`.**
  - Four steps in order: stop accepting, drain the queue, flush, seal.
  - "reports the unflushed remainder" is the honest part. Worked example: a shutdown deadline of 10 seconds expires with 3,000 records still queued. Rather than pretending a clean shutdown, the daemon reports that 3,000 records were not flushed, so the gap is known.

---

> | State | Key | Fields/invariant |
> |---|---|---|
> | Record frame | `(stream, writer_epoch, sequence)` | Length, checksum, ingest time, event time, severity, payload |
> | Producer checkpoint | `(stream, producer_id)` | Epoch, last acknowledged sequence; makes batch replay idempotent |
> | Segment | `(stream, writer_epoch, segment_sequence)` | Path, start/end offset, actual time range, state, checksum |
> | Manifest | `(stream, generation)` | Immutable ordered segment references and durable boundary |
> | Upload progress | `(segment_id, destination)` | Stable object identity, verified checksum, committed state |

**What this says in plain words**
Five identities, and two of them include a writer epoch for the same reason.

- **Row: Record frame, keyed by stream, writer epoch, and sequence.**
  - The epoch is what makes sequences unique across restarts. Worked example: the writer restarts and its sequence counter returns to zero. Without an epoch, sequence 1 after the restart collides with sequence 1 before it. With epoch 7 and epoch 8, they are distinguishable forever.
- **Row: Producer checkpoint.** The record of how far each producer has been acknowledged, which is what `appendBatch` consults.
- **Row: Segment.** Note "actual time range", which the time block below explains: the real observed minimum and maximum, not an assumed range.
- **Row: Manifest, keyed by stream and generation.** Immutable and ordered, so a pinned generation cannot change underneath a reader.
- **Row: Upload progress, keyed by segment and destination.**
  - Keyed by destination as well, so the same segment can be uploaded to more than one place and each tracked separately.

---

> - Reference DDL
>   - Keys and uniqueness match the state table above and the [companion schema](../../1sde/1.backend/2.rest102.md#infra-7).
>   - Record frames, segment bytes, and the manifest file live on local disk; only the stream registry, segment index, producer checkpoints, manifest generations, and upload progress are relational.

**What this says in plain words**
The SQL holds metadata about files. The files themselves, and the records inside them, are on disk and never in the database.

**Why it matters**
Worked example of the ratio: 172.8 gigabytes per day of records, against a few thousand rows describing where they live. Putting records in a database would be the naive design's mistake in a different costume.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE log_streams (                  -- stream registry (control plane)
>   stream_id text NOT NULL, tenant_id text NOT NULL, name text NOT NULL,
>   stream_class text NOT NULL CHECK (stream_class IN ('DEBUG','AUDIT')),  -- audit has stricter retention and permissions
>   retention_seconds bigint NOT NULL CHECK (retention_seconds > 0),
>   quota_bytes bigint NOT NULL CHECK (quota_bytes > 0),
>   upload_required boolean NOT NULL,         -- local deletion waits for a verified central upload
>   version bigint NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (stream_id),
>   UNIQUE (tenant_id, name)
> );
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The registry: one row per stream.

- **`stream_class` restricted to `DEBUG` or `AUDIT`.** The non-goal about audit ledgers appearing as a schema column: audit is a different class with stricter rules, not a mode.
- **`upload_required boolean`** with "local deletion waits for a verified central upload".
  - The single most consequential column in the schema. Worked example: for a debug stream it is false, so retention deletes old segments whether or not they were uploaded. For an audit stream it is true, so a segment that has not been verified centrally cannot be deleted, even if the disk is filling. That is what turns a retention requirement into something enforceable rather than aspirational, and it is why the failure table has mandatory streams applying backpressure.
- **`retention_seconds` and `quota_bytes`, both positive.** Zero would mean delete immediately or store nothing, which is a disable rather than a configuration.
- **`UNIQUE (tenant_id, name)`.** Stream names are unique within a tenant, so two tenants may both have a stream called `api`.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE producer_checkpoints (
>   stream_id text NOT NULL, producer_id text NOT NULL,
>   epoch bigint NOT NULL CHECK (epoch >= 0),
>   last_acked_seq bigint NOT NULL CHECK (last_acked_seq >= 0),
>   last_offset bigint NOT NULL CHECK (last_offset >= 0),
>   updated_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (stream_id, producer_id),
>   FOREIGN KEY (stream_id) REFERENCES log_streams
> );
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
One row per producer per stream, recording how far it has been acknowledged.

- **`epoch` and `last_acked_seq` together** are what `appendBatch` compares against.
  - Worked example: a batch arrives claiming epoch 3, first sequence 5,000. The checkpoint says epoch 3, last acknowledged 5,099. The whole batch is already durable, so the prior result is returned and nothing is written.
- **`last_offset`** records where in the segment those records landed, which lets the prior result include a real location rather than only an acknowledgment.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE segments (                     -- segment index; frames stay in the segment file
>   stream_id text NOT NULL, writer_epoch bigint NOT NULL, segment_sequence bigint NOT NULL,
>   path text NOT NULL,
>   start_offset bigint NOT NULL CHECK (start_offset >= 0), end_offset bigint,
>   ingest_min timestamptz NOT NULL, ingest_max timestamptz,
>   event_min timestamptz, event_max timestamptz,   -- true observed range; may overlap neighbours
>   state text NOT NULL CHECK (state IN ('ACTIVE','SEALED','UPLOADED','DELETED')),
>   size_bytes bigint NOT NULL CHECK (size_bytes >= 0),
>   footer_checksum text,                     -- written at seal; null only while ACTIVE
>   PRIMARY KEY (stream_id, writer_epoch, segment_sequence),
>   UNIQUE (path),
>   FOREIGN KEY (stream_id) REFERENCES log_streams,
>   CHECK (end_offset IS NULL OR end_offset >= start_offset),
>   CHECK (state = 'ACTIVE' OR (end_offset IS NOT NULL AND footer_checksum IS NOT NULL))
> );
> CREATE UNIQUE INDEX segments_one_active ON segments (stream_id) WHERE state = 'ACTIVE';
> CREATE INDEX segments_time_range ON segments (stream_id, ingest_min);
> CREATE INDEX segments_retention ON segments (stream_id, ingest_max)
>   WHERE state IN ('SEALED','UPLOADED');
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The segment index, and it contains the schema's two cleverest constraints.

- **Four states: `ACTIVE`, `SEALED`, `UPLOADED`, `DELETED`.** A segment's whole life, with `UPLOADED` distinct from `SEALED` because central durability is a separate promise.
- **`event_min` and `event_max`** with "true observed range; may overlap neighbours".
  - Worked example of the overlap: segment 40 contains events from 14:00 to 14:05, and segment 41 contains an event timestamped 14:03 that arrived late. Segment 41's event range starts before segment 40's ends. The ranges genuinely overlap, and the query section explains why that breaks a naive search.
- **`CHECK (state = 'ACTIVE' OR (end_offset IS NOT NULL AND footer_checksum IS NOT NULL))`.**
  - Read it as: any state other than active must have both an end offset and a footer checksum.
  - Worked example of what it prevents: a segment marked `SEALED` with a null checksum. A reader would trust it as complete and have no way to verify it. The database makes that row impossible to write.
- **`segments_one_active`, a unique partial index on `(stream_id) WHERE state = 'ACTIVE'`.**
  - The single-writer invariant enforced by the database. At most one active segment per stream, because the index permits only one row per stream matching the condition.
  - Worked example: a second daemon starts and tries to create an active segment for the same stream. The insert violates the unique index and fails, so the split-brain writer is stopped by the schema rather than by hoping the ownership lock worked.
- **`segments_time_range` on `(stream_id, ingest_min)`.** Finds candidate segments for a time-range query.
- **`segments_retention` on `(stream_id, ingest_max)` where sealed or uploaded.** The retention worker's queue: only segments eligible for deletion, ordered by how old their newest record is.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE manifests (
>   stream_id text NOT NULL, generation bigint NOT NULL CHECK (generation >= 0),
>   path text NOT NULL,                       -- fsync'd manifest file; holds the ordered segment references
>   durable_offset bigint NOT NULL CHECK (durable_offset >= 0),
>   published_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (stream_id, generation),
>   FOREIGN KEY (stream_id) REFERENCES log_streams
> );
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
One row per published manifest generation, pointing at the real manifest file.

- **The row records the generation and the durable boundary; the file holds the ordered segment list.**
  - Worked example of why the split: the ordered list can be long, and it must be durable on the host itself so recovery can read it without a database. The row is an index into that, so other components can see which generation is current without reading the file.
- **`durable_offset` in the manifest** ties the published view to the durability boundary, so a reader pinning a generation also learns how far it may read.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE upload_progress (
>   stream_id text NOT NULL, writer_epoch bigint NOT NULL, segment_sequence bigint NOT NULL,
>   destination text NOT NULL,
>   object_ref text NOT NULL,                 -- stable object identity so retries overwrite, never duplicate
>   verified_checksum text,
>   state text NOT NULL CHECK (state IN ('PENDING','UPLOADING','COMMITTED','FAILED')),
>   attempts integer NOT NULL DEFAULT 0 CHECK (attempts >= 0),
>   updated_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (stream_id, writer_epoch, segment_sequence, destination),
>   FOREIGN KEY (stream_id, writer_epoch, segment_sequence) REFERENCES segments
> );
> CREATE INDEX upload_progress_pending ON upload_progress (updated_at) WHERE state <> 'COMMITTED';
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
One row per segment per destination, tracking the upload.

- **`object_ref`** with "stable object identity so retries overwrite, never duplicate", naming the rule at the point of definition.
- **`verified_checksum` nullable.**
  - Worked example of what verification means: after upload, read the object's checksum back from the store and compare it to the segment's. Only then is the copy trustworthy. A successful upload response says the bytes were accepted, not that they are intact.
  - This is what `upload_required` depends on: local deletion waits for a *verified* upload, not merely a completed one.
- **`attempts`** counts retries, so a segment that has failed 200 times is visibly different from one that failed twice.
- **`upload_progress_pending`, a partial index on everything not committed**, ordered by update time. The uploader's work queue, and it stays small regardless of how many segments have been successfully shipped.

---

> - Not captured by the DDL
>   - The record frame (`length | crc32c | writer_epoch | sequence | ...`), the sparse offset index, `durable_offset` on the writer thread, and reader pins with their leases are in-memory or on-disk structures, not rows.
>   - The manifest row records the published generation and durable boundary; the ordered segment list itself is the fsync'd file.
>   - Deviation from the companion: keys are `stream_id`-scoped as in the state table, with `tenant_id` a column of the registry only; the companion's `partition_id`/`base_offset` segment key becomes `(writer_epoch, segment_sequence)`; the state table's `segment_id` is that composite key.

**What this says in plain words**
Three notes.

1. **Four things deliberately absent from SQL:** the frame layout, the sparse index, the writer's in-memory durable offset, and reader pins with their leases.
   - Worked example of why the durable offset is not a row: it advances 20 times per second per stream. A database write on that path would put the database in the durability loop, which is exactly what the design avoids.
2. **The manifest row versus the manifest file.** The row is metadata; the file is the durable artifact recovery reads.
3. **The companion deviations**, with the reasoning: keys are stream-scoped, and the segment key is the epoch and sequence pair rather than a base offset.

---

> - Separate time concepts.
>   - Ingest sequence defines append order even if the wall clock moves backward.
>   - Event time can arrive late or out of order.
>   - Preserve actual event-time minimum/maximum if queries use event time.
>   - Never replace a real minimum with `max(seen)` and call the range correct.

**What this says in plain words**
Four rules about time, and the last is a warning about a specific tempting shortcut.

1. **Ingest sequence orders appends, regardless of the clock.**
   - Worked example: the host's clock is corrected backwards by 2 seconds mid-stream. Record 4,000 has a later ingest timestamp than record 4,001. The sequence numbers still order them correctly, which is why ordering is defined on the sequence rather than the timestamp.
2. **Event time arrives late and out of order.** A property of the data, not a defect.
3. **Preserve the actual minimum and maximum.** The true observed range for each segment.
4. **Never replace a real minimum with the maximum seen so far.**
   - The shortcut being forbidden: forcing each segment's event range to start where the previous one ended, so the ranges tile neatly and a binary search works.
   - Worked example of the damage: segment 41 genuinely contains an event from 14:03, but is recorded as starting at 14:05 to avoid overlapping segment 40. A query for 14:03 to 14:04 now excludes segment 41 and silently misses that record. The index was made convenient by making it false, and the resulting gap is invisible.

---

> - Frame convention
>   - `length | crc32c | writer_epoch | sequence | ingest_time | event_time | severity | payload`.
>   - The length prefix bounds the read
>   - The checksum covers everything after it.
>   - A format version in the segment header lets an old reader refuse rather than misparse.

**What this says in plain words**
The on-disk layout of one record, and three properties of the ordering.

1. **The field order.** Length first, then checksum, then metadata, then payload.
2. **The length prefix bounds the read.**
   - It must come first. Worked example: without knowing the length up front, a reader cannot tell where this record ends and the next begins, so it cannot even attempt to validate. The length says how many bytes to read before anything else is interpreted.
3. **The checksum covers everything after it.**
   - Worked example of what this catches: a torn write leaves 80 of 200 bytes. The reader reads the length, reads 200 bytes, computes the checksum over them, and it does not match, because 120 of those bytes are not this record's data. The record is rejected rather than parsed into nonsense.
4. **A format version in the segment header lets an old reader refuse.**
   - Worked example: a new daemon writes version 3 frames with an extra field. An old daemon reads the header, sees version 3, does not recognize it, and refuses the file. Without the version it would parse version 3 bytes as version 2, and the field offsets would all be wrong, producing records with corrupted timestamps and severities rather than an error.
   - Refusing is the correct failure: a loud stop beats silently wrong data.

## Group commit, rotation, and crash recovery — IC3 baseline

The first deep dive, and the one that reasons at the filesystem boundary rather than the language API. Pseudocode, the concurrency boundary, rotation, the filesystem facts, recovery, and writer identity.

---

> ```text
> WRITE_BATCH(batch):
>     encode bounded frames
>     loop until every frame byte is written or an error occurs
>     flush file data according to the selected durability mode
>     if flush succeeds:
>         advance durable_offset
>         acknowledge records through durable_offset
>     otherwise:
>         leave durable_offset unchanged
>         fail/block pending durable acknowledgments by contract
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The write path, and the ordering of the last two branches is the entire correctness argument.

1. **Encode bounded frames.** Bounded because the maximum record size caps how much memory one frame needs.
2. **Loop until every byte is written.**
   - A write call may transfer fewer bytes than asked. Worked example: a request to write 100 kilobytes returns 60. This is normal, not an error, and code that assumes otherwise silently loses 40 kilobytes with no exception and no log line.
3. **Flush according to the selected durability mode.** Best-effort may skip the flush; durable modes wait for it.
4. **On success: advance the durable offset, then acknowledge.**
   - Both, and in that order.
5. **On failure: leave the offset unchanged, and fail or block pending acknowledgments.**
   - The critical branch. Worked example of the alternative: the flush fails, the code logs a warning and advances the offset anyway. Every caller waiting on those records is told they are durable. They are not, and nothing will ever discover it.

**Why it matters**
The invariant "published durable offset never exceeds a successfully flushed prefix" is implemented by exactly these five lines, and the failure branch is where it is actually enforced.

---

> - Concurrency boundary
>   - Producers touch only the queue
>   - The writer thread solely owns the file descriptor and `durable_offset`.
>   - Advance durable_offset before completing acknowledgment futures.
>   - Dispatch completions on a bounded executor so inline user callbacks cannot block the writer thread.

**What this says in plain words**
Four rules about who touches what.

1. **Producers touch only the queue.** They never see the file, the offset, or the writer's state.
2. **The writer alone owns the file descriptor and the durable offset.**
   - Worked example of what this removes: no lock is needed around either, because nothing else can reach them. The naive design's lock contention is solved by eliminating the sharing rather than by making the lock faster.
3. **Advance the offset before completing futures.**
   - Worked example of the wrong order: complete the futures, then advance the offset. A caller whose future just completed immediately issues a query and reads up to the durable offset, which does not yet include its own record. The caller is told its write is durable and cannot see it.
4. **Dispatch completions on a bounded executor.**
   - The subtlest rule. Completing a future runs whatever callback the application attached.
   - Worked example of the danger: an application's callback logs a message, updates a metric, and makes a blocking network call taking 200 milliseconds. If the writer thread runs that callback inline, the writer is blocked for 200 milliseconds, during which no records are written and the queue grows. One application's slow callback has stalled logging for every stream on the host.
   - A bounded executor means completions run elsewhere, and *bounded* means a flood of completions cannot spawn unlimited threads.

---

> - Rotation sequence
>
> → Stop appending to the old segment under writer ownership.  
> → Write the old segment's final footer and checksum.  
> → Flush the complete sealed segment before publishing it.  
> → Create the next segment with a unique writer epoch and sequence.  
> → Persist the new segment header.  
> → Write and fsync the temporary manifest before atomic replacement where supported.  
> → Apply required directory synchronization for crash-durable namespace updates.  
> → Publish the manifest generation to readers and upload workers.

**What this says in plain words**
Eight steps, in an order where each one exists because of a specific crash.

1. **Stop appending, under writer ownership.** No record can land in a segment being sealed.
2. **Write the footer and checksum.** The completeness proof.
3. **Flush before publishing.**
   - Worked example of the wrong order: publish first, then flush. A reader sees the segment in the manifest and reads it. The machine loses power. The segment's tail was never on disk, and the manifest references a file that is now incomplete.
4. **Create the next segment with a unique epoch and sequence.** Uniqueness so nothing collides with a prior run.
5. **Persist the new segment header.** Including the format version, so it is identifiable before any records are in it.
6. **Write and fsync a temporary manifest, then replace atomically.**
   - The standard safe-replacement pattern: write the new content to a temporary file, flush it, then rename over the old name. A rename within a directory is atomic on common filesystems, so a reader sees either the old manifest or the new one, never a half-written file.
   - Worked example of the unsafe version: open the manifest and overwrite it in place. A crash midway leaves a file that is neither the old manifest nor the new one, and recovery has no valid manifest at all.
7. **Apply directory synchronization.**
   - The step almost everyone omits, explained in the filesystem block below.
8. **Publish the generation.** Only now do readers and the uploader see it.

---

> - Filesystem boundary
>   - `write` or language-level buffered flush alone does not establish disk durability.
>   - On Linux, file `fsync` does not necessarily persist the containing directory entry.
>   - Fdatasync must persist metadata needed to retrieve the data, including file size when required.
>   - It can omit unrelated metadata such as access timestamps.
>   - Neither file-level call alone establishes directory-entry durability.
>   - Exact persistence behavior must match the target filesystem and storage stack.
>   - See [fsync semantics](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/fsync.2.html).

**What this says in plain words**
Six facts about what actually reaches the disk, and this is the block that separates a storage answer from a library answer.

1. **A write call or a language-level flush is not durability.**
   - Worked example of the layers a byte passes through: the program's own buffer, then the operating system's page cache when `write` is called, then the device when the kernel decides or is told to flush, and possibly a volatile cache on the device itself.
   - A language-level flush moves bytes from layer one to layer two. Power loss at that point destroys them.
2. **`fsync` on a file does not necessarily persist the directory entry.**
   - The fact that surprises people. Worked example: a new segment file is created and written, and `fsync` is called on it. The machine loses power. On restart the file's *contents* are safe, and the file may not appear in the directory, because the directory entry recording its existence was itself a change to a different object that was never flushed. The data survived and the name did not, which means nothing can find it.
3. **`fdatasync` persists the data and the metadata needed to retrieve it.**
   - Including file size where required, because a file whose data is durable but whose recorded length is stale reads as shorter than it is.
4. **`fdatasync` may omit unrelated metadata**, such as access timestamps. That is precisely the point: it does less work by skipping metadata that does not affect retrieval.
5. **Neither file-level call establishes directory-entry durability.** So a rename or a file creation needs the directory itself synchronized.
6. **Exact behavior depends on the filesystem and storage stack.**
   - Worked example of why this hedge is honest rather than evasive: different filesystems make different guarantees, a virtualized disk adds another layer, and a device with a volatile write cache may acknowledge a flush before the bytes are on stable media. The correct engineering position is to state the requirement and verify it on the actual stack, not to assume a universal rule.

**Why it matters**
The level-signal table names this exactly: "reasons at the filesystem boundary, not the language API". A candidate who says "we call flush" has described an intention. A candidate who distinguishes `fsync` from `fdatasync` from directory synchronization has described a mechanism.

---

> - Recovery
>   - Read the last durable manifest.
>   - Scan the final active segment using record lengths and checksums.
>   - Truncate only an incomplete/torn tail under the recovery policy.
>   - Treat corruption in the middle of an allegedly durable segment as corruption, not an ordinary tail truncation.
>   - Reconcile orphan complete segments from their headers before deleting them.
>   - Exact failure: a crash after bytes reach the page cache but before `fsync` can leave a valid frame that was never acknowledged.
>     - Retaining a valid unacknowledged frame is allowed by the append contract.
>     - Exact replay deduplication requires a stable producer event ID, not only the writer's new epoch and sequence.

**What this says in plain words**
Five recovery steps and one exact failure, and the distinction in the fourth bullet is the important one.

1. **Read the last durable manifest.** The starting point: what the system believed existed.
2. **Scan the final active segment using lengths and checksums.**
   - Worked example: read the length, read that many bytes, verify the checksum, repeat. Each valid frame advances the position. The first invalid one marks where the good data ends.
3. **Truncate only an incomplete or torn tail.**
   - Worked example: a segment ends with 80 bytes of a 200-byte record. Those 80 bytes are discarded and the file is truncated to the last valid frame.
4. **Corruption in the middle is corruption, not truncation.**
   - The distinction that matters most. Worked example: frames 1 to 500 are valid, frame 501 fails its checksum, and frames 502 to 900 are valid. This is not a torn tail. A crash can only damage the end of a file, so damage in the middle means something else happened: a storage fault, a bug, or tampering.
   - Treating it as a tail truncation would discard 399 valid frames and report success. The correct response is to quarantine the segment and raise a data-integrity incident, because the cause is unknown and silently deleting evidence is the worst possible reaction.
5. **Reconcile orphan complete segments from their headers.**
   - Worked example: a segment file exists on disk but no manifest mentions it, because the crash happened between sealing and publishing. Its header identifies its stream, epoch, and sequence, so it can be reinstated rather than deleted as unknown debris.

**The exact failure, walked through**

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1 | The writer writes a batch. Bytes reach the operating system's page cache. |
| 2 | The flush has not yet been called or has not yet returned. |
| 3 | The process crashes, but the machine keeps running. |
| 4 | The operating system writes its page cache to disk as it normally would. |
| 5 | On restart, recovery finds a complete, valid, checksummed frame that was never acknowledged to anyone. |

- **Retaining it is allowed.** The contract says an acknowledged record is durable. It does not say an unacknowledged record was discarded. The caller was never told it succeeded, so it may retry, and the retained copy plus the retry is a duplicate rather than a loss.
- **The consequence, stated precisely:** this is a duplication problem, not a loss problem, and duplication is the recoverable direction.
- **Deduplicating it requires a stable producer event ID.**
  - Worked example of why the writer's epoch and sequence are insufficient: after a restart the writer is at epoch 8. The retained frame carries epoch 7 sequence 4,182. The producer retries and the record is written again as epoch 8 sequence 1. Both frames contain the same logical record with different writer identities. Only an identity the *producer* assigned, carried in the payload, can reveal that they are the same event.

---

> - Writer identity
>   - Use a new epoch on restart to avoid sequence reuse.
>   - A daemon or OS-enforced ownership lock prevents two writers sharing one active file.
>   - Distributed ownership additionally needs fencing at the storage boundary.

**What this says in plain words**
Three rules about who is allowed to write.

1. **A new epoch on restart.**
   - Worked example: before the crash the writer reached sequence 4,182 in epoch 7. On restart it begins epoch 8 sequence 1. Without the epoch change, sequence 1 would collide with the sequence 1 written hours earlier, and no reader could tell them apart.
2. **An ownership lock prevents two writers sharing one file.**
   - An operating-system file lock is released automatically when the holding process dies, which is what makes it suitable. Worked example: the daemon is killed without cleanup. The lock is released by the kernel, and the replacement daemon acquires it immediately with no stale-lock cleanup step.
3. **Distributed ownership needs fencing at the storage boundary.**
   - The case where the file is on shared or network storage rather than a local disk.
   - Worked example of why a lock is not enough there: daemon A holds the lock, is partitioned from the lock service, and its lock expires. Daemon B acquires it and starts writing. A is not dead and still holds an open file handle, and it writes too. Both are writing to one file, and the lock service cannot stop A because A is no longer talking to it.
   - Fencing at the storage boundary means the storage layer itself rejects writes carrying an old epoch, so A's writes fail even though A still believes it is the owner. The enforcement has to live where the writes land, not where the lock is held.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Partial write | A write call transferring fewer bytes than requested. | Normal behavior; ignoring it loses data silently. |
| Durable offset | The published boundary below which bytes are genuinely on disk. | Advanced only on flush success, never on failure. |
| File descriptor ownership | One thread alone holding and using the open file. | Removes lock contention by removing sharing. |
| Bounded executor | A thread pool with a fixed size, used to run completions. | Stops an application callback blocking the writer thread. |
| Inline callback | Application code run directly on the completing thread. | The specific hazard the executor exists to prevent. |
| Segment footer | A checksummed record written at seal proving completeness. | Written and flushed before publication. |
| Atomic replacement | Writing a temporary file and renaming it over the target. | A reader sees the old or the new manifest, never a partial one. |
| Page cache | The operating system's in-memory copy of file data. | Where bytes sit after `write`, before reaching the device. |
| `fsync` | A call forcing a file's data and metadata to stable storage. | Does not necessarily persist the containing directory entry. |
| `fdatasync` | A call forcing data plus the metadata needed to retrieve it. | Skips unrelated metadata such as access times. |
| Directory entry durability | Making the record of a file's existence survive a crash. | Requires synchronizing the directory, not the file. |
| Volatile device cache | Memory on the storage device that can acknowledge before persisting. | Why exact behavior must be verified on the real stack. |
| Torn tail | An incomplete record at the end of a file, caused by a crash. | The only damage a crash can produce, and the only kind truncation may fix. |
| Mid-file corruption | An invalid frame with valid frames after it. | Cannot be crash damage, so it is an integrity incident. |
| Orphan segment | A complete segment file no manifest references. | Reinstated from its header rather than deleted. |
| Unacknowledged durable frame | A valid record on disk that no caller was told succeeded. | Produces duplication on retry, which is the recoverable direction. |
| Producer event ID | An identity assigned by the producer and carried in the payload. | The only thing that can deduplicate across a writer restart. |
| Writer epoch | A number identifying one run of the writer. | Prevents sequence reuse after a restart. |
| Ownership lock | An operating-system lock the kernel releases when the holder dies. | Prevents two local writers sharing one file. |
| Storage-boundary fencing | The storage layer rejecting writes from a superseded owner. | Required when the file is on shared storage and a lock is not enough. |

## Time-range query and retention — IC3 baseline

The second deep dive. Ingest-time queries, the event-time follow-up, compression, and retention.

---

> - Ingest-time query baseline
>   - Use ingest sequence for append order.
>   - A wall-clock ingest timestamp can move backward and cannot be assumed sorted without a clamping contract.
>   - For wall-clock ranges, use interval-aware segment summaries or a declared logical ingest clock.
>   - Find the first relevant indexed block, then scan framed records.
>   - Include active data only up to the reader's pinned visibility boundary.
>   - Concrete cost: a five-minute range at `2 MB/s` is about `600 MB` across roughly ten segments
>     - The page limit bounds bytes per call.

**What this says in plain words**
Six points, and the second is a correction of a comfortable assumption.

1. **Ingest sequence defines append order.** Always, regardless of clocks.
2. **The ingest timestamp can move backward and is not sorted.**
   - Worked example: a time correction moves the clock back 2 seconds at 14:00:00. Record 4,000 is stamped 14:00:00.5 and record 4,001 is stamped 13:59:58.5. Their timestamps are out of order while their sequence numbers are not. A binary search over timestamps assumes sortedness and can therefore land in the wrong place.
3. **So for wall-clock ranges, use interval-aware summaries or a declared logical clock.**
   - Interval-aware means treating each segment as covering a range that may overlap its neighbours, rather than assuming the ranges tile.
   - A logical ingest clock means enforcing monotonicity by clamping: never record a timestamp earlier than the previous record's. That buys sortedness at the price of the recorded time being slightly wrong during a correction, and it must be a declared contract rather than an accident.
4. **Find the first relevant block, then scan.** The sparse index locates the block; framed scanning takes over inside it.
5. **Include active data only up to the pinned visibility boundary.**
   - Worked example: the active segment has 40 megabytes written, of which 39 are below the durable offset. A reader stops at 39. Beyond it, the bytes may be a partly-written frame.
6. **The concrete cost: about 600 megabytes across roughly ten segments for a five-minute range.**
   - 2 megabytes per second × 300 seconds = 600 megabytes, and at 64 megabytes per segment that is about ten.
   - "The page limit bounds bytes per call" is the consequence: nobody receives 600 megabytes in one response. The query returns a bounded page and a cursor.

---

> - Event-time query follow-up
>   - Late events can make segment event-time ranges overlap.
>   - Binary-searching only `t_min` and starting at one predecessor can skip older overlapping segments.
>   - Use interval-aware candidate selection or a bounded scan of all overlapping range summaries.
>   - Return sorted event-time results only if the merge cost is within the query budget.

**What this says in plain words**
Four points about why event-time queries are genuinely harder, and the second describes a specific silent bug.

1. **Late events make ranges overlap.**
   - Worked example: segment 40 covers events from 14:00 to 14:05. An event timestamped 14:02 arrives at 14:06 and lands in segment 41, whose range therefore starts at 14:02, before segment 40 ended. The ranges intersect, and no ordering of segments makes them tile.
2. **Binary search plus one predecessor can skip segments.**
   - The natural algorithm: sort segments by their minimum event time, binary search for the query's start, and step back one segment in case of a boundary straddle.
   - Worked example of it failing: the query asks for 14:02 to 14:03. Segments 36 through 45 all have minimum event times before 14:02, because each contains some late arrivals. The binary search lands at segment 45 and steps back to 44. Segments 36 through 43 are never examined, and any of them may contain events in the requested window. The result is missing records with no error, which is the worst kind of wrong answer.
3. **So use interval-aware selection or scan all overlapping summaries.**
   - Interval-aware selection means an index designed for ranges that overlap, which can answer "which intervals intersect this query?" directly rather than assuming order.
   - The bounded alternative: examine every segment's range summary and select the overlapping ones. Worked example: 3,200 local segments, each with a small summary, so the selection scan reads a few hundred kilobytes of metadata and returns the correct candidate set. That is entirely affordable and much simpler than a specialized index.
4. **Return sorted results only if the merge cost fits the budget.**
   - Worked example: candidates come from twelve segments, each internally sorted by ingest order but not by event time. Producing a globally event-time-sorted result means a twelve-way merge over 600 megabytes. If the query budget is one second, that may not fit, and the honest choice is to return results grouped by segment with their event times, rather than to blow the budget or to claim a sort that was not performed.

---

> - Compression
>   - Whole-file compression may require decompressing from the beginning.
>   - Block compression with an offset index preserves bounded seeking.

**What this says in plain words**
Two compression strategies with very different query consequences.

1. **Whole-file compression destroys seeking.**
   - Worked example: a 64-megabyte segment compressed as one stream to 12 megabytes. A query needs records near the end. Because the compression state at any point depends on everything before it, the reader must decompress from byte zero, doing 64 megabytes of work to read 64 kilobytes of records.
   - This silently negates the sparse index: the index says exactly where to look, and the compression makes that position unreachable without processing everything before it.
2. **Block compression preserves it.**
   - Each block, say 64 kilobytes, is compressed independently, and the index records where each compressed block starts.
   - Worked example: the reader looks up the block, seeks directly to its compressed offset, decompresses that block alone, and scans it. The positioned-read property survives.
   - The cost: independent blocks compress slightly worse, because each starts without the context of the previous one. A few percent of ratio bought back the entire query design.

---

> - Retention
>   - Delete oldest eligible sealed segments after reader pins expire.
>   - Define whether local deletion requires a verified central upload.
>   - If central upload is mandatory and unavailable, backpressure or reject according to stream mode.
>   - A retention requirement cannot be satisfied by silently deleting unshipped mandatory records.
>   - Exact failure: a pin with no expiry plus a stuck uploader fills the disk
>     - Pins carry a lease, and disk runway below one hour pages before admission fails.

**What this says in plain words**
Five rules, and the fourth is the one that makes retention a real guarantee.

1. **Delete oldest eligible sealed segments after pins expire.** Three conditions: oldest first, sealed only, and unpinned.
2. **Define whether deletion requires a verified upload.** The `upload_required` flag from the schema.
3. **If upload is mandatory and unavailable: backpressure or reject.**
   - Worked example: an audit stream requires central upload, the object store has been unreachable for two hours, and the disk is nearly full. The segments cannot be deleted because they were never shipped. The only remaining options are to stop accepting new audit records, or to reject them explicitly.
4. **A retention requirement cannot be satisfied by silently deleting unshipped mandatory records.**
   - The sentence that turns the policy into a guarantee. Worked example of the violation: the disk fills, so the retention worker deletes the oldest segments regardless of upload state to keep the service running. The service stays up and the audit records are gone. The retention promise was quietly converted into a best-effort one at the exact moment it mattered.
   - Stopping the stream is a visible failure someone will notice and escalate. Deleting the data is an invisible one nobody discovers until an audit.
5. **The exact failure: a pin with no expiry plus a stuck uploader fills the disk.**
   - Two independent problems combining. Worked example: a crashed query process left pins on 40 segments, and the uploader has been failing for hours. Neither the pinned segments nor the unshipped ones can be deleted. Free space falls steadily until writes fail.
   - **The two mitigations:**
     - **Pins carry a lease.** A pin expires unless renewed, so a dead reader's pins release themselves. Worked example: a 5-minute lease means a crashed query's segments become deletable 5 minutes later, automatically.
     - **Disk runway below one hour pages before admission fails.**
       - Runway is free space divided by write rate, expressed in hours. Worked example: 7 gigabytes free at 2 megabytes per second is about one hour of runway, which pages someone while there is still an hour to act. Alarming on percentage free would be far weaker, because 5% free means something different at every disk size and write rate.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Ingest sequence | The writer-assigned number defining append order. | Sorted by construction, unlike timestamps. |
| Clamping contract | Declaring that recorded timestamps never go backwards, enforced by adjusting them. | Buys sortedness at the price of slight inaccuracy during a correction. |

## Failure, operations, and security

A nine-row failure table, then what to observe, retry discipline, tenant isolation, and rollout safety.

---

> | Failure | Blast radius | Detection | Degraded behavior | Recovery |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Process dies before flush | Unflushed records on one host | Restart recovery | Best-effort loss possible; durable futures never completed | Scan valid tail; resume with new epoch |
> | Disk full | All streams on the host | Free bytes and writer error | Drop/count best-effort logs; fail durable admission | Restore space under retention policy |
> | Disk flush stalls | Pending durable appends | Oldest queue age and flush latency | Bound waiting producers | Diagnose storage; fail over only with known durable state |
> | Uploader or object store unavailable | Central freshness | Oldest unshipped segment | Continue within local budget; mandatory streams backpressure | Retry stable object IDs; verify checksums |
> | Stream registry (control plane) unavailable | New streams and policy changes | Registry poll failures | Data plane appends on cached config | Refresh config; reconcile quota and retention drift |
> | Query races with deletion | Pinned segments | Reader reference count | Retention waits or query returns documented expiry | Snapshot/pin lifecycle |
> | Record framing corrupt | Segment tail or middle | Length/checksum validation | Quarantine invalid durable segment | Restore replica or escalate data-integrity incident |
> | Wall clock jumps backward | Time-range results on one host | Ingest time below previous frame | Sequence still orders append | Record skew in header; queries key on sequence |
> | Two writers after a failed restart | One stream's active segment | Ownership lock or epoch mismatch | Refuse the second writer | Fence by epoch; recover under surviving owner |

**What this says in plain words**
Nine failures, with radius, detection, degraded behavior, and recovery.

- **Row: Process dies before flush.**
  - Degraded behavior distinguishes the two modes precisely: best-effort records may be lost, and durable futures are *never completed*. The caller waiting on a durable acknowledgment does not receive a false success; it receives nothing, and its own timeout handles it.
- **Row: Disk full.**
  - Radius is every stream on the host, because they share one disk.
  - The degraded behavior is a two-tier response: drop and count best-effort records, and refuse durable admission. Worked example of why the tiers differ: dropping a debug line is the documented contract. Accepting a durable record that cannot be written would be a false promise, so it is refused instead.
- **Row: Disk flush stalls.**
  - Detection is oldest queue age plus flush latency, the two metrics from the capacity section.
  - Recovery says "fail over only with known durable state", which is a caution against a tempting action. Worked example: the disk is stalling, so an operator moves the workload to another host. Records queued on the original host were never flushed and are not on the new one. Failing over without knowing what was durable converts a slow host into lost data.
- **Row: Uploader or object store unavailable.**
  - Detection is oldest unshipped segment, in time rather than count.
  - "mandatory streams backpressure" is the retention rule appearing operationally: a stream requiring upload stops accepting rather than deleting unshipped records.
- **Row: Stream registry unavailable.**
  - Radius is new streams and policy changes only. The data plane appends on cached configuration.
  - Recovery mentions reconciling quota and retention drift, which is the debt the cached window created.
- **Row: Query races with deletion.**
  - Detection is the reader reference count, and the behavior offers two acceptable outcomes: retention waits, or the query returns a documented expiry. Both are explicit; neither is a silent gap.
- **Row: Record framing corrupt.**
  - The radius column names both cases, "tail or middle", which the recovery section distinguished.
  - Recovery is "restore replica or escalate a data-integrity incident". Note that repairing is not offered: a corrupt durable segment is evidence, and the response is to obtain a good copy or investigate, not to patch it.
- **Row: Wall clock jumps backward.**
  - Detection is an ingest time lower than the previous frame's, which is the only reliable local signal.
  - Degraded behavior is one line: "Sequence still orders append". The failure is contained because ordering never depended on the clock.
  - Recovery records the skew in the segment header, so a later reader can see that this segment's timestamps were affected.
- **Row: Two writers after a failed restart.**
  - Detection is the ownership lock or an epoch mismatch, two independent signals.
  - Behavior is to refuse the second writer outright. Worked example of the alternative: allowing both and reconciling later. Two writers interleaving bytes into one file produce frames that are torn in the middle, which recovery must then treat as a data-integrity incident.

---

> - Observe
>   - Accepted versus durable acknowledgment counts.
>   - Queue bytes and oldest age.
>   - Flush latency.
>   - Dropped records by mode and severity.
>   - Disk runway in hours at current rate.
>   - Unshipped segment age.

**What this says in plain words**
Six measurements, and each corresponds to one of the design's promises.

1. **Accepted versus durable acknowledgment counts.**
   - Worked example of the gap being the signal: 10,000 accepted per second and 9,000 durably acknowledged per second means 1,000 per second are accumulating unflushed. The gap, not either number alone, shows the writer falling behind.
2. **Queue bytes and oldest age.** The memory bound and the real loss window.
3. **Flush latency.** The direct measurement of the weakest assumption.
4. **Dropped records by mode and severity.**
   - Both dimensions. Worked example: 5,000 dropped best-effort debug records is expected under load. 5 dropped error-severity records is a different situation entirely, and an undifferentiated drop count would show 5,005 and hide it.
5. **Disk runway in hours.** Free space divided by write rate, which alarms with time to act.
6. **Unshipped segment age.** The central-durability promise, measured the same way as the local one.

---

> - Retry discipline
>   - Uploader retries with backoff and jitter against a stable object identity, so a retry cannot create a second object.
>   - Producers retry only admission timeouts and replay with the same `(epoch, seq)` so the checkpoint dedups.
>   - A flush error is not retried in place
>   - The writer surfaces it and recovery owns the segment.

**What this says in plain words**
Three rules, and the third is the unusual one.

1. **The uploader retries against a stable object identity.** The retry overwrites rather than duplicating.
2. **Producers retry only admission timeouts, replaying the same epoch and sequence.**
   - Both halves matter. Worked example of the first: a rejection for oversize is not a timeout and must never be retried, because it will be rejected identically forever. Worked example of the second: the replay carries the original identity, so the checkpoint recognizes it and returns the prior result.
3. **A flush error is not retried in place; the writer surfaces it and recovery owns the segment.**
   - Counter-intuitive, because retrying a failed flush seems obviously right.
   - Worked example of why it is wrong: the flush failed, so the state of the file is unknown. Some bytes may have reached the device and others not. Retrying the same flush does not establish what happened, and continuing to append to a file in an unknown state compounds the uncertainty.
   - The correct response is to stop using that segment, report the error, and let recovery scan it and establish the truth by validating checksums. Recovery is the component that can determine what is actually on disk; the writer cannot.

---

> - Tenant isolation
>   - Streams are tenant-scoped
>   - Authorization is checked at append and query, not only at creation.
>   - Per-stream queue-byte and disk-share quotas stop a noisy tenant from evicting another's durable records.
>   - Overload sheds best-effort records by severity inside the offending stream only.
>   - Redact secrets before storage
>   - Bound record sizes to prevent memory abuse.
>   - Retention workers hold least-privilege deletion rights
>   - Audit retention is separate from debug-log retention.

**What this says in plain words**
Six rules.

1. **Authorization at append and query, not only at creation.**
   - Worked example of the gap this closes: a producer is authorized when its stream is created. Six months later its permission is revoked, and it keeps appending because nothing re-checks. Checking on every append means revocation actually takes effect.
2. **Per-stream queue and disk quotas.**
   - The phrasing names the specific harm: stopping one tenant "from evicting another's durable records". Worked example: without a disk share, one tenant's flood fills the disk, and retention starts deleting the oldest segments, which belong to a quiet tenant whose records are within their retention window.
3. **Overload sheds by severity, inside the offending stream only.**
   - Two properties. Worked example: a stream exceeds its quota, so debug records are dropped while error records are kept, and no other stream is affected. Shedding across streams would make one tenant's overload another tenant's data loss.
4. **Redact secrets before storage.**
   - Worked example of why this is a logging-specific concern: applications log request bodies and headers during debugging, which routinely contain tokens and passwords. Once written to a segment and uploaded, that secret exists in object storage for the full retention period and in every copy of it.
5. **Bound record sizes to prevent memory abuse.** The 64-kilobyte maximum, framed as a defence rather than a limitation.
6. **Retention workers hold least-privilege deletion rights, and audit retention is separate.**
   - Worked example of the separation mattering: the debug retention worker has a bug and deletes aggressively. Because it has no rights over audit segments, the damage is confined to debug logs. One process able to delete everything would make that bug an audit-data incident.

---

> - Rollout and rollback safety
>   - The segment header carries a format version
>   - A new daemon reads old segments and an old daemon refuses new ones instead of misparsing.
>   - Manifest schema changes follow expand, migrate, contract, so rollback never leaves the manifest unreadable.
>   - Deploy drains the queue under a bounded `SIGTERM` deadline and reports the unflushed remainder.
>   - Canary on a host subset
>   - Watch dropped-record and flush-latency deltas.

**What this says in plain words**
Four rules.

1. **The format version makes the compatibility asymmetric, deliberately.**
   - New reads old, old refuses new. Worked example of why refusal is right: an old daemon encountering a version-3 segment could try to parse it as version 2, and the field offsets would be wrong, producing records with garbage timestamps and severities. Refusing produces an error someone investigates; misparsing produces plausible-looking wrong data nobody questions.
2. **Manifest schema changes follow expand, migrate, contract.**
   - Three phases: readers accept both formats, writers switch to the new one, and only then is the old format removed.
   - The stated purpose is "rollback never leaves the manifest unreadable". Worked example: a new daemon writes a new-format manifest and is rolled back. The old daemon must still be able to read it, or the rollback leaves the host unable to recover at all, which is worse than the bug being rolled back.
3. **Deploy drains under a bounded termination deadline and reports the remainder.**
   - Worked example: on shutdown the daemon stops accepting, drains the queue, flushes, and seals, within, say, 20 seconds. If 3,000 records remain, it says so. An unbounded drain would hang a deploy indefinitely; a silent one would hide the gap.
4. **Canary on a subset, watching dropped-record and flush-latency deltas.**
   - The two metrics chosen are exactly the ones a logging regression would move first. Worked example: a new version's encoding is slightly slower, so flush latency rises from 8 to 40 milliseconds and drops begin under load. Neither shows up as an error; both show up as deltas against the un-upgraded hosts.

| Durable future never completed | A pending acknowledgment that receives no answer rather than a false success. | The honest behavior when the process dies before flushing. |

## Alternatives and IC4 stretch

The option table, four stretch items, the weakest assumption, and the over-engineering warning.

---

> | Option | What it is | Strength | Weakness | Choose when |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Per-record durable append | Flush each required append | Simple acknowledgment boundary | More flush overhead | Low-volume strict local durability |
> | Group commit | Flush batches | Amortizes storage cost | Pending records wait and can fail | Baseline durable throughput |
> | Memory-only enqueue | Async best-effort logger | Low producer latency | Crash loss and overload drops | Noncritical diagnostics |
> | Direct central ingestion | Network append | Central visibility | Network dependency on hot path | Remote durability is explicitly required |
> | Local segments plus upload | Local first, remote later | Tolerates bounded central outages | Two durability/freshness states | Host diagnostics with central search |

**What this says in plain words**
Five options across two decisions: how a record becomes durable locally, and where durability ultimately lives.

- **Row: Per-record durable append.**
  - Genuinely the right answer at low volume, and the strength is real: the acknowledgment boundary is trivially simple, because each record's flush is its own.
  - Worked example of its proper home: 50 records per second of high-value data. 50 flushes per second is affordable, and the simplicity is worth more than the saved operations.
- **Row: Group commit.** The chosen baseline, with a precisely-stated weakness: pending records wait, and they can fail as a batch.
  - Worked example of the shared fate: 500 records are in one batch and the flush fails. All 500 durable acknowledgments fail together, even though most of those records might individually have been written. Batching shares success and failure alike.
- **Row: Memory-only enqueue.** Best-effort mode as a standalone option, right for diagnostics nobody will miss.
- **Row: Direct central ingestion.**
  - "Network dependency on hot path" is the disqualifying weakness for general logging, and the premise-reversal question examines it directly.
- **Row: Local segments plus upload.** The chosen baseline for the durability path, with the honest cost: two states to observe, not one.

---

> - IC4 stretch — audit contract
>   - If an operation requires durable audit before success, best-effort dropping is invalid.
>   - Commit the business operation and durable audit intent in one transaction when possible.
>   - Ship through an outbox and expose reconciliation gaps.
>   - Local logging alone is not an atomic business/audit transaction.

**What this says in plain words**
Four points, and the last is the one that matters most.

1. **Best-effort dropping is invalid under an audit requirement.** If the business rule is "no operation completes without a durable audit record", a mode that may discard records cannot serve it.
2. **Commit the business operation and the audit intent in one transaction.**
   - Worked example: a payment is written to the payments table, and an audit intent row is written to an outbox table, in the same database transaction. Either both exist or neither does.
3. **Ship through an outbox and expose reconciliation gaps.**
   - A separate process reads outbox rows and delivers them onward. Worked example of the reconciliation: compare the count of committed payments against delivered audit records. Any gap is visible and investigable rather than assumed absent.
4. **Local logging alone is not an atomic business/audit transaction.**
   - The sentence that closes off the tempting shortcut. Worked example of the failure: the payment is committed to the database, then the application calls the logger in durable mode. The process dies between the two. The payment exists and no audit record does, and there is nothing anywhere recording that one is missing.
   - No amount of durability in the logging component fixes this, because the problem is that two separate systems were updated in sequence with no atomicity between them. The fix has to live in the business database, which is the only place where the operation and the audit intent can be committed together.

---

> - IC4 stretch — 10× load
>   - At `1,000 hosts`, raw volume is about `172.8 TB/day`.
>   - Central indexing, retention, and compression strategy dominate cost.
>   - Index only necessary fields and keep immutable raw objects for the agreed retention.
>   - On one host, `100,000 records/s` exceeds one writer thread's encode loop
>   - Partition the stream so each partition keeps its own single writer.

**What this says in plain words**
Two different tenfold increases, treated separately.

1. **More hosts: 1,000 hosts at 172.8 gigabytes per day each is 172.8 terabytes per day.**
   - Worked example of what dominates: each host's local design is unchanged, because each still handles 2 megabytes per second. What changes is central cost, where 172.8 terabytes per day arrives at object storage and any index over it.
   - "Index only necessary fields and keep immutable raw objects" is the cost strategy: a full index over 172.8 terabytes per day is far more expensive than the raw storage, so index selectively and keep the raw data available for the rare query that needs it.
2. **More records per host: 100,000 records per second exceeds one writer thread.**
   - Worked example: the encode loop must frame, checksum, and copy each record. At 100,000 per second that is 10 microseconds per record for all of it, which a single thread will not sustain.
   - "Partition the stream so each partition keeps its own single writer" is the answer, and the phrasing preserves the invariant: not multiple writers per segment, but multiple segments each with one writer.
   - The cost, from the non-goals: ordering is per stream per writer, so partitioning means records in different partitions have no defined relative order.

---

> - IC4 stretch — regional durability
>   - Select whether acknowledgment requires a remote copy.
>   - Remote acknowledgment increases latency and availability coupling.
>   - State RPO/RTO for the specified host or regional failure.

**What this says in plain words**
Three points about the replicated mode.

1. **It is a choice, made per stream.**
2. **Remote acknowledgment costs latency and availability coupling.**
   - Worked example of the coupling: with local-durable mode, the object store being down affects freshness only. With replicated mode, the object store being down means durable appends cannot be acknowledged at all. The logging path's availability is now the object store's availability.
3. **State RPO and RTO for the specific failure.**
   - Recovery point objective is how much data may be lost; recovery time objective is how long recovery may take.
   - Worked example of stating them properly: for a single host failure with local-durable mode, the recovery point is everything unshipped, bounded by the 5-minute upload target, so up to 5 minutes of that host's logs. For a regional failure, it is everything in that region not yet replicated elsewhere, which under this baseline is all of it, because there is no cross-region copy.
   - The discipline is naming the failure before quoting the numbers, because the same system has different objectives for different failures.

---

> - IC4 stretch — cost and blast radius
>   - Object storage bytes dominate cost at fleet scale
>   - Tier sealed segments by age and index only the hot window.
>   - Keep the stream registry regional and cached per host so a registry outage affects policy changes, not the append path.
>   - A per-host disk failure loses at most `24 h` of unshipped local data
>   - The upload SLO bounds that to minutes for shipped streams.

**What this says in plain words**
Three points about cost and containment.

1. **Object storage bytes dominate, so tier by age and index only the hot window.**
   - Worked example: the last 7 days in a fast tier and fully indexed, and everything older in an archival tier with no index. Queries over the hot window are fast; queries over older data are slow and rare, and the storage cost of the cold portion falls substantially.
2. **Keep the registry regional and cached per host.** The static-stability property restated as a cost-and-blast-radius decision.
3. **A per-host disk failure loses at most 24 hours, and the upload objective bounds it to minutes.**
   - Worked example of the two bounds: the theoretical worst case is 24 hours, which is all the local retention. The practical case is about 5 minutes, because the upload objective ships sealed segments within that window, so only the active segment and the most recent sealed one are exposed.
   - Naming both is the honest version: the worst case is what happens if uploads have been failing, and the expected case is what happens when they have not.

---

> - Weakest assumption
>   - Healthy storage sustains about `20 flushes/s` of `100 KB` under `50 ms`
>   - A shared or virtualized disk under neighbor load can stall for seconds and turn the whole queue into the loss window.
>   - Mitigation: measure flush latency p99 on the target host class, bound queue age, and expose disk runway before promising any durable-mode latency.

**What this says in plain words**
The assumption underneath every durability promise in the design.

1. **The assumption.** 20 flushes per second of 100 kilobytes each, completing within 50 milliseconds.
2. **Where it breaks: shared or virtualized disks under neighbour load.**
   - Worked example: the host is a virtual machine whose storage is shared with other tenants. A neighbour starts a heavy sequential job. This host's flush latency goes from 8 milliseconds to 4 seconds. Nothing about this host changed, and its durable-mode acknowledgment target of 100 milliseconds is now missed by a factor of forty.
   - The consequence, from the buffering estimate: during a 4-second stall, records keep arriving and the queue keeps growing. The loss window is no longer 50 milliseconds; it is however long the stall lasted.
3. **Three mitigations, in a deliberate order.**
   - **Measure flush latency p99 on the target host class.** Not on a developer machine with a local disk, which would confirm the assumption while testing nothing relevant.
   - **Bound queue age.** Worked example: if the oldest record exceeds a threshold, start rejecting durable appends rather than accumulating an ever-larger loss window. Bounding the age bounds the damage.
   - **Expose disk runway before promising any durable-mode latency.** The ordering is the point: publish the measurement before making the promise, rather than promising a number and discovering later that the storage cannot deliver it.

---

> - Over-engineering warning
>   - Do not promise a `50 ms` maximum loss window from a periodic flush setting alone.
>   - Do not build a replicated distributed log for a host-local daemon whose central copy already lives in object storage.

**What this says in plain words**
Two warnings, of different kinds.

1. **Do not promise a loss window from a timer setting.**
   - Not an effort warning but a correctness one, and it is the file's most repeated point.
   - Worked example of the false claim: "we flush every 50 milliseconds, so at most 50 milliseconds can be lost". During a 4-second disk stall, 4 seconds are at risk. The timer describes how often a flush is attempted, not how long one takes, and the claim is wrong precisely when it matters.
   - The honest version: the loss window equals the oldest queued record's age, which is measured and bounded.
2. **Do not build a replicated distributed log here.**
   - Worked example of the temptation: a consensus-replicated log would give strong durability guarantees and is a well-understood design.
   - Worked example of why it is wrong here: the central durable copy already exists in object storage, which is itself replicated. Building a second replication layer on the hosts duplicates a guarantee you are already buying, adds a cluster to operate, and puts a network dependency on the append path that the local-file design deliberately avoided.

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Acknowledgment boundary | The point at which a caller is told a record is durable. | Trivially simple per record, batched under group commit. |

- **Row: Requirements.**

The second is the whole content of this row.

## Interview pushback and validation

Eight challenges, each with a baseline and a stretch answer, then eight things to test.

---

> - Q1: Is queue acceptance durable?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - No
>     - It only reserves volatile memory.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Name separate local and replicated acknowledgment contracts.

**What is really being asked**
Whether you conflate "the call returned successfully" with "the data is safe".

**The baseline answer, expanded**
No. Acceptance means bytes were copied into memory owned by a process that can die.

- Worked example: a record is accepted at 14:00:00.000 in best-effort mode. The process is killed at 14:00:00.008, before the next flush. The record is gone, and the caller was told it was accepted.
- The word "accepted" is doing exactly the work it should: it means taken in, not made durable, and those are different events separated by up to 50 milliseconds.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Give the full price list rather than only correcting the question.

- **Best-effort:** survives nothing beyond the current process. Sub-millisecond.
- **Local-durable:** survives the process dying, not the disk or host. Up to 100 milliseconds.
- **Replicated:** survives the host being destroyed. Adds a network round trip.
- Naming all three converts a "no" into a description of what the caller can actually choose, which is the answer the question was fishing for.

---

> - Q2: The disk stalls for 30 seconds. How much can be lost?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Queued and unflushed records can exceed the nominal flush interval.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Bound admission and make durability futures fail or block within their deadlines.

**What is really being asked**
The direct test of whether you will repeat the 50-millisecond claim.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Far more than 50 milliseconds.

- Worked example: the disk stalls at 14:00:00. Records keep arriving at 2 megabytes per second. The flush timer fires every 50 milliseconds and each attempt blocks. After 30 seconds the queue holds up to 60 megabytes, roughly 300,000 records. A crash now loses all of them.
- The timer governs how often a flush is attempted. It has no influence on how long one takes, and therefore none on the loss window.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Two mechanisms that bound the damage rather than merely describing it.

1. **Bound admission.** Worked example: when the oldest queued record exceeds a threshold, stop accepting durable appends. The queue stops growing, so the loss window stops growing. Callers receive explicit rejections, which is information they can act on.
2. **Make durability futures fail or block within their deadlines.**
   - Worked example: a caller requested local durability with a 1-second deadline. At 1 second the future fails rather than waiting 30. The caller learns its record is not durable and can decide what to do, instead of being blocked indefinitely on storage.
   - The principle: under a stall, the useful thing is not to keep hoping but to convert the stall into explicit, timely failures.

---

> - Q3: Why does a single-predecessor time search miss records?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Event-time segment ranges can overlap because events arrive late.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Interval indexing or bounded late-event partitions change the query architecture.

**What is really being asked**
Whether you understand why the obvious search algorithm is wrong, specifically.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Because the ranges overlap, so sorting by minimum time does not order the segments meaningfully.

- Worked example of the bug in full: segments 36 through 45 each contain some late arrivals, so each has a minimum event time earlier than 14:02. A query for 14:02 to 14:03 binary-searches on minimum time, lands at segment 45, steps back one to 44, and starts scanning there. Segments 36 through 43 are never opened, and any of them may hold events in the window.
- The result has missing records and no error, which is worse than a failure.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Two architectural options, with different costs.

1. **Interval indexing.** A structure designed for overlapping ranges that answers "which intervals intersect this window?" directly.
2. **Bounded late-event partitions.**
   - Worked example: route events more than 5 minutes late into a separate partition. The main partitions then have non-overlapping ranges and binary search works, and the late partition is always scanned as well. Complexity moves from the query to the write path.
- The simpler third option from the source, worth offering: scan every segment's small range summary and select the overlapping ones. At 3,200 segments that is a few hundred kilobytes of metadata and always correct.

---

> - Q4: The caller requires audit persistence before completing a payment.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Use a durable mode and clarify the atomicity boundary with the business write.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Use a transactional audit intent/outbox where the business database can support it.

**What is really being asked**
Whether a durable logging mode is sufficient for an audit requirement. It is not.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
A durable mode is necessary and not sufficient, and the second clause is the answer.

- Worked example of the gap: the payment is committed to the database, then the logger is called in durable mode. The process dies between them. The payment exists and no audit record does.
- Worked example of the reverse order: the audit record is written durably, then the payment fails. An audit record exists for a payment that never happened.
- Either ordering has a window, because two separate systems are being updated in sequence with no atomicity between them. "Clarify the atomicity boundary" means saying this out loud rather than assuming durability solves it.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Put the audit intent in the same transaction as the business write.

- Worked example: the payment row and an audit intent row are inserted in one database transaction. Either both commit or neither does, with no window. A separate process then reads intent rows and ships them to the logging system.
- The logging system's durability still matters, for the delivery step. It simply cannot provide the atomicity, because the atomicity has to exist where the business data lives.
- "where the business database can support it" is the honest hedge: this requires a transactional store. Against a system without transactions, the answer is different and harder.

---

> - Q5: Can retention delete a file while a reader uses it?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Use reader pins or a defined snapshot/expiry contract.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Keep reclamation behavior portable across local and object storage implementations.

**What is really being asked**
The retention-versus-readers conflict, asked directly.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Two acceptable designs, and both must be explicit.

1. **Reader pins.** Retention waits for the pin to release or expire.
2. **A snapshot or expiry contract.** Retention proceeds, and the reader receives a documented expiry error when its pinned view is gone.
- Worked example of the unacceptable third option: retention deletes, and the query silently returns fewer records than the range contained. Either the reader is protected or the reader is told, and never neither.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
The two storage layers behave differently, and the design must not depend on one's behavior.

- Worked example of the local-filesystem behavior people rely on: deleting a file with an open descriptor removes the name, and the data survives until the last descriptor closes. A reader mid-scan is unaffected.
- Worked example of object storage: there are no open descriptors. Deleting an object makes subsequent reads fail immediately, mid-pagination.
- So a design whose safety comes from filesystem semantics breaks when segments move to object storage, which is exactly where they go. The pin-and-lease mechanism works in both because it does not depend on either.

---

> - Q6: Is compression free on immutable data?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Immutability removes write races, not CPU/I/O cost.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Block compression changes seekability and indexing design.

**What is really being asked**
Whether "immutable" is being treated as a word that makes problems disappear.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Immutability solves a concurrency problem and nothing else.

- What it removes: no reader can see a partially-rewritten file, and no lock is needed between reader and compressor.
- What it does not remove: compressing 64 megabytes costs CPU, reading it costs input-output, and writing the result costs more input-output.
- Worked example: at 2 megabytes per second, a 64-megabyte segment is sealed every 32 seconds. Compressing each one competes for the same disk the writer is using, so the compression work has to be scheduled and bounded rather than assumed free.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
The compression choice reaches into the query design.

- Worked example of whole-file compression: a query needs records near the end of a segment, and the compression state at any point depends on everything before it, so the reader decompresses 64 megabytes to read 64 kilobytes. The sparse index still says exactly where to look, and the position is unreachable.
- Worked example of block compression: each 64-kilobyte block is compressed independently and the index records its compressed offset. The reader seeks, decompresses one block, and scans it.
- The cost is a few percent of compression ratio, because each block starts without the previous block's context. That few percent buys the entire positioned-read property.

---

> - Q7 (premise reversal): Why not send every record straight to the central service and skip the local file?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - The hot path takes a network dependency
>     - A central outage becomes a local write failure or an unbounded client buffer.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Do it only for the audit stream through an outbox in the business database
>     - Keep diagnostics local-first with bounded central staleness.

**What is really being asked**
The premise reversal. The local file, the segments, the manifest, and the recovery logic all exist to avoid the network. Was that worth it?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
The network dependency moves to the hot path, and the failure has only two shapes, both bad.

- Worked example of shape one: the central service is unreachable and the logging call fails. Every application logging anything now receives errors, during an incident, which is exactly when they are logging most.
- Worked example of shape two: the client buffers instead of failing. That buffer is unbounded, or it is bounded and drops. An unbounded buffer grows inside the application process until it runs out of memory, so a logging outage becomes an application outage.
- The deeper point: logging is most valuable during an incident, and an incident is when the network is most likely to be part of the problem. A logging design that requires the network fails at the moment it is needed.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Concede the case where central-first is right, and scope it precisely.

- **For the audit stream, through an outbox in the business database.** The audit record needs atomicity with the business write, which a local file cannot provide, so it goes through the outbox and onward centrally.
- **For diagnostics, stay local-first with bounded central staleness.** Volume is high, value per record is low, and the 5-minute upload target is sufficient.
- The closing framing: the correct answer is not one or the other, it is that the two streams have different value and different requirements, and using one mechanism for both means over-paying for diagnostics or under-protecting audit.

---

> - Q8: The stream registry is down. Can hosts still log?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Yes, on cached last-known-good config
>     - Only new streams and policy changes wait.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Bound the staleness and reconcile quota and retention drift on recovery.

**What is really being asked**
Does a control-plane outage become a data-plane outage?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Yes, hosts keep logging, from cached configuration.

- Worked example: a 1-hour registry outage. Every host continues appending, flushing, sealing, and uploading against the configuration it already holds. What fails is creating a new stream and changing a policy.
- "last-known-good" matters: a configuration that failed to parse is never adopted, so a bad push cannot leave a host with nothing.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Two additions, one preventive and one restorative.

1. **Bound the staleness.** The 1-hour policy validity budget from the requirements table. Worked example of why: without a bound, a registry down for a week leaves hosts enforcing week-old quotas and retention, and an authorization revoked on day one has still not taken effect on day seven.
2. **Reconcile quota and retention drift on recovery.**
   - Worked example: retention was shortened from 24 hours to 6 during the outage. Hosts kept 24 hours. On recovery, they hold 18 hours of data that policy says should be gone, and the reconciliation is deleting it deliberately rather than discovering the discrepancy later.
   - Worked example of quota drift: a stream's quota was reduced, and during the outage it exceeded the new limit. Recovery has to decide whether to shed immediately or converge gradually, and having thought about it beforehand is the difference.

---

> - Validation scenarios
>   - Partial frame write.
>   - Crash between flush and acknowledgment.
>   - Crash during manifest replacement.
>   - Disk-full group commit.
>   - Late event overlapping multiple earlier segments.
>   - Retention while a reader pins a segment.
>   - Producer batch replay with the same `(epoch, seq)`.
>   - Registry unavailable during a retention change.

**What this says in plain words**
Eight tests, each mapping to a mechanism discussed earlier.

1. **Partial frame write.** Write 80 bytes of a 200-byte record, then recover. Assert the tail is truncated and no invalid frame is returned to a reader.
2. **Crash between flush and acknowledgment.** The exact failure from the recovery section. Assert the frame is retained, the caller was never told it succeeded, and a producer replay is deduplicated by its own event identity.
3. **Crash during manifest replacement.** Kill the process between writing the temporary manifest and the rename. Assert recovery finds either the old manifest or the new one, never a partial file.
4. **Disk-full group commit.** Fill the disk mid-flush. Assert the durable offset does not advance, durable acknowledgments fail rather than succeeding falsely, and best-effort records are dropped and counted.
5. **Late event overlapping multiple earlier segments.** The direct test of the query bug. Insert a late event and assert a range query finds it rather than skipping its segment.
6. **Retention while a reader pins a segment.** Assert the segment is not deleted, and separately that an expired pin produces a documented error rather than a silent gap.
7. **Producer batch replay with the same epoch and sequence.** Assert the prior result is returned and no duplicate records are written.
8. **Registry unavailable during a retention change.** Assert appends continue on cached configuration, and that the drift is reconciled when the registry returns.

**Why it matters**
Each item names a mechanism that could be wrong and the observation that would reveal it. Four of the eight reproduce failures described earlier in the file.

|---|---|---|
| Volatile memory | Memory lost when the process or machine stops. | What queue acceptance actually reserves. |
| Acknowledgment contract | The stated promise attached to each mode. | Three of them, with different survivable failures. |
| Admission bounding | Refusing new durable appends when the queue is too old. | Stops the loss window growing during a stall. |
| Durability deadline | A caller's limit on how long it will wait for durability. | Converts a stall into a timely, explicit failure. |

| Open-descriptor semantics | A local filesystem keeping deleted file data alive while it is open. | Not available in object storage, so it cannot be relied on. |
| Whole-file compression | Compressing a segment as one continuous stream. | Forces decompression from the start, defeating the index. |
| Block compression | Compressing fixed-size blocks independently with an offset index. | Preserves positioned reads at a small ratio cost. |
| Hot-path network dependency | Requiring a network call before a local operation completes. | Fails exactly when logging matters most. |
| Unbounded client buffer | An in-process buffer with no size limit. | Turns a logging outage into an application outage. |
| Last-known-good config | The most recent configuration that parsed and validated. | Keeps a bad push from leaving a host with nothing. |
| Policy validity budget | How long cached configuration may be used before expiring. | Bounds how long a revocation can go unenforced. |
| Configuration drift | Divergence between cached and current policy during an outage. | Reconciled deliberately on recovery. |

## Recall card

Five one-liners and a four-sentence closing script, memorized rather than improvised.

---

> - “Separate memory acceptance, local durability, and remote durability.”

**What this says in plain words**
Three promises, named as three, in seven words.

**Why it matters**
It is the file's central idea reduced to its shortest form.

- Worked example of the same record under all three: accepted in memory at 0.5 milliseconds and lost if the process dies; locally durable at 58 milliseconds and lost only if the disk dies; remotely durable a few minutes later and lost only if the object store dies.
- A candidate who says "the logs are durable" cannot answer what a given failure costs. A candidate who names three promises can answer it three times.

---

> - “One writer owns framed append and the durable prefix.”

**What this says in plain words**
A single thread owns the file and the offset, and every record it writes is framed with a length and a checksum.

**Why it matters**
Two properties in one sentence, and each solves a distinct problem from the naive design.

- *One writer* removes lock contention by removing sharing, rather than by making a lock faster.
- *Owns the durable prefix* is what keeps the invariant true: nothing else can advance the offset, so it cannot be advanced without a successful flush.
- Worked example of the cost, which you should be ready to name: one writer means one thread's throughput, and scaling past it requires partitioning the stream, not adding writers to a segment.

---

> - “Crash recovery validates the tail; retention respects readers and upload policy.”

**What this says in plain words**
Two unrelated rules paired because both are about not destroying data carelessly.

1. **Recovery validates the tail**, and only the tail. Worked example: damage in the middle of a segment cannot be crash damage, so truncating there would discard valid records and hide an integrity problem.
2. **Retention respects readers and upload policy.** Two conditions: no deletion of a pinned segment, and no deletion of an unshipped segment on a stream that requires upload.

**Why it matters**
Both halves are about the same temptation, which is to delete something inconvenient. Recovery wants to truncate to the last valid frame; retention wants to free space. Each has a rule saying when it may not.

---

> - “Flush interval is a target, not a proof of maximum data loss.”

**What this says in plain words**
The 50-millisecond timer says how often a flush is attempted. It says nothing about how much data a crash destroys.

**Why it matters**
It is the file's most repeated warning, and the one most likely to be tested directly.

- Worked example: during a 30-second disk stall, the timer keeps firing and each flush blocks. Records accumulate, and a crash loses 30 seconds of data, not 50 milliseconds.
- The honest replacement: the loss window equals the oldest queued record's age, which is measured and alarmed on. That is a number the system actually knows, rather than one derived from a configuration setting.

---

> - “Hosts log on cached config; a registry outage stays in the control plane.”

**What this says in plain words**
A registry outage does not stop logging, because each host already holds the configuration it needs.

**Why it matters**
It names the static-stability boundary precisely.

- Worked example: a 1-hour registry outage during which every host keeps appending, flushing, and uploading. What fails is creating a stream and changing a policy.
- The qualifier to carry with it: the cached configuration has a 1-hour validity budget, so "stays in the control plane" holds for a bounded period rather than indefinitely.

---

> - Wrap-up script
>   - “The design is a bounded queue, one writer per active segment, checksummed frames, and a manifest that readers pin and the uploader ships.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence one: four components, each named by the property that matters.

**Why it matters**
The manifest is described by its two consumers rather than by what it is, which is the more useful description: one publication serves both the read path and the upload path.

---

> -   - “The key invariant is that the published durable offset never exceeds a successfully flushed prefix, so acknowledgment and durability cannot disagree.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence two: the correctness property and the consequence of holding it.

**Why it matters**
The second clause is what makes the first meaningful.

- Worked example of them disagreeing: the offset advances on a failed flush, so callers are acknowledged for records that were never written. The system's report and reality have diverged, and nothing will ever detect it.
- Holding the invariant means an acknowledgment is always true, which is the only property that makes durable mode worth having.

---

> -   - “The main tradeoff is group commit: it buys durable throughput at the price of a queue that becomes the loss window under a disk stall.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence three: what you chose and exactly what it costs.

- Bought: 20 flushes per second instead of 10,000, which is the difference between possible and impossible on this hardware.
- Paid: records wait in a queue, and under a stall that queue is precisely what is at risk.

**Why it matters**
The cost is stated in the same sentence as the benefit, and it is the real cost rather than a generic one. Batching does not merely add latency; it creates the loss window the weakest assumption is about.

---

> -   - “My weakest assumption is healthy flush latency; I would measure it on the real host class before promising any durable-mode latency.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence four: the flaw, and the specific first action.

**Why it matters**
"on the real host class" is what makes it a plan rather than a gesture.

- Worked example of the useless version: benchmarking on a developer machine with a local disk, which confirms the assumption while testing nothing about a shared virtualized volume under neighbour load.
- "before promising any durable-mode latency" sets the ordering: measure, then promise. The 100-millisecond target in the requirements table is a claim that has to be earned on the hardware it will run on.

|---|---|---|
| Memory acceptance | A record copied into process memory, with no durability. | The first of three promises. |
| Local durability | Surviving a process crash, because the bytes reached the device. | The second promise; it does not survive losing the disk. |
| Remote durability | Surviving loss of the host, because a copy exists elsewhere. | The third promise. |
