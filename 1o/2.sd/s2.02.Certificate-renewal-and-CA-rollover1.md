## Interview framing

This section of the source does five jobs: it states what level of answer you are aiming at, it states where the question came from, it names why the problem is hard, it gives you the one-line crux, and it gives you the exact sentences to open with plus a clock. We take them in order.

---

> - Target: OCI IC3 ownership of a bounded platform component.

**What this says in plain words**
You are answering as an engineer who owns one clearly-bordered piece of infrastructure end to end: the certificate lifecycle service. Not the whole identity platform, not the whole load-balancer fleet — one component, including its failures and its on-call pager.

**Why it matters**
The word *bounded* controls how much you design. A bounded answer draws the box around "renew, distribute, roll over" and says out loud what is outside the box: issuance policy, revocation, the root key ceremony. An unbounded answer wanders into redesigning the CA itself and runs out of clock.

- Worked example of the boundary
  - Inside the box: when a certificate is renewed, how a new version reaches 1,000 load balancers, how a new CA is introduced without breaking a client.
  - Outside the box: how a certificate signing request is validated, how a revoked certificate is announced, how the root key is generated inside a hardware security module.
  - The source names those three outside items as non-goals later, each with the sibling file that owns it. That is what "bounded" looks like in practice.

---

> - Why this is hard
>   - Naive: a cron job renews certificates, copies PEM files to every host, and swaps the CA certificate on a Friday.

**What this says in plain words**
The obvious first design. A scheduled job wakes up, asks the CA for a fresh certificate, uses `scp` or similar to copy the resulting PEM text files onto every server, and when it is time for a new CA, someone edits the trust file on every machine at the end of the week.

**Why it matters**
This is the design an interviewer expects you to produce in 30 seconds and then immediately attack. Naming the naive design first is what earns you the right to say "and here is where it breaks". Skipping straight to the sophisticated design reads as memorisation.

- Worked example of why the naive design looks fine at first
  - 50 servers, one certificate, renewed by a cron job every 60 days.
  - The job runs, copies one 4 KB PEM file to 50 hosts in 10 seconds, restarts the web server on each.
  - It has worked 20 times in a row over three years. Nobody has touched it.
  - It works *only while every consumer is a host you can copy to, every copy succeeds, and nobody has to change which CA they trust*. The next lines are about what happens when each of those stops being true.

---

>   - First break: a leaf signed by the new CA reaches a client whose trust store lacks that CA, and every handshake to that service fails at once.

**What this says in plain words**
The server got a new certificate signed by the new CA. The client checking that certificate has a trust list that still only contains the old CA. The client cannot find the signer, so it rejects the connection. Every client in that state rejects every connection at the same moment.

**Why it matters**
This is the one failure the entire file is built to prevent. Everything else — versions, generations, acks, evidence — exists to guarantee this sentence never comes true. Notice the shape: the failure is *not* on the server that got the new certificate. The server is perfectly healthy. The failure is one network hop away, on whoever verifies it.

**Worked example: the Friday swap**

- 200,000 load balancers in the fleet. A new subordinate CA is created.
- The Friday script swaps the CA and re-issues the service's leaf at 17:00.
- 160,000 load balancers happened to pull the new trust file already. 40,000 are on a slower schedule and still have only the old CA.
- Every connection landing on those 40,000 now fails with `unknown issuer`. That is 20% of all traffic, instantly.
- Nobody is watching the server that got the new leaf, because it is fine. The alarms fire on client error rates, 45 minutes later, in a different team.

→ Leaf switched before trust reached every verifier.  
⇒ 20% of handshakes fail at once, with the failure appearing one hop away from the change.  
→ This is why the source insists on "trust before leaves" and on evidence from the verifiers, not the servers.

---

>   - Second break: the control plane cannot force consumers to switch
>     - A retried renewal issues two versions
>     - A stale rollout overwrites a newer one
>     - Nobody knows which serial is serving traffic.

**What this says in plain words**
Four distinct problems are packed here. Split them:

1. **The control plane can only offer, not force.** The service that manages certificates can publish a new one. It cannot reach inside a load balancer and make it use the new one. Adoption is the consumer's decision and happens on the consumer's schedule.
2. **A retried renewal makes two certificates.** The renewal job times out and is retried. Both attempts succeed at the CA. Now there are two valid certificates for the same name, and nobody decided which is the real one.
3. **A slow, old rollout can overwrite a newer one.** Rollout A starts at 09:00 and is slow. Rollout B starts at 09:30 and finishes fast. Rollout A's last few hosts get updated at 10:00 — with A's content, which is older than B's. Those hosts have now gone backwards.
4. **Nobody can say which certificate is actually in use.** Each certificate has a serial number. With two versions and half-applied rollouts, you cannot answer "which serial is presenting to clients right now?" And if you cannot answer that, you cannot safely retire anything.

**Worked example for (2): the double renewal**

- The cron job calls `renew`. The CA signs a certificate with serial `A1` at 1.9 seconds, but the response is lost at 2.0 seconds when the job's timeout fires.
- The job retries. The CA signs serial `A2`.
- Both `A1` and `A2` are valid for 90 days. `A1` is on disk on 30 hosts because a helper copied it before the timeout. `A2` is on the other 20.
- A later revocation check, an audit, or a "which certificate are we serving?" question now has two right answers. When you later revoke `A1` thinking it is an orphan, 30 hosts break.

**Worked example for (3): the stale rollout**

- Rollout A pushes certificate version 7 to 1,000 hosts starting at 09:00, at 5 hosts per second, so it finishes at about 09:03:20.
- Rollout A's pusher stalls for 40 minutes on host 900 because of a network partition, then resumes.
- Meanwhile an operator notices a bad extension in version 7, issues version 8, and rollout B pushes it to all 1,000 hosts from 09:30 to 09:34.
- At 09:43 rollout A wakes up and pushes version 7 to hosts 900 through 1,000. Those 100 hosts now serve the certificate that was just replaced.
- Without a "which rollout is newer" number that each host can check, nothing stops this.

---

>   - Minimum primitives
>     - Immutable versions
>     - An idempotent scheduler
>     - Deployments fenced by a generation with per-target acks
>     - Data-plane adoption evidence
>     - A rollover state machine that orders trust before leaves.

**What this says in plain words**
Five building blocks, one per failure above. Take them one at a time:

1. **Immutable versions.** Every certificate and every trust bundle gets a version number and, once created, never changes. Version 7 is version 7 forever. If you want different bytes, that is version 8.
2. **An idempotent scheduler.** Running the renewal job twice for the same input produces the same single result, not two certificates. "Idempotent" means "doing it again changes nothing".
3. **Deployments fenced by a generation with per-target acks.** Each rollout carries a strictly increasing number, the generation. Hosts refuse anything with a lower generation than they already have. Each host reports back — "acks" — what it applied and at which generation.
4. **Data-plane adoption evidence.** The servers that actually perform TLS handshakes report which certificate serial they are presenting and which issuer clients are seeing. That is proof, as opposed to intent.
5. **A rollover state machine that orders trust before leaves.** Replacing a CA is a sequence of phases persisted in a database, and the sequence is fixed: publish the new trust everywhere first, only then switch certificates, only then retire old trust.

**Why it matters**
Each primitive is the smallest fix for one specific break. Immutable versions fix "which one is real". The idempotent scheduler fixes the double renewal. Generations fix the stale rollout. Adoption evidence fixes "nobody knows which serial serves". The state machine fixes the Friday swap. If you can pair each primitive with its break in the interview, you have shown you designed from failures rather than from a template.

- Worked example of the primitives fixing the stale rollout
  - Rollout A is generation 12. Rollout B is generation 13.
  - Host 950 already applied generation 13 at 09:33.
  - At 09:43 rollout A offers it generation 12. Host 950 compares 12 < 13 and ignores it.
  - Host 950 sends an ack for generation 13. The control plane accepts it because 13 is the current generation. If host 950 had somehow sent an ack for 12, the control plane would reject it as stale.
  - Neither side can go backwards. That is what "fenced" means.

---

> - 🗣 “Renewal is a signing job; rollover is coordination with consumers you do not control, so distribute trust first, switch leaves second, and retire old trust only on serving evidence.”

**What this says in plain words**
This is the one sentence to say if you can only say one. Renewing a certificate is easy: ask the CA to sign. Replacing a CA is hard because it needs every *verifier* — including ones outside your control — to change what they trust, and you cannot make them. So you do it in a fixed order: hand out the new trust, then switch certificates, then remove the old trust only after you have proof nobody is still using it.

**Why it matters**
The 🗣 marker means "quotable". This sentence contains the whole design: the split between the easy job and the hard job, the ordering rule, and the evidence rule. An interviewer who hears this in the first two minutes knows you understand the problem before you have drawn a box.

- Worked example of "consumers you do not control"
  - Your service has 200,000 load balancers with an agent you wrote. You control those.
  - It also has 3,000 customer appliances, 40 Java services with a hand-maintained `cacerts` file, and an unknown number of mobile apps that pinned the old CA. You control none of those.
  - "Distribute trust first" for the ones you control means a deployment. For the others it means a notification, a deadline, and a long overlap. The order is the same; the mechanism differs.

> - Opening script
>   - “I will treat this as three control-plane workflows over one data plane: automatic renewal, versioned distribution, and CA rollover.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence one frames the whole answer. There is one *data plane* — the load balancers and gateways that actually do TLS. There are three *control-plane* jobs that feed it: renewing, distributing, and rolling over the CA.

**Why it is placed here**
An interviewer's first worry is scope. This sentence names the three things you will design and implies the thing you will not (the data plane itself is a consumer, not a thing you build). It also plants the control-plane / data-plane split you will lean on for static stability later.

---

>   - “First I confirm who the consumers are, the leaf and CA lifetimes, and whether the service or the customer holds the private keys.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence two lists the three clarifying questions that most change the design. Who pulls the certificates. How long certificates and CAs live. Who owns the private key.

**Why it is placed here**
Each of these flips a major decision. Consumers with agents can ack; consumers without cannot. A 90-day leaf renews every 60 days; a 24-hour leaf renews hourly. A service-held key can be regenerated by the service; a customer-held key means waiting for the customer. Naming them up front shows you know which questions matter and lets the interviewer redirect you early.

---

>   - “Then I show why the numbers make this a correctness problem, and define an API where every version is immutable and every deployment is fenced by a generation.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence three previews the estimates and the API. The claim "a correctness problem" is a specific finding: the renewal rate is tiny (about 0.2 per second), so throughput is not the challenge; getting exactly one version and never going backwards is.

**Why it is placed here**
This sentence tells the interviewer what your estimates will *conclude* before you do them. That is a strong signal: you are not estimating for its own sake, you are estimating to decide something. It also names the two API properties — immutable versions, generation fencing — that you will keep returning to.

---

>   - “The deep dives are idempotent renewal at two-thirds lifetime and the five-phase rollover gated on adoption evidence.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence four names the two deep dives. One: renewing exactly once, triggered when two-thirds of the certificate's life has passed. Two: replacing a CA in five ordered phases, where each phase only advances when the verifiers have proven they are ready.

**Why it is placed here**
Announcing the deep dives lets the interviewer say "skip the first, I want the second" and saves you clock. It also makes the two numbers — two-thirds and five — memorable hooks that you will fill in later.

---

>   - “I will close with static stability during a control-plane outage, rollback limits, and what changes for a root rollover.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence five promises the ending. Static stability: the data plane keeps serving even if the control plane is down. Rollback limits: you cannot always go back. Root rollover: replacing the top-level CA, which is rarer and harder than replacing a subordinate.

**Why it is placed here**
These are the three things a strong interviewer will ask about if you do not raise them. Promising them up front means you get credit for knowing they exist even if the clock runs out before you reach them.

## Requirements — IC3 baseline

This section turns the prompt into a contract. It has five parts: a hedge about numbers, a clarifying-questions table, the functional scope, the numbered non-functional requirements (NFRs), the invariants, and the non-goals. Every number in the NFR table is a promise you will be asked to defend.

---

> - Numerical targets are interview assumptions unless an adjacent primary source states otherwise.

**What this says in plain words**
The numbers below — 90 days, 50 milliseconds, 15 minutes — are things you decided for the sake of the interview, not measurements from a real system. Where a public document supports a number, the References section links it.

**Why it matters**
An interviewer will pick one number and ask "where does that come from?". "It is an assumption I chose so I could size the design; if you have a real value I will use it" is the right answer. "That is the industry standard" is not, unless you can cite it.

---

> | Question | Assumed answer | Why it changes the design |
> |---|---|---|
> | Who consumes certificates? | Load balancers, gateways, sidecars, VM agents; pull and cache | Pull with ETag and a local cache is what makes the data plane statically stable |
> | Leaf and CA lifetimes? | Leaf `90 d`; subordinate CA `5 y`; root `20 y` (assumed) | Fixes renewal cadence, overlap windows, and how rare a rollover is |
> | Who holds private keys? | CA keys in Vault HSM by handle; leaf keys managed or customer CSR | Managed keys can be rekeyed; customer keys need a CSR workflow and a deadline |
> | Targets per certificate? | Up to `1,000` in a target group | Fan-out, ack table size, rollout batching |
> | Partial rollout acceptable? | Yes, staged with canary; every step rollback-able | Rollout policy, generation fencing, rollback as a forward transition |
> | Slow or offline verifiers? | Yes, some appliances update trust manually | Adoption evidence per cohort; retirement needs explicit exemptions |

**What this says in plain words**
Six questions you ask in the first six minutes, the answer you assume if the interviewer shrugs, and the design decision each answer locks in.

**Row: Who consumes certificates?**
- Assumed: four kinds of consumer — load balancers, API gateways, sidecar proxies next to application containers, and agents on virtual machines. All of them *pull* (ask for updates) and *cache* (keep a copy).
- Why it changes the design: if consumers pull and cache, they keep working when the control plane is unreachable. If the control plane had to *push* to them, a control-plane outage would freeze rotation and a consumer restart during the outage would have nothing to load.
- Worked example: 200,000 consumers each keep last-known-good on disk. The control plane is down for 6 hours. Zero handshakes fail, because every consumer already has a valid certificate and chain locally. With push and no cache, any of the 200,000 that restarted in those 6 hours would come up with nothing to serve.

**Row: Leaf and CA lifetimes?**
- Assumed: a leaf lives 90 days, a subordinate CA 5 years, a root 20 years. All labelled assumed.
- Why it changes the design: 90 days sets renewal at day 60 (two-thirds), so about every two months per certificate. 5 years means a subordinate rollover is a once-per-half-decade event, so it can be slow and careful. 20 years means a root rollover is something most engineers on the team will do once.
- Worked example: if the interviewer instead says "leaves live 24 hours", renewal happens every 16 hours per certificate and issuance becomes a hot path with its own latency budget. The alternatives table later covers exactly that case.

**Row: Who holds private keys?**
- Assumed: CA private keys are inside a hardware security module (HSM) in a Vault service and are referred to by a *handle* — an identifier — never by their bytes. Leaf private keys are either *managed* (the service generates them) or *customer-held* (the customer generates a key and sends a certificate signing request, CSR).
- Why it changes the design: if the service holds the leaf key it can make a fresh key pair at every renewal. If the customer holds it, the service cannot renew alone; it must wait for the customer to send a CSR, and that wait needs a deadline and an alarm.
- Worked example: 1 million leaves, 5% customer-held. That is 50,000 renewals every 60 days that cannot complete without a human or a customer's automation acting. Without a deadline and a page, some fraction of those quietly expire.

**Row: Targets per certificate?**
- Assumed: one certificate is deployed to a *target group* of up to 1,000 consumers.
- Why it changes the design: fan-out of 1,000 means 1,000 ack rows per deployment, and a rollout that must be batched rather than done all at once.
- Worked example: at 1,000 targets and a canary-then-10%-then-50%-then-100% policy, the steps touch 1, 100, 500, and 1,000 targets. Each step waits 10 minutes, so one deployment takes at least 40 minutes to converge. That number appears again in the convergence NFR.

**Row: Partial rollout acceptable?**
- Assumed: yes. You may update one target first, watch it, then the rest in stages. Every stage must be reversible.
- Why it changes the design: staged rollout needs a *rollout policy* (the stage sizes and the wait), needs generation fencing so stages cannot cross, and needs rollback to be "another forward step to an older version" rather than "undo".
- Worked example: stage two (10%, 100 targets) shows a handshake error rate jump from 0.01% to 0.5%. The system automatically creates a new generation pointing at the previous version. The 100 affected targets go back within 5 minutes. The other 900 never saw the bad version.

**Row: Slow or offline verifiers?**
- Assumed: yes. Some hardware appliances have no agent and someone updates their trust list by hand.
- Why it changes the design: you cannot count on an ack from these. Evidence has to be collected per *cohort* — per group of similar verifiers — and retirement of old trust needs a human to explicitly *exempt* any cohort that cannot report.
- Worked example: 3,000 customer appliances, no agent. During rollover, the aggregate says "99.8% of handshakes use the new chain". That 0.2% is all 3,000 appliances, and if you retire old trust on the aggregate you break every one of them. Per-cohort gating catches this; a fleet percentage does not.

---

> - Functional scope
>   - Renew certificates automatically into a new immutable version.
>   - Version trust bundles and distribute them ahead of any leaf switch.
>   - Deploy explicit certificate and bundle versions to target groups with staged rollout and acks.
>   - Collect adoption evidence: downloaded, loaded, serving.
>   - Roll a subordinate or root CA over with an ordered, resumable state machine.
>   - Roll back a deployment to a still-valid previous version.

**What this says in plain words**
Six things the system does. One per line:

1. **Renew automatically into a new immutable version.** No human triggers it. The output is a new version number, and that version's contents never change afterwards.
2. **Version trust bundles and distribute them ahead of any leaf switch.** Trust bundles get version numbers too. A new bundle is always sent out before any certificate that depends on it.
3. **Deploy explicit versions with staged rollout and acks.** A deployment names version numbers — "certificate version 7, bundle version 3" — never "latest". It proceeds in stages and collects acks.
4. **Collect adoption evidence at three levels.** Downloaded (the bytes arrived), loaded (the consumer is ready to use them), serving (live handshakes are using them).
5. **Roll over a CA with a state machine.** Phases in a fixed order, persisted, so a crash resumes where it stopped.
6. **Roll back to a still-valid previous version.** You can go back — but only to a version that has not expired or been revoked.

**Why it matters**
Everything not on this list is out of scope, which protects your clock. And each item maps to a section later: 1 is the renewal deep dive, 2 and 5 are the rollover deep dive, 3 and 4 are fencing and the evidence ladder, 6 is rollback limits.

- Worked example of "explicit, never latest"
  - A deployment says "bundle version 3". Six hours later someone creates bundle version 4 with a mistake in it.
  - The in-flight deployment is unaffected, because it named version 3, not "latest".
  - Had it said "latest", the mistake would have reached every target still in the rollout, and the rollout's canary results would describe a different bundle than the one now being shipped.

---

> | Requirement | Target | Enforced by |
> |---|---|---|
> | Renewal lead time | Enqueue at `2/3` lifetime; `90 d` leaf renews on day `60` | Scheduler predicate over an expiring-versions index |
> | Expiry SLO | `0` auto-renew certificates reach `notAfter` while `CURRENT` | Page at `14 d` to expiry; escalate at `7 d` |
> | Overlap | Both leaf versions valid `≥ 7 d`; old CA trust kept `≥ 30 d` after leaves switch | Version state machine and rollover gates |
> | Assignment read | p99 `< 50 ms`, `99.99%` | ETag on signed manifests; artifacts by hash from object storage or CDN |
> | Convergence | `95%` of targets `LOADED` within `15 min`; `100%` or exemption before retirement | Ack table joined against the target registry |
> | Static stability | Drill 24 h only with > 24 h remaining required validity | Cached leaf, chain, key, and acceptable status; nominal 30 d renewal margin is not a fleet-wide guarantee |
> | Control-plane availability | `99.9%` for lifecycle APIs | Stateless API fleet; replicated metadata store |
> | Rollback | Previous version serving within `5 min` while unexpired and unrevoked | Rollback is a new higher generation, never a decrement |

**What this says in plain words**
Eight non-functional requirements. Each has a number and the mechanism that makes the number true. That third column is the point: a target without an enforcer is a wish.

**Row: Renewal lead time.**
- Target: start the renewal when two-thirds of the lifetime has elapsed. For a 90-day leaf, that is day 60.
- Enforced by: a scheduler runs a query — the *predicate* — over an index of certificates sorted by expiry, and picks the ones past the two-thirds mark.
- Why: day 60 leaves 30 days to notice and fix anything that goes wrong before the certificate stops working.
- Worked example: leaf issued 1 January, expires 1 April (90 days). Two-thirds is 2 March. The scheduler's 5-minute tick on 2 March selects it. If the renewal fails, there are 30 days of retries and alarms before 1 April.

**Row: Expiry SLO.**
- Target: zero. Not "rare". No certificate that is set to auto-renew should ever reach its `notAfter` date while it is still the `CURRENT` version.
- Enforced by: a page (an on-call alert) at 14 days before expiry, escalated at 7 days.
- Why: a certificate expiring in production is a total outage for that service. The SLO is zero because the cost of one is unbounded.
- Worked example: renewal on day 60 fails silently because the customer never sent a CSR. Day 76: page fires (14 days left). Day 83: escalation (7 days left). Two independent human chances before day 90.

**Row: Overlap.**
- Target: both leaf versions — old and new — are valid at the same time for at least 7 days. The old CA stays in trust bundles for at least 30 days after leaves have switched to the new CA.
- Enforced by: the version state machine (a version is not deprecated until its successor is serving) and the rollover gates (the retire phase cannot start until 30 days have passed).
- Why: overlap is what makes rollback possible. If the old version expired the moment the new one was issued, there would be nothing to roll back to.
- Worked example: leaf n expires 1 April. Leaf n+1 issued 2 March. Between 2 March and 1 April both are valid. A rollback on 20 March works. A rollback on 5 April does not; the old version is expired. That is the "while unexpired" clause in the rollback row.

**Row: Assignment read.**
- Target: when a consumer asks "what should I be running?", the 99th percentile response is under 50 milliseconds, and the endpoint is available 99.99% of the time (about 52 minutes of downtime a year).
- Enforced by: an ETag — a short hash of the answer — so most polls return "unchanged" (`304`) with no body, and the actual certificate bytes are fetched separately by hash from object storage or a CDN.
- Why: 200,000 consumers polling means this is the hottest read in the system. Making the common case a tiny `304` keeps it cheap.
- Worked example: 200,000 consumers, hourly polls, so 55 requests per second. 99% of those are `304` responses of a few hundred bytes. The remaining 1% fetch an 8 KB manifest. No consumer ever downloads a certificate from the API fleet; they download from the CDN by hash.

**Row: Convergence.**
- Target: 95% of targets report `LOADED` within 15 minutes of a deployment starting. Before old trust is retired, 100% must be accounted for, either as loaded or as explicitly exempted.
- Enforced by: joining the ack table against the registry of targets. The registry says who *should* ack; the ack table says who *did*.
- Why: without the join, a target that never acks is invisible. It just does not appear. The join turns "did not appear" into "missing", which is a number you can alarm on.
- Worked example: registry lists 1,000 targets. Ack table has 987 `LOADED` rows after 15 minutes. That is 98.7%, passing the 95% bar. The 13 missing are listed by name. Before retirement, each of the 13 must either ack or be exempted by a named owner with an expiry date.

**Row: Static stability.**
- Target: you can *drill* — deliberately cut off — the data plane from the control plane for 24 hours, but only after checking every consumer in the drill has more than 24 hours of validity left.
- Enforced by: every consumer caches its leaf, chain, private key, and the last acceptable revocation status locally. The note that "30 d renewal margin is not a fleet-wide guarantee" means: on average there are 30 days of slack, but some individual certificate might be on day 89, and that one does not have 24 hours.
- Why: static stability is a claim about the *worst* consumer, not the average one. The drill precondition forces you to check the worst one.
- Worked example: 200,000 consumers. 199,990 have between 30 and 89 days remaining. 10 are stuck at 20 hours remaining because their renewals are in `AWAITING_CSR`. A 24-hour drill breaks those 10. The precondition catches it; "we renew at day 60 so we always have 30 days" does not.

**Row: Control-plane availability.**
- Target: the lifecycle APIs — create, renew, deploy, roll back — are up 99.9% of the time (about 8.8 hours of downtime a year).
- Enforced by: the API servers hold no state, so any of them can serve any request, and the metadata database is replicated.
- Why: 99.9%, not 99.99%. The control plane is allowed to be less available than the assignment read, because the data plane does not depend on it minute to minute. That asymmetry is deliberate and worth saying out loud.

**Row: Rollback.**
- Target: after a rollback is requested, the previous version is serving within 5 minutes, provided it is still unexpired and unrevoked.
- Enforced by: rollback creates a new, higher generation that points at the older version. It never lowers the generation number.
- Why: if rollback decremented the generation, a consumer that already applied the higher number would ignore the rollback as stale. Making rollback a forward step means the fencing logic needs no special case.
- Worked example: generation 13 shipped version 8, which is bad. Rollback creates generation 14 pointing at version 7. Consumers see 14 > 13, apply it, and are now serving version 7. Nothing in the consumer knows or cares that 7 is "older".

---

> - Invariants
>   - Trust before leaves: no target serves a leaf whose issuer is missing from the bundle deployed to its verifiers.

**What this says in plain words**
The primary invariant. At no moment may any server present a certificate signed by a CA that the server's clients have not yet been told to trust.

**Why it matters**
This is the first break from the framing section, restated as a rule that must hold at every instant. Everything about rollover ordering exists to keep it true. If an interviewer asks "what is the one thing your system guarantees?", this is the sentence.

- Worked example
  - Verifiers of service X currently have bundle version 3 (old CA only).
  - Bundle version 4 (old CA + new CA) is deployed. 990 of 1,000 verifiers load it. 10 are silent.
  - The invariant says: service X may not switch to a leaf under the new CA yet, because 10 verifiers still lack the new CA.
  - Either those 10 load, or a human exempts them (accepting that they will break), before the leaf switch. The invariant is never silently violated.

---

>   - Versions are immutable
>   - A deployment references explicit versions, never `latest`.

**What this says in plain words**
Two related rules. Once a version exists, its bytes never change. And every deployment names version numbers, not a moving pointer.

**What breaks without it**
Without immutability, "version 7 passed canary" says nothing about version 7 an hour later. Without explicit references, a rollout half-way through could silently start shipping different content than the half already done. Both destroy the meaning of an ack: "I loaded version 7" has to mean one specific thing forever.

---

>   - One authoritative deployment generation per target group
>   - Acks with an older generation are rejected.

**What this says in plain words**
For each group of targets, exactly one generation number is "current" at any moment. A consumer that reports back with an older generation is told no.

**What breaks without it**
The stale-rollout break from the framing section. Two deployments in flight for the same group would each accept acks, and the counts of "who has loaded what" would be meaningless.

- Worked example
  - Group G is at generation 13.
  - A consumer whose clock is slow sends an ack for generation 12 at 09:43.
  - The server replies `409 STALE_GENERATION`. The consumer polls, sees generation 13, and applies it.
  - Had the server accepted the ack, generation 12's deployment might have counted one more `LOADED` and — if it were the last one needed — flipped to `CONVERGED` for a deployment that has already been superseded.

---

>   - Renewal of `(certificate, base version)` produces at most one new version.

**What this says in plain words**
Renewing certificate C from its version 7 can produce version 8 once. A second attempt to renew C from version 7 gets version 8 again, or an error — never a version 9.

**What breaks without it**
The double-renewal break. Two valid certificates for the same name, both on disk somewhere, and no rule for which is real.

- Worked example
  - Renewal request for (C, 7) arrives at 09:00:00. Worker begins signing.
  - A retry for (C, 7) arrives at 09:00:02. It sees version 8 is `PENDING` or `CURRENT` already and returns `409 VERSION_SUPERSEDED`, or returns the same version 8.
  - The database primary key `(tenant, cert, version_num)` is the last line of defence: two inserts of version 8 cannot both succeed.

---

>   - Old trust is retired only after serving evidence shows zero old-chain handshakes in every cohort.

**What this says in plain words**
You remove the old CA from bundles only when every group of verifiers has reported zero handshakes using the old chain. Not "almost zero". Not "zero on average". Zero in each group.

**What breaks without it**
The 3,000-appliance example from the clarifying table. The aggregate hides small cohorts. Retiring on aggregate breaks the smallest cohort every time.

---

>   - CA keys stay in the HSM by handle
>   - A manifest carries public material and a key-delivery reference only.

**What this says in plain words**
The CA's private key never leaves the hardware security module. Software refers to it by a name (the handle) and asks the HSM to sign. The manifest that consumers download contains certificates (public) and, for the leaf private key, a *reference* to where the consumer can get it securely — never the key bytes themselves.

**What breaks without it**
If a CA key were ever in software memory, every certificate it signed becomes untrustworthy the moment that software is compromised, and the only fix is a full rollover. If manifests carried private keys, every cache, CDN edge, and log line that touched a manifest would be a key leak.

- Worked example
  - Manifest for target T contains: generation 13, certificate version 8, chain hash `sha256:ab12…`, bundle version 4, validity dates, a signature over all of that, and `keyRef: vault://tenant-A/keys/leaf-8`.
  - Target T fetches the chain from the CDN by hash and fetches the private key from Vault with its own identity. The CDN never sees the key.

---

> - Non-goals
>   - Revocation and CRL/OCSP distribution (`s2.03`).
>   - Issuance policy and CSR validation (`s2.01`).
>   - Root key ceremony and HSM operations (`s2.04`).

**What this says in plain words**
Three neighbouring problems that you explicitly do not solve here, each with the sibling file that does.

1. **Revocation.** Announcing that a certificate should no longer be trusted before it expires, via certificate revocation lists (CRL) or the online certificate status protocol (OCSP). File `s2.03`.
2. **Issuance policy and CSR validation.** Deciding whether a certificate signing request is allowed and correct. File `s2.01`.
3. **Root key ceremony and HSM operations.** The audited procedure for creating a root key inside the HSM. File `s2.04`.

**Why it matters**
Saying these out loud does two things: it shows you know they exist, and it stops the interviewer from dragging you into them. "Revocation is a separate control plane; I will assume it exists and consume its status" is a complete answer that costs 10 seconds.

## Capacity and consequence — IC3 baseline

Five estimates, each ending in a decision. The pattern to notice: every number is followed by "Decision:", because a number that changes nothing is a number not worth computing.

---

> - Renewals
>   - `1M` leaves renewed every `60 d` is about `16.7k` per day, roughly `0.2/s`.
>   - A `5 min` scheduler tick selects on the order of `60` rows.
>   - Decision: renewal is a correctness workload
>     - One leased scheduler with idempotent enqueue is enough, and the clock goes to fencing and rollover.

**What this says in plain words**
One million certificates, each renewed every 60 days, is a tiny rate. So renewal is not a scale problem.

**The arithmetic, step by step**

1. 1,000,000 leaves ÷ 60 days = about 16,700 renewals per day.
2. 16,700 ÷ 86,400 seconds = about 0.19 per second, rounded to `0.2/s`.
3. A scheduler waking every 5 minutes covers 300 seconds, so it finds about 0.2 × 300 = 60 rows.

**Why it matters**
Sixty rows per tick is nothing. A laptop handles it. That single observation reshapes the whole answer.

- **"Renewal is a correctness workload"** — the sentence the numbers buy. The difficulty is not keeping up; it is never missing one, never issuing two, and never losing track of one.
- Worked example of the contrast: a system doing 50,000 issuances per second needs sharding, connection pooling, and careful backpressure. A system doing 0.2 per second needs a single scheduler that is *definitely* running and a deduplication key that is *definitely* correct.
- **"One leased scheduler with idempotent enqueue is enough."**
  - **Leased** means exactly one instance holds a time-bounded claim on the job, so there is one scheduler at a time and a crashed one is replaced automatically after the lease expires.
  - **Idempotent enqueue** means queueing the same renewal twice produces one renewal.
  - Worked example of why both are needed even at this rate: a lease prevents two schedulers running simultaneously in the normal case. But during a lease handover — the old holder is stalled, not dead, and the new one has taken over — both may briefly be live. Idempotent enqueue is what makes that overlap harmless rather than a duplicate issuance.
- **"The clock goes to fencing and rollover."**
  - "The clock" here means interview time. Having established that renewal throughput is trivial, you spend your 60 minutes on the parts that are actually hard: deployment fencing and CA rollover.
  - This is a strong move to make explicitly. Worked example of the alternative: a candidate who spends 15 minutes designing a sharded, horizontally scaled renewal pipeline has spent a quarter of the interview optimizing something that runs at 0.2 per second, and has not yet reached the part of the question that is difficult.

---

> - Rollover burst
>   - Rolling a subordinate CA with `100k` leaves re-issues all of them.
>   - HSM asymmetric signing is order of magnitude hundreds per second
>   - `100k / 100 per s ≈ 17 min` of signing, closer to `1 h` with policy checks and commits.
>   - Decision: `SWITCH_LEAVES` is a queued, rate-limited batch with jitter that lasts hours to days by design.

**What this says in plain words**
Rolling over a CA means re-issuing every certificate under it, and that burst is the one place the hardware's throughput limit actually bites.

**The arithmetic**

1. A subordinate CA with 100,000 leaves. Rolling it means all 100,000 need a new certificate signed by the new CA.
2. An HSM signs asymmetric operations at "order of magnitude hundreds per second" — the hedge is deliberate, since it depends on the device, the algorithm, and the key size.
3. 100,000 ÷ 100 per second ≈ 1,000 seconds ≈ 17 minutes of pure signing.
4. "Closer to 1 hour with policy checks and commits" — signing is not the only work. Each issuance also evaluates policy and commits a database transaction, and those add up to roughly a 3–4× multiplier.

**Why it matters**
This is the one estimate in the file where the number is genuinely constraining, and the decision it forces is counterintuitive.

- **The HSM is the bottleneck that cannot be scaled out.** Worked example: adding 50 issuance workers does not help. They all queue behind the same hardware, and the hardware signs at its rated speed regardless of how many things are asking.
- **"`SWITCH_LEAVES` is a queued, rate-limited batch with jitter that lasts hours to days by design."**
  - **`SWITCH_LEAVES`** is a state in the rollover state machine — the phase where leaves move from the old CA to the new one.
  - **"By design"** is the important phrase. The slowness is chosen, not suffered.
  - Worked example of why going as fast as possible is wrong: running the burst at full HSM speed for an hour means routine renewals — the 0.2 per second that must never be missed — queue behind it for that entire hour. A rollover would starve the workload whose failure mode is certificates expiring.
  - **Rate-limited** means the batch is given a budget, say 20 signatures per second, leaving the rest of the HSM's capacity for normal work. At 20 per second, 100,000 leaves take about 83 minutes of signing spread across a much longer wall-clock window.
  - **Jitter** spreads the work so it does not arrive in synchronized clumps.
  - **"Hours to days"** is the honest range. A rollover is a campaign, not an operation.

---

> - Distribution reads
>   - `200k` targets polling hourly with ETag is about `55 req/s`, mostly `304`.
>   - A fleet restart reloading `200k` targets in `5 min` is about `670 req/s`
>   - At `8 KB` per manifest that is about `5 MB/s`.
>   - Decision: pull with jitter and ETag
>     - Push events only hint to poll sooner
>     - Bundle bytes live in object storage or a CDN by content hash.

**What this says in plain words**
Two hundred thousand things asking for their configuration. The steady state is trivial; the restart case is 12× worse and is the one to size for.

**The arithmetic**

1. Steady state: 200,000 targets ÷ 3,600 seconds = about 55 requests per second. "Mostly `304`" — an ETag match returns *Not Modified* with no body.
2. Restart: 200,000 targets all reloading within 5 minutes = 200,000 ÷ 300 = about 670 requests per second.
3. At 8 kibibytes of manifest each, 670 × 8 KB ≈ 5 megabytes per second.

**Why it matters**
The gap between 55 and 670 is the whole point, and the restart case is not hypothetical.

- Worked example of when it happens: a fleet-wide deploy, a region recovering after an outage, or a bad configuration rolled back everywhere at once. In each case every agent starts fresh and asks for its assignment at roughly the same moment.
- 5 megabytes per second is genuinely small — a single server handles it. But 670 requests per second against a control-plane database that normally sees 55 is a 12× spike, and that is where things break if nothing absorbs it.

**The three-part decision**

1. **Pull with jitter.**
   - **Pull** means agents ask; the control plane does not push.
   - Worked example of why pull is safer here: with push, the control plane must track 200,000 connections and know which ones are alive. With pull, an agent that is down simply does not ask, and one that comes back asks immediately. The control plane holds no per-target state at all.
   - **Jitter** randomizes each agent's poll time within the hour, so the 55 per second is genuinely spread rather than 200,000 requests at the top of every hour.
2. **Push events only hint to poll sooner.**
   - The subtle and correct design choice: events exist, but they are never the mechanism that delivers the change.
   - Worked example: a new deployment is created and an event is published. Agents receiving it poll immediately instead of waiting up to an hour. Agents that miss the event poll on their normal schedule and get the same result, just later.
   - The property this buys: the event bus can be completely down and the system still converges, only more slowly. If events carried the change, a dropped message would be a permanently stale agent.
3. **Bundle bytes live in object storage or a CDN by content hash.**
   - The manifest is small and comes from the control plane. The actual certificate and bundle bytes are fetched from object storage, addressed by their SHA-256 hash.
   - **Content-addressed** means the address *is* the hash of the contents, so the same bytes always have the same address and different bytes can never share one.
   - Worked example of what that buys: an agent that already has the artifact with hash `a1b2c3…` does not fetch it again, because the hash is all the identity there is. And an artifact fetched from an untrusted CDN edge can be verified by hashing it — if the hash matches what the signed manifest said, the bytes are right, regardless of who served them.

---

> - Acks and bundles
>   - `200k` targets at about `200 B` per ack row is `40 MB` per fleet-wide deployment
>   - Three retained generations is order of `100 MB`.
>   - Five CAs at about `2 KB` PEM each is `10 KB`
>   - Overlap plus a cross-certificate doubles it to `20 KB`.
>   - Decision: acks are overwritten in place per `(deployment, target)`
>     - Bundle contents are immutable by `sha256` and verified before load.

**What this says in plain words**
Two storage estimates: what the acknowledgements cost, and how big a trust bundle actually is.

**The ack arithmetic**

1. 200,000 targets × 200 bytes per ack row = 40 megabytes for one fleet-wide deployment.
2. Retaining three generations ≈ 120 megabytes, rounded to "order of 100 MB".

**Why it matters**
Forty megabytes is nothing — which is exactly the finding. Acknowledgement tracking is affordable, so there is no reason to sample or approximate it.

- Worked example of the alternative and why it is worse: sampling acks from 10% of targets would save 36 megabytes and destroy the property the whole design depends on. Convergence is defined by *every* target reporting, and you cannot conclude that from a sample.
- **"Acks are overwritten in place per `(deployment, target)`."**
  - One row per target per deployment, updated as the target progresses through `DOWNLOADED → LOADED → SERVING`.
  - Worked example of why in-place rather than append: an append-only ack log would hold 4+ rows per target per deployment, and answering "has target X converged?" would mean finding the latest of several. Overwriting means the row *is* the current state, and counting converged targets is one indexed query.

**The bundle arithmetic**

1. Five CA certificates at about 2 kibibytes of PEM each = 10 kibibytes.
2. "Overlap plus a cross-certificate doubles it to 20 KB" — during a rollover the bundle holds both old and new CAs, plus the cross-certificate linking them.

**Why it matters**
Twenty kibibytes is the size of a small image. That fact quietly justifies several choices elsewhere.

- Worked example: because the bundle is tiny, there is no reason to send deltas, compress cleverly, or ship partial bundles. Every agent gets the complete bundle every time, which removes an entire category of "the agent has an incomplete trust store" bugs.
- It also means keeping the old CA in the bundle for months costs 2 kibibytes per target. The overlap window's storage cost is irrelevant, so the only thing that should ever set its length is safety.
- **"Bundle contents are immutable by `sha256` and verified before load."** Two properties: the bytes cannot change under a given hash, and the agent checks the hash before trusting anything in the file.
  - Worked example of what verification prevents: a truncated download produces a file that parses as a valid bundle but is missing the last CA. Without a hash check, the agent loads it and silently loses trust in one issuer. With the check, the hash mismatch rejects it and the agent keeps its last-known-good.

---

> - Adoption evidence
>   - `200k` targets emitting per-serial counters every `60 s` is about `3.3k` datapoints per second.
>   - Decision: aggregate at the agent as `{issuer SKI, leaf serial} → count` per minute
>     - Ship counters, not handshake events.

**What this says in plain words**
Proving that a rollover is finished requires telemetry from every target, and the shape of that telemetry is the entire decision.

**The arithmetic**
200,000 targets × 1 report per 60 seconds = 200,000 ÷ 60 ≈ 3,300 datapoints per second.

**Why the shape matters more than the number**

- 3,300 datapoints per second is a routine metrics load. Any time-series system handles it.
- The comparison that makes the decision obvious: **"Ship counters, not handshake events."**
  - Worked example of shipping events: a busy load balancer handles 10,000 handshakes per second. Across 200,000 targets, emitting one event per handshake would be on the order of a billion events per second. That is not a telemetry pipeline, it is an impossibility.
  - Worked example of shipping counters: the same load balancer counts handshakes locally, grouped by which certificate was presented, and emits one small row per group per minute. Ten thousand handshakes per second collapses into a handful of counter rows.
  - The reduction is roughly six orders of magnitude, and nothing needed is lost — the question being answered is "is anyone still serving the old chain?", which a count answers exactly as well as a list of individual events.
- **The key: `{issuer SKI, leaf serial} → count`.**
  - **SKI** is the Subject Key Identifier — a short hash of a CA's public key, carried in every certificate it signs. It identifies *which CA* signed this certificate, and it stays stable for that CA's key.
  - **Leaf serial** identifies *which specific certificate* was presented.
  - Together they answer both questions a rollover asks: has this target moved to the new CA, and has it moved to the new leaf certificate.
  - Worked example of why the issuer SKI is needed and not just the serial: during a rollover you care about the CA. A target could be serving a brand-new leaf serial that is still chained to the *old* CA. The serial alone would look like progress; the SKI reveals it is not.
- **Aggregate at the agent**, not centrally. The reduction happens before the data crosses the network, which is what makes the volume manageable at all.

## Architecture and flow — IC3 baseline

A two-zone diagram, seven components each with what it buys and what it costs, then one renewal traced from the scheduler's tick to the old version being retired.

---

> ```text
> [control plane]
> Operator / CI -> API gateway (IAM policy, compartment) -> lifecycle API -> metadata DB
>                                                              (certs, versions, bundles,
>                                                               deployments, acks, audit)
>    Renewal scheduler --(2/3 lifetime, idempotent enqueue)--> queue --> issuance worker --> Vault / HSM Sign
>    Rollover orchestrator --(per-CA state machine)--> deployments (generation g, g+1, ...)
>    Bundle publisher --> object storage (immutable, keyed by sha256) --> CDN
>
> [data plane]
>    Consumer agent (LB / gateway / sidecar)
>      GET assignment (If-None-Match) -> signed manifest
>      -> fetch artifacts by hash -> verify chain against bundle -> hot-swap TLS context
>      -> PUT ack {generation, DOWNLOADED | LOADED | SERVING | FAILED}
>      -> handshake counters by issuer SKI and serial -> adoption evidence store
>    TLS handshakes never call the control plane; last-known-good lives on local disk
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
Two zones. The control plane decides what should be deployed; the data plane fetches it, loads it, and reports back. The last line is the rule that makes the whole thing survivable.

**Why the split is the design**
The same reason as in every certificate system: if a TLS handshake needed the control plane, the control plane's availability would become every service's availability.

- Worked example: the lifecycle API is down for 30 minutes during a deploy. With handshakes depending on it, every service-to-service connection in the fleet fails for 30 minutes. With the split, nothing notices — no new certificates are deployed during that window, and every existing one keeps working.
- "Last-known-good lives on local disk" is the second half of the rule. In-memory state is lost on restart; disk state survives one. A fleet that is statically stable only until something restarts is not statically stable, because in a fleet of 200,000 things restart continuously.

**Reading the control plane, top to bottom**

- `Operator / CI -> API gateway (IAM policy, compartment) -> lifecycle API -> metadata DB`
  - Two kinds of caller: a human and a pipeline. Both go through the same gateway, which checks IAM policy against the compartment the resources live in.
  - The database holds six things, listed in the diagram: certificates, versions, bundles, deployments, acks, and audit. Note that **deployments** and **acks** are first-class rows — this design tracks not just what exists but what has been delivered and confirmed.
- `Renewal scheduler --(2/3 lifetime, idempotent enqueue)--> queue --> issuance worker --> Vault / HSM Sign`
  - The renewal pipeline: a sweep finds certificates past two thirds of their lifetime, enqueues them idempotently, and a worker calls the HSM.
- `Rollover orchestrator --(per-CA state machine)--> deployments (generation g, g+1, ...)`
  - A separate component from the scheduler, and deliberately so. Renewal is routine and automatic; rollover is a supervised campaign with a state machine and human gates.
  - **Generation** is the fencing number. Each deployment to a target group carries a generation, and generations only increase.
- `Bundle publisher --> object storage (immutable, keyed by sha256) --> CDN`
  - Trust bundles are published as immutable, content-addressed artifacts and distributed by CDN.

**Reading the data plane, in the agent's own order**

- `GET assignment (If-None-Match) -> signed manifest`
  - The agent asks "what should I be running?" and sends the ETag it already has. Most of the time the answer is `304`.
  - The response is a **signed manifest** — a small document, signed by the control plane, listing what to fetch and which hashes to expect.
- `-> fetch artifacts by hash`
  - The bytes come from object storage or a CDN, addressed by hash. The manifest said what the hash should be, and the manifest was signed, so the untrusted CDN cannot substitute anything.
- `-> verify chain against bundle`
  - **Before loading anything**, the agent checks that the new leaf actually chains to a CA in the bundle it currently has.
  - Worked example of what this pre-flight catches: a new leaf signed by a CA that has not yet been distributed. Loading it would make every handshake fail with `unknown issuer`. Verifying first means the agent refuses the deployment and acks `FAILED`, and the fleet keeps serving the old, working certificate.
  - This single check is the difference between a rollover that breaks one target and one that breaks the fleet.
- `-> hot-swap TLS context`
  - Load the new certificate without restarting the process.
- `-> PUT ack {generation, DOWNLOADED | LOADED | SERVING | FAILED}`
  - Four states, and they are genuinely different claims:
    - **`DOWNLOADED`** — I have the bytes.
    - **`LOADED`** — I have installed them into my TLS context.
    - **`SERVING`** — I have actually completed a handshake with them.
    - **`FAILED`** — something went wrong; here is the generation it went wrong at.
  - Worked example of why `LOADED` and `SERVING` must be separate: an agent loads a new certificate at 09:00 and receives no connections until 11:00. At 09:05 it reports `LOADED` — true — but nothing has yet proved the certificate works in a real handshake. If the private key were mismatched, the failure appears at 11:00, two hours after the deployment looked successful.
- `-> handshake counters by issuer SKI and serial -> adoption evidence store`
  - The counters from the capacity section, feeding the store that gates retirement.

---

> - Lifecycle API and metadata store own desired state.
>   - Mechanism bought: one source of truth for immutable versions
>     - Mutations return `202` with a work-request identity.
>   - Cost paid: the certificate or target-group row is the consistency boundary, so every version flip and generation bump serializes on it.

**What this says in plain words**
One database holds what *should* be true, and one row at a time decides it.

**The mechanism**

- "One source of truth for immutable versions" — versions never change once written. Version 7 is version 7 forever.
- Worked example of why immutability matters here: an agent that fetched version 7 yesterday and an agent fetching it today get identical bytes. If versions were mutable, two agents could both believe they are running "the current certificate" and be running different things.
- `202` with a work-request identity is the same async control-plane pattern as every other question in this group.

**The cost, which is the interesting half**

- "The certificate or target-group row is the consistency boundary, so every version flip and generation bump serializes on it."
- **Serializes** means those operations happen one at a time for a given row, because each takes a lock on it.
- Worked example of why that is correct rather than a flaw: two concurrent generation bumps on one target group must not both succeed at generation `g+1`. Serializing on the row means the second reads `g+1` as current and becomes `g+2`, so generations stay strictly increasing.
- Worked example of the cost showing up: a target group receiving frequent deployments has all of them queued behind one row's lock. At this design's rates that is irrelevant; at much higher rates it would be the bottleneck, which is why the row is named as the boundary rather than left implicit.

---

> - Renewal scheduler owns "when".
>   - Mechanism bought: a partitioned scan of the expiring-versions index with a dedup identity per `(certificate, base version)`.
>   - Cost paid: one leased leader
>     - A stalled scheduler is invisible until the `14 d` alarm, so scheduler lag is itself a metric.

**What this says in plain words**
One component decides what is due. Its failure mode is silence.

**The mechanism**

- **Partitioned scan** — the sweep is split across partitions so it can be parallelized or resumed, rather than being one enormous query.
- **Expiring-versions index** — an index ordered by expiry, so finding what is due is a range scan rather than a table scan.
- **Dedup identity per `(certificate, base version)`** — the uniqueness key that makes enqueue idempotent. Renewing certificate `X` from version 7 is one thing, no matter how many times it is queued.

**The cost, and the metric it forces**

- "One leased leader" — a single instance at a time, so there is a single point whose failure stops all renewals.
- **"A stalled scheduler is invisible until the `14 d` alarm."** This is the sentence to understand.
  - Worked example: the scheduler dies at 02:00 on a Tuesday. It produces no errors, because nothing is failing — nothing is happening. Renewal requests stop arriving, which looks identical to a quiet period. Certificates renew at day 60 of 90, so the first *symptom* is a certificate reaching 14 days from expiry, which is 16 days after the scheduler died.
  - Sixteen days of a completely broken renewal pipeline, with every dashboard green.
- **"So scheduler lag is itself a metric."** The fix: measure the scheduler's own progress, not just its outputs.
  - Worked example of what that metric looks like: "seconds since the scheduler last completed a sweep". Normally 300 or less. A dead scheduler drives it to 600, then 1,200, then upward without bound, and it alarms within minutes rather than within weeks.
  - The general principle, worth saying out loud: **a component that fails by doing nothing needs a liveness metric, because its output metrics look the same whether it is idle or dead.**

---

> - Issuance worker and Vault HSM own "sign".
>   - Mechanism bought: the worker sends a digest and receives a signature
>     - Documented OCI behavior is that HSM-protected keys cannot be exported.
>   - Cost paid: HSM throughput caps rollover speed and forces queueing with jitter.

**What this says in plain words**
The worker never holds a key. It sends a hash and gets a signature back.

**The mechanism**

- "Sends a digest and receives a signature" — the request is a fixed-size hash regardless of certificate size, and the response is a signature. No key material moves in either direction.
- "Documented OCI behavior is that HSM-protected keys cannot be exported" — a citable platform fact rather than a design aspiration. Worked example of its weight in an interview: it converts "we keep the key safe" into "the platform does not offer an export operation for this key type", which is not arguable.

**The cost**
HSM throughput caps rollover speed — the constraint computed in the capacity section — and forces queueing with jitter. The hardware is the one component that cannot be scaled by adding instances.

---

> - Rollover orchestrator owns ordering.
>   - Mechanism bought: a persisted per-CA state machine whose transitions are gated on evidence.
>   - Cost paid: slow by design
>     - The last gate needs a human to own exemptions.

**What this says in plain words**
A separate component drives rollovers, one state at a time, and each state change requires proof rather than a timer.

**The mechanism**

- **Persisted** — the state machine lives in the database, not in the orchestrator's memory. Worked example of why: a rollover takes days. The orchestrator will be restarted and redeployed during it, and an in-memory state machine would lose its place every time.
- **Per-CA** — each CA rolls independently.
- **"Transitions are gated on evidence."** This is the phrase that distinguishes this design from a scripted rollout.
  - Worked example of the difference: a timer-gated transition says "seven days have passed, move on". An evidence-gated transition says "adoption counters show zero handshakes on the old issuer across all reporting targets, and every target is reporting — move on".
  - The first can proceed while 40 hosts are still on the old chain. The second cannot.

**The cost, and the honest admission at the end**

- **"Slow by design"** — same phrase as the capacity section, for the same reason.
- **"The last gate needs a human to own exemptions."**
  - The final transition — actually retiring the old CA — cannot be fully automated, because there will always be a small cohort that has not converged and will not converge on its own.
  - Worked example: 199,987 of 200,000 targets have adopted. Thirteen are appliances in a datacenter nobody has touched in two years. The evidence gate will block forever, correctly. Someone has to decide: fix them, exempt them, or accept breaking them.
  - "Own exemptions" means that decision is recorded against a person. Worked example of why that matters: an automated system that skipped the stragglers after a timeout would silently break thirteen things, and nobody would know who chose that. A named exemption is a decision with an owner.

---

> - Bundle publisher, object storage, and CDN own bytes.
>   - Mechanism bought: content-addressed immutable artifacts that consumers verify offline.
>   - Cost paid: CDN TTL bounds propagation, so freshness lives in the manifest, not the artifact.

**What this says in plain words**
The actual bytes are distributed by boring infrastructure, and the agent verifies them itself so that infrastructure need not be trusted.

**The mechanism**

- **"Verify offline"** — the agent checks the artifact's hash against the signed manifest, with no call to the control plane.
- Worked example of the security property: a compromised CDN edge serves modified bundle bytes. The hash does not match the one in the signed manifest, the agent rejects it, and nothing bad is loaded. The CDN is treated as an untrusted delivery mechanism, which is exactly what a CDN should be.

**The cost, and the resolution**

- **"CDN TTL bounds propagation"** — a CDN caches, so a newly published artifact may take up to its TTL to reach every edge.
- **"So freshness lives in the manifest, not the artifact."** The resolution, and it is a clean piece of design.
  - Worked example: the artifact for hash `a1b2c3…` is cached for 24 hours at the edge, and that is fine, because those bytes will never change — the hash guarantees it. What must be fresh is the *manifest*, which says which hash to fetch. The manifest comes from the control plane with a short cache time, and the artifact comes from the CDN with a long one.
  - The general pattern: **put the mutable pointer somewhere fresh and the immutable content somewhere cheap.** It is the same reason bundle endpoints are immutable per version elsewhere in this group.

---

> - Consumer agent owns "load".
>   - Mechanism bought: pull, verify, hot-swap the TLS context, ack, emit counters
>     - The process never restarts.
>   - Cost paid: agent version skew is a compatibility surface
>     - The manifest schema is a frozen contract.

**What this says in plain words**
The agent does five things, and the fifth — reporting — is what makes the rest of the system able to reason about the fleet.

**The mechanism**

- Pull, verify, hot-swap, ack, emit counters. **"The process never restarts."**
- Worked example of why that matters at this scale: 200,000 targets restarting to pick up a certificate would drop every connection in the fleet simultaneously. Connection pools empty, upstreams see a synchronized surge of reconnections, and retries amplify it — an outage caused entirely by *how* the certificate was loaded, not by anything being wrong with it.

**The cost, which is specific to a fleet this size**

- **"Agent version skew is a compatibility surface."**
  - Worked example: 200,000 agents cannot be upgraded at once. At any moment the fleet runs three or four agent versions, some of them a year old on hosts nobody redeploys.
- **"The manifest schema is a frozen contract."**
  - Because old agents must keep parsing new manifests. Worked example of violating it: version 5 of the control plane renames a manifest field. Every agent older than version 5 fails to parse, refuses the deployment, and stops receiving certificate updates — silently, since they keep serving their last-known-good, until those certificates expire weeks later.
  - "Frozen" means additive changes only: new optional fields may appear, existing ones never change name, type, or meaning.

---

> - Adoption evidence store owns "proof".
>   - Mechanism bought: counters per `(target, issuer SKI, serial, minute)` answer "who still serves the old chain".
>   - Cost paid: a silent cohort blocks retirement, so targets that stop reporting must be enumerated.

**What this says in plain words**
A separate store holds the evidence that gates every risky transition. Its weakness is targets that say nothing.

**The mechanism**

- The key is `(target, issuer SKI, serial, minute)`. Adding **target** to the capacity section's key is what makes the store able to answer "*who* still serves the old chain" rather than only "*does anyone*".
- Worked example of the difference: a fleet-level count of old-chain handshakes tells you the rollover is not finished. A per-target breakdown tells you it is these seventeen hosts, in this availability domain, all running the same agent version — which is a diagnosis rather than a number.

**The cost, and the rule it forces**

- **"A silent cohort blocks retirement."** A target that reports nothing is indistinguishable, in the data, from a target that has adopted.
  - Worked example of the trap: 200,000 targets, 199,000 reporting zero old-chain handshakes, 1,000 reporting nothing at all. The dashboard shows zero old-chain traffic. Retiring the old CA breaks whichever of those 1,000 were still using it — and they were silent precisely because their agent was old, which correlates with not having adopted.
- **"Targets that stop reporting must be enumerated."** The fix, and it is a fail-safe default: silence is not evidence of adoption, it is an item on a list someone must resolve.
  - The general principle: **when a safety gate depends on a signal, decide what the absence of that signal means, and make the absence conservative.**

---

> → Scheduler selects a `CURRENT` version past `2/3` lifetime with no successor and enqueues `renew:{cert}:{version}`.
> → Worker locks the certificate row, re-checks that no successor exists, builds the CSR or waits for a customer CSR.
> → Worker calls Vault `Sign` on the CA key handle and inserts version `n+1` as `PENDING`.
> → Worker flips `n+1` to `CURRENT` and `n` to `PREVIOUS` in one transaction and publishes `CertificateRenewed`.
> → Orchestrator creates a deployment at generation `g+1` referencing version `n+1` and the current bundle version.
> → Agents poll, see the new ETag, fetch artifacts by hash, and pre-flight the chain against the deployed bundle.
> → Agents hot-swap the TLS context and ack `LOADED` with generation `g+1`; new handshakes report serial `n+1`.
> → Deployment reaches `CONVERGED` on acks; version `n` becomes `DEPRECATED` only after counters show serial `n` at zero.

**What this is**
One renewal traced from the scheduler's tick to the old version being retired. Eight steps. This is the narration to give when asked to walk through the flow.

**Step 1 — selection**

> Scheduler selects a `CURRENT` version past `2/3` lifetime with no successor and enqueues `renew:{cert}:{version}`.

- Three conditions: the version is `CURRENT`, it is past two thirds of its lifetime, and **it has no successor**.
- Worked example of why the third condition is there: a certificate renewed manually an hour ago already has version 8. Without the no-successor check, the sweep would still see version 7 as due — it is past two thirds and was `CURRENT` when the query ran — and queue a redundant renewal.
- The queue key `renew:{cert}:{version}` embeds both the certificate and the base version, which is the dedup identity from the component list.

**Step 2 — the worker takes ownership and re-checks**

> Worker locks the certificate row, re-checks that no successor exists, builds the CSR or waits for a customer CSR.

- The lock makes this the single writer for that certificate. The re-check happens *under* the lock, because the state may have changed between selection and processing.
- Worked example: the sweep selected at 09:00:00; a manual renewal committed at 09:00:02; the worker processes at 09:00:05 and finds a successor, so it stops. Without the re-check it would issue a duplicate.
- **"Builds the CSR or waits for a customer CSR"** — two modes. Either the service generates the key and request itself, or the certificate's owner supplies one.
  - Worked example of the second mode mattering: a customer holding their own private key must produce the CSR, because the service cannot. Automatic renewal for them means waiting for their submission, which is why the renewal path has to accommodate a step it does not control.

**Step 3 — signing**

> Worker calls Vault `Sign` on the CA key handle and inserts version `n+1` as `PENDING`.

- The signature happens against a key handle, and the result lands as a `PENDING` version — created but not yet in use.

**Step 4 — the flip**

> Worker flips `n+1` to `CURRENT` and `n` to `PREVIOUS` in one transaction and publishes `CertificateRenewed`.

- Both state changes in one transaction, so there is never an instant with two `CURRENT` versions or none.
- Note that `n` becomes `PREVIOUS`, not deleted. It stays valid, which is what lets unconverged agents keep working.

**Step 5 — the deployment is created**

> Orchestrator creates a deployment at generation `g+1` referencing version `n+1` and the current bundle version.

- The step that separates this design from a simpler one. Issuing a certificate and *deploying* it are two different acts, with a `deployment` row in between.
- The deployment references **two** things: the new certificate version and the **current bundle version**. Worked example of why both: an agent must know which certificate to load *and* which trust bundle to verify it against. Pinning both in one deployment means the pre-flight check in step 6 is checking the right pair, rather than a new certificate against whatever bundle the agent happened to have.
- **Generation `g+1`** is the fence. An agent that later receives a stale deployment at generation `g` will reject it, because generations only move forward.

**Step 6 — agents fetch and pre-flight**

> Agents poll, see the new ETag, fetch artifacts by hash, and pre-flight the chain against the deployed bundle.

- The ETag changed, so the poll returns content instead of `304`.
- **Pre-flight** is the safety check: verify the chain *before* loading. Worked example of the catastrophe it prevents: a leaf signed by a CA missing from the bundle. Loading it makes every subsequent handshake fail with `unknown issuer`. Pre-flighting means the agent refuses, acks `FAILED`, and keeps serving the certificate that works.

**Step 7 — load and acknowledge**

> Agents hot-swap the TLS context and ack `LOADED` with generation `g+1`; new handshakes report serial `n+1`.

- Hot-swap, no restart. The ack carries the generation, so the control plane knows exactly which deployment was loaded.
- "New handshakes report serial `n+1`" — the counters begin showing the new certificate, which is the evidence step 8 depends on.

**Step 8 — convergence, then retirement**

> Deployment reaches `CONVERGED` on acks; version `n` becomes `DEPRECATED` only after counters show serial `n` at zero.

- **Two separate gates, and the distinction is the most important idea in the flow.**
  - **`CONVERGED` on acks** means every target said "I loaded it". That is a claim about the deployment.
  - **`DEPRECATED` on counters at zero** means no handshake anywhere used the old serial. That is an observation about traffic.
- Worked example of why acks are not enough: an agent acks `LOADED` at 09:00, and a long-lived connection established at 08:00 is still using the old certificate at 14:00. Acks say the deployment succeeded; counters say the old certificate is still in use. Retiring on acks alone would break that connection's peer on reconnect.
- Worked example of the stronger case: an agent acks `LOADED` but a configuration error means it is still presenting the old chain on new handshakes. The ack is honest and wrong. Only the counters catch it.
- The general shape: **an acknowledgement is a claim about intent; a counter is evidence about behavior.** Deployment progress can be gated on the first. Anything irreversible — and deprecation is one-way — must be gated on the second.

## API and data model — IC3 baseline

Eight endpoints, six state tables, reference DDL, three things the DDL deliberately does not hold, and the service conventions.

---

> | Surface | Contract | Idempotency | Conflict/error behavior |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | `POST /v1/certificates/{id}/renewals` | `{baseVersion, csrPem?}` returns `202 {newVersion, operationId}` | `I` on `(certificate, baseVersion)` | `409 VERSION_SUPERSEDED` if a successor exists |
> | `POST /v1/trust-bundles/{id}/versions` | `{issuerRefs, reason}` returns `201 {bundleVersion, sha256}` | `I` | Contents immutable; identical contents return the existing version |
> | `POST /v1/certificate-deployments` | `{certificateVersion, trustBundleVersion, targetGroup, rolloutPolicy}` returns `202 {deploymentId, generation}` | `I` | `409 STALE_REFERENCE` if a version is deprecated, revoked, or expired |
> | `GET /v1/certificate-deployments/{id}` | `{state, desired, applied, failed, pending, silent}` | read | Convergence is computed from acks, not from the create call |
> | Agent `GET /v1/certificate-assignments/{target}` | `If-None-Match` returns `200` signed manifest or `304` | read | Agent identity maps to one target; public artifacts plus a key-delivery reference only |
> | Agent `PUT /v1/certificate-deployments/{id}/acks/{target}` | Generation, assignment hash, sequence, load state, health | Replay of an old sequence is a no-op | Reject any generation/hash mismatch; health may later degrade |
> | `POST /v1/certificate-deployments/{id}/rollbacks` | `{targetVersion, reason}` returns `202 {generation}` | `I` | `409 UNRECOVERABLE` if the target version is revoked, expired, or its issuer retired |
> | `POST /v1/ca-rollovers/{id}:advance` | `{expectedPhase}` returns `202` | compare-and-set on phase | `409 GATE_NOT_MET` with the failing gate and the silent-target list |

**What this says in plain words**
Eight surfaces split across three audiences: operators create things, agents fetch and acknowledge, and the rollover has its own advance button. `I` in the third column means "idempotent".

**Row by row**

- **`POST /v1/certificates/{id}/renewals` — renew a certificate.**
  - `{baseVersion, csrPem?}` — the caller says which version they are renewing *from*, and optionally supplies a CSR.
  - `I` on `(certificate, baseVersion)` — the dedup identity. Retrying the same renewal produces the same result rather than a second certificate.
  - **`409 VERSION_SUPERSEDED` if a successor exists.** Worked example: the scheduler queued a renewal from version 7 at 09:00; an operator renewed manually at 09:01 producing version 8; the queued job arrives at 09:02 and is rejected. Correctly — the renewal it wanted has already effectively happened, and proceeding would create a redundant version 9.
  - The error name is informative rather than generic, which matters because the caller's correct response differs by cause: `VERSION_SUPERSEDED` means "nothing to do", not "retry".
- **`POST /v1/trust-bundles/{id}/versions` — publish a new trust bundle.**
  - `{issuerRefs, reason}` — which CA certificates go in it, and why. Returns `201` with the version and its SHA-256.
  - **"Contents immutable; identical contents return the existing version."** Worked example of why that last rule is right: an operator runs the create-bundle command twice with the same list. Without the rule, two bundle versions exist with identical bytes, and the fleet is now split across two version numbers that mean the same thing — which makes every convergence count meaningless. With it, the second call returns version 4 again.
  - `reason` is stored, not just accepted. Worked example of its value: eight months later, someone asks why CA `X` is in the bundle. The answer is in the row.
- **`POST /v1/certificate-deployments` — deploy a version to a group.**
  - Four inputs: which certificate version, which trust bundle version, which target group, and the rollout policy.
  - The pairing of certificate *and* bundle in one deployment is the design point from the architecture section: agents verify one against the other, so both must be pinned together.
  - **`409 STALE_REFERENCE` if a version is deprecated, revoked, or expired.** Worked example: an automation script holds a certificate version reference from last month and creates a deployment for it. That version was deprecated two weeks ago. Accepting the deployment would push a dead certificate to the fleet; rejecting it at create time catches the mistake before any agent sees it.
- **`GET /v1/certificate-deployments/{id}` — how is it going?**
  - Returns six numbers: `state, desired, applied, failed, pending, silent`.
  - **`silent` is the field to notice.** It is separate from `pending`, and the distinction is the whole safety model.
    - **`pending`** — the target has not finished yet, but it is talking to us.
    - **`silent`** — the target has said nothing for 24 hours. It may have adopted, may have failed, may not exist any more.
    - Worked example of collapsing them: if silent targets counted as pending, an operator watching the number fall toward zero would see it stall and assume slow progress. If they counted as applied, the deployment would report success while an unknown number of targets were in an unknown state. Separating them makes "we do not know about these 47" a visible fact.
  - **"Convergence is computed from acks, not from the create call."** The create call returns a deployment id, not a result. Whether it worked is a separate question answered by evidence.
- **Agent `GET /v1/certificate-assignments/{target}` — what should I be running?**
  - `If-None-Match` gives `200` with a signed manifest or `304`. This is the 55-requests-per-second steady state from the capacity section.
  - **"Agent identity maps to one target"** — an agent can only ever ask about itself. Worked example of the isolation this gives: a compromised agent cannot enumerate the fleet's assignments, because its credential resolves to exactly one target id and the path parameter is checked against it.
  - **"Public artifacts plus a key-delivery reference only."** The manifest contains public material and a *reference* to where a private key can be collected, never the key itself. Worked example of why: the assignment endpoint is polled by 200,000 agents every hour and its responses are cached by ETag. Putting private keys in a cacheable, high-volume response is exactly the wrong place for them.
- **Agent `PUT .../acks/{target}` — here is my status.**
  - Five fields: generation, assignment hash, sequence, load state, health.
  - **`sequence`** makes replays harmless. Worked example: an agent's ack for sequence 5 is delayed by the network and arrives after its ack for sequence 6. Without a sequence number, the stale message would overwrite newer state with older state. With it, the older sequence is a no-op.
  - **"Reject any generation/hash mismatch."** Worked example: an agent acks generation `g` after the fleet has moved to `g+1`. That ack describes work on a superseded deployment and must not count toward the current one's convergence.
  - **"Health may later degrade."** The subtle clause. An ack is not final — a target that reported `SERVING` at 09:00 can report unhealthy at 11:00. Worked example of why this matters: a certificate loads and works, then an hour later handshakes start failing because of a chain issue that only appears with certain clients. A one-shot ack model would have recorded success and stopped listening.
- **`POST .../rollbacks` — go back.**
  - Returns `202` with a **generation**, which is the crucial detail: a rollback is a *forward* deployment of older content, not an undo.
  - Worked example of why it must be: generations only increase. If rollback decremented the generation, agents that had already moved to `g+1` would reject the rollback at `g` as stale — the fence would block the very operation meant to save you. Issuing the old version at `g+2` means every agent accepts it.
  - **`409 UNRECOVERABLE` if the target version is revoked, expired, or its issuer retired.** The honest error. Worked example: you rolled forward, retired the old CA, and now want to roll back. The old certificate's issuer is gone from every trust bundle, so deploying it would break everything. `UNRECOVERABLE` says so at the API instead of letting you discover it across the fleet.
  - That error is also the reason the rollover state machine retires trust *last*: retiring it is the step after which rollback stops being possible.
- **`POST /v1/ca-rollovers/{id}:advance` — move to the next phase.**
  - `{expectedPhase}` with **compare-and-set on phase**. The caller says which phase they believe the rollover is in, and the advance applies only if that is still true.
  - Worked example: two operators both decide the rollover is ready to advance from `VERIFY_TRUST`. The first succeeds and the phase becomes `SWITCH_LEAVES`. The second's compare-and-set fails, because the expected phase no longer matches. Without it, the second call would advance again, skipping a phase entirely.
  - **`409 GATE_NOT_MET` with the failing gate and the silent-target list.** The best error in the table.
    - It does not merely refuse; it says *which* gate failed and *which* targets are silent.
    - Worked example of the difference: "gate not met" alone sends an operator to query three systems. "Gate `VERIFY_SERVING` failed; 47 targets silent: [list]" is the diagnosis and the work item in the error response.
    - The general principle: **an error that blocks an action should carry everything needed to unblock it.**

---

> | State | Key | Fields/invariant |
> |---|---|---|
> | Certificate version | `(tenant_id, cert_id, version_num)` | `PENDING`, `CURRENT`, `PREVIOUS`, `DEPRECATED`, `REVOKED`, `FAILED`; serial, chain, validity; one `CURRENT` |
> | Trust bundle version | `(tenant_id, bundle_id, bundle_version)` | `manifest_ref`, `sha256`, `created_at`; contents immutable |
> | Deployment | `(tenant_id, deployment_id)` | Version refs, `target_group`, `generation`, `state`, `policy`; generation strictly increases per group |
> | Target ack | `(tenant_id, deployment_id, target_id)` | `generation`, `state`, `loaded_version_ref`, `observed_at`, `error_code` |
> | Rollover | `(tenant_id, ca_id, rollover_id)` | `phase`, old and new CA versions, bundle before/with-both/after, exemptions with owner and expiry |
> | Adoption evidence | `(target_id, issuer_ski, leaf_serial, minute)` | Handshake count; retained `30 d`; rolled up per cohort |

**What this says in plain words**
Six kinds of state. The structural observation is that three of them — deployment, ack, and adoption evidence — exist only because this design cares about *delivery*, not just about certificates.

- **Certificate version.** Six stages and one invariant: exactly one `CURRENT` per certificate.
- **Trust bundle version.** Immutable contents, addressed by hash.
- **Deployment.** "Generation strictly increases per group" is the fencing invariant, stated as a property of this table.
- **Target ack.** `observed_at` is what makes silence detectable — a row whose timestamp is old is a target that stopped talking.
- **Rollover.** Three bundle references: **before**, **with-both**, and **after**.
  - Worked example of why all three: a rollover moves the fleet through three trust states — trusting only the old CA, trusting both, then trusting only the new one. Recording all three version numbers means the rollover knows exactly which bundle each phase expects, and a rollback to an earlier phase has a concrete bundle to return to.
  - **"Exemptions with owner and expiry."** The human-owned exemptions from the architecture section, made structural. An exemption is not a flag — it names a person and a date.
  - Worked example of why expiry matters: an exemption granted during a rollover with no expiry becomes permanent by accident. With one, it lapses and reappears as a gate failure, forcing the question to be asked again.
- **Adoption evidence.** Not relational — a counter series with 30-day retention.

> - Reference DDL
>   - Keys and uniqueness match the state table above and the [companion schema](../../1sde/1.backend/2.rest102.md#pki-2).
>   - Adoption evidence counters live in the metrics store keyed by `(target_id, issuer_ski, leaf_serial, minute)`; only versions, bundles, groups, deployments, acks, and rollovers are relational.

**What this says in plain words**
Six tables are in SQL. The seventh kind of state lives somewhere else on purpose.

**Why adoption evidence is not relational**

- Worked example of the volume: 3,300 datapoints per second, retained for 30 days, is about 8.5 billion rows. That is a time-series workload, and a relational database is the wrong tool for it.
- Worked example of the access pattern: the only question ever asked is "what is the count for this issuer and serial over the last hour, grouped by target?" — a range aggregation over time, which is exactly what a metrics store is built for and what a relational store does badly at this size.
- The consequence, stated explicitly later: the `VERIFY_SERVING` gate *reads* the metrics store rather than joining in SQL. The gate crosses a system boundary, and saying so is more honest than pretending one query answers everything.

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE certificate_versions (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, cert_id text NOT NULL, version_num bigint NOT NULL CHECK (version_num >= 1),
>   stage text NOT NULL
>     CHECK (stage IN ('PENDING','CURRENT','PREVIOUS','DEPRECATED','REVOKED','FAILED')),
>   serial text, issuer_ca_version_ref text NOT NULL,
>   chain_ref text,                           -- chain artifact by hash in object storage
>   not_before timestamptz, not_after timestamptz,
>   key_ref text,                             -- Vault/HSM handle, never key material
>   created_at timestamptz NOT NULL, version bigint NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, cert_id, version_num),   -- successor is version_num + 1, so one renewal per base version
>   CHECK (not_before IS NULL OR not_after > not_before)
> );
> CREATE UNIQUE INDEX certificate_versions_one_current ON certificate_versions (tenant_id, cert_id)
>   WHERE stage = 'CURRENT';
> CREATE UNIQUE INDEX certificate_versions_serial ON certificate_versions (issuer_ca_version_ref, serial)
>   WHERE serial IS NOT NULL;
> CREATE INDEX certificate_versions_expiring ON certificate_versions (not_after) WHERE stage = 'CURRENT';
> ```

**The five things worth stopping on**

1. **`PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, cert_id, version_num)` with the comment "successor is version_num + 1, so one renewal per base version".**
   - The comment explains a piece of design hidden inside a primary key. Because version numbers are consecutive integers, the successor of version 7 is *by definition* version 8, and the primary key makes version 8 unique.
   - Worked example of what that buys: two concurrent renewals from base version 7 both try to insert version 8. One succeeds; the other gets a primary-key violation. The idempotency guarantee is enforced by the key itself, with no extra table and no lock.
2. **`certificate_versions_one_current` — a unique partial index on `(tenant_id, cert_id) WHERE stage = 'CURRENT'`.**
   - A **partial index** covers only rows matching a condition. Unique and partial together means: at most one row per certificate may be `CURRENT`.
   - Worked example of the bug this makes impossible: a flip that sets version 8 to `CURRENT` without demoting version 7. The database rejects it. The invariant "one `CURRENT`" is not application discipline — it is a constraint that holds against every bug and every ad-hoc statement.
3. **`certificate_versions_serial` on `(issuer_ca_version_ref, serial)`.**
   - Serial uniqueness scoped by *issuer*, which is the correct X.509 scope. Worked example of why: two certificates from the same issuer sharing a serial means revoking one revokes both, because a CRL identifies certificates by serial and nothing else.
   - `WHERE serial IS NOT NULL` because a `PENDING` row has no serial yet.
4. **`certificate_versions_expiring` on `(not_after) WHERE stage = 'CURRENT'`.**
   - The index the renewal sweep uses. Partial, because superseded versions have expiry dates too and no sweep ever cares about them.
   - Worked example of the size difference: over three years a certificate accumulates roughly a dozen versions, one of which is `CURRENT`. The partial index is about one twelfth the size, and every entry is a row the sweep actually wants.
5. **`key_ref text, -- Vault/HSM handle, never key material`.** The custody rule, restated in a comment where a future maintainer will see it.

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE trust_bundle_versions (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, bundle_id text NOT NULL, bundle_version bigint NOT NULL CHECK (bundle_version >= 1),
>   manifest_ref text NOT NULL, sha256 text NOT NULL, created_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, bundle_id, bundle_version),
>   UNIQUE (tenant_id, bundle_id, sha256)     -- identical contents return the existing version
> );
> CREATE TABLE target_groups (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, target_group text NOT NULL,
>   current_generation bigint NOT NULL CHECK (current_generation >= 0),   -- bumped under this row's lock; never decrements
>   owner_epoch bigint NOT NULL,              -- orchestrator lease epoch; a resumed old owner fails the CAS
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, target_group)
> );
> ```

**`trust_bundle_versions`**

- `UNIQUE (tenant_id, bundle_id, sha256)` is the API table's "identical contents return the existing version" rule, enforced in the database rather than in code.

**`target_groups` — a two-column table doing two important jobs**

1. **`current_generation ... -- bumped under this row's lock; never decrements`.**
   - This row *is* the fence. Every deployment to this group reads and increments it under the row's lock, which is why generations are strictly increasing rather than merely usually increasing.
   - Worked example: two orchestrator instances create deployments for the same group at the same instant. Both lock the row in turn; one gets generation 12, the other 13. Without the shared row, both could pick 12 and agents would have two different deployments at the same generation with no way to order them.
2. **`owner_epoch bigint -- orchestrator lease epoch; a resumed old owner fails the CAS`.**
   - A **fencing token** for the orchestrator itself. Each time an orchestrator takes the lease, the epoch increments.
   - Worked example of the failure it prevents: orchestrator A holds the lease and stalls for two minutes on a garbage-collection pause. Its lease expires; orchestrator B takes over at epoch 8 and advances the rollover. A wakes up believing it is still in charge and tries to write. Its compare-and-set is against epoch 7, the row is at 8, and the write is refused.
   - Without this, a stalled orchestrator could advance a rollover phase that a newer orchestrator had already moved past — for example re-entering `SWITCH_LEAVES` after the fleet had moved to `VERIFY_SERVING`.

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE certificate_deployments (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, deployment_id text NOT NULL,
>   cert_id text NOT NULL, version_num bigint NOT NULL,
>   bundle_id text NOT NULL, bundle_version bigint NOT NULL,
>   target_group text NOT NULL, generation bigint NOT NULL,
>   assignment_hash text NOT NULL,            -- hash of the signed manifest agents must ack
>   state text NOT NULL CHECK (state IN ('ROLLING','PAUSED','CONVERGED','SUPERSEDED','FAILED')),
>   policy jsonb NOT NULL, created_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, deployment_id),
>   UNIQUE (tenant_id, target_group, generation),
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, cert_id, version_num) REFERENCES certificate_versions,
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, bundle_id, bundle_version) REFERENCES trust_bundle_versions,
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, target_group) REFERENCES target_groups
> );
> ```

**The four things worth stopping on**

1. **`UNIQUE (tenant_id, target_group, generation)`.** One deployment per group per generation, enforced. Combined with the never-decrementing counter in `target_groups`, generations form a total order over deployments to a group.
2. **`assignment_hash text -- hash of the signed manifest agents must ack`.**
   - The agent acknowledges a *hash*, not just a generation. Worked example of why both: the generation says *which deployment*; the hash says *exactly what content*. An agent that somehow fetched a stale or corrupted manifest would ack the wrong hash, and the mismatch is detected rather than counted as success.
3. **Five deployment states, and two of them are worth naming.**
   - **`PAUSED`** — a rollout deliberately stopped part-way. Worked example: 10% of targets are in `FAILED`, so an operator pauses rather than letting the rollout continue into the remaining 90%.
   - **`SUPERSEDED`** — a deployment overtaken by a newer generation. Not failed, not converged; simply no longer the current intent. Worked example: a rollback at `g+2` supersedes the deployment at `g+1` that was still rolling.
4. **Three foreign keys**, all composite and tenant-scoped. The deployment cannot reference a certificate version, bundle version, or target group that does not exist in the same tenant — enforced structurally rather than by validation code.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE certificate_target_acks (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, deployment_id text NOT NULL, target_id text NOT NULL,
>   generation bigint NOT NULL,
>   sequence bigint NOT NULL DEFAULT 0 CHECK (sequence >= 0),   -- replay of an older sequence is a no-op
>   state text NOT NULL CHECK (state IN ('ASSIGNED','DOWNLOADED','LOADED','SERVING','FAILED')),
>   loaded_version_ref text, health text, error_code text,
>   observed_at timestamptz NOT NULL,         -- no ack for 24 h makes the target silent, not done
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, deployment_id, target_id),
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, deployment_id) REFERENCES certificate_deployments
> );
> CREATE INDEX certificate_target_acks_rollout ON certificate_target_acks
>   (tenant_id, deployment_id, state, target_id);
> CREATE INDEX certificate_target_acks_silent ON certificate_target_acks (observed_at)
>   WHERE state NOT IN ('SERVING','FAILED');
> ```

**The four things worth stopping on**

1. **`PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, deployment_id, target_id)`** — one row per target per deployment, overwritten in place. The 40-megabyte figure from the capacity section, and why convergence is one indexed count.
2. **`sequence ... -- replay of an older sequence is a no-op`.** The out-of-order ack protection, as a column.
3. **`observed_at ... -- no ack for 24 h makes the target silent, not done`.**
   - The comment states the rule that the whole safety model rests on. Silence is its own category.
   - Worked example one more time, because it is the failure this design is built around: 1,000 of 200,000 targets stop reporting. Counting them as done means retiring an old CA that some of them still depend on. Counting them as silent means a gate fails and names them.
4. **The two indexes serve the two questions asked of this table.**
   - `certificate_target_acks_rollout` on `(tenant_id, deployment_id, state, target_id)` answers "how many targets of this deployment are in each state?" — the rollout dashboard.
   - `certificate_target_acks_silent` on `(observed_at) WHERE state NOT IN ('SERVING','FAILED')` answers "who has gone quiet?"
     - The partial condition is precise: a target in `SERVING` is done and a target in `FAILED` is already a known problem. Neither can be silent in the sense that matters. Only targets in an *intermediate* state can go quiet, and the index covers exactly those.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE ca_rollovers (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, ca_id text NOT NULL, rollover_id text NOT NULL,
>   phase text NOT NULL CHECK (phase IN
>     ('PUBLISH_TRUST','VERIFY_TRUST','SWITCH_LEAVES','VERIFY_SERVING','RETIRE_OLD_TRUST','DONE')),
>   old_ca_version_ref text NOT NULL, new_ca_version_ref text NOT NULL,
>   bundle_id text NOT NULL,
>   bundle_before bigint NOT NULL, bundle_with_both bigint, bundle_after bigint,
>   exemptions jsonb NOT NULL DEFAULT '[]',   -- [{target_id, owner, expires_at}]
>   gate_results jsonb NOT NULL DEFAULT '{}', -- persisted per phase so advance is auditable
>   owner_epoch bigint NOT NULL, version bigint NOT NULL, updated_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, ca_id, rollover_id),
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, bundle_id, bundle_before) REFERENCES trust_bundle_versions
> );
> CREATE UNIQUE INDEX ca_rollovers_one_active ON ca_rollovers (tenant_id, ca_id) WHERE phase <> 'DONE';
> ```

**The six phases, read as a sentence**
`PUBLISH_TRUST → VERIFY_TRUST → SWITCH_LEAVES → VERIFY_SERVING → RETIRE_OLD_TRUST → DONE`

- Publish the new trust, verify it arrived, switch the leaves, verify they are serving, retire the old trust, done.
- The shape to notice: **every action phase is followed by a verification phase.** Publish then verify; switch then verify. Nothing irreversible happens without an intervening check.
- And **retiring old trust is last**, which is the ordering that keeps rollback possible for as long as possible.

**The other things worth stopping on**

1. **`bundle_before NOT NULL, bundle_with_both, bundle_after`** — the first is required at creation; the other two are nullable because they do not exist yet when the rollover starts. The row grows as the rollover progresses.
2. **`exemptions jsonb -- [{target_id, owner, expires_at}]`.** Three fields per exemption: which target, who decided, and when the decision lapses. An exemption without an owner is an anonymous decision; without an expiry it is a permanent one.
3. **`gate_results jsonb -- persisted per phase so advance is auditable`.**
   - Every gate evaluation is stored, not just its verdict.
   - Worked example of why: three months later, someone asks why the rollover advanced past `VERIFY_SERVING` on 14 March when target `X` broke on 15 March. With persisted gate results, the answer is the recorded evidence — what the counters said, which targets were exempted, and by whom. Without it, the answer is a guess.
4. **`owner_epoch` and `version`** — a lease epoch and an optimistic lock, the same fencing pattern as `target_groups`.
5. **`ca_rollovers_one_active ON (tenant_id, ca_id) WHERE phase <> 'DONE'`.**
   - At most one active rollover per CA, enforced by a unique partial index.
   - Worked example of what it prevents: two rollovers of the same CA started a week apart, each moving the fleet toward a different new CA. The bundle contents, the leaf re-issuance, and the retirement steps would interleave into something with no coherent end state. The constraint makes it impossible rather than merely discouraged.

---

> - Not captured by the DDL
>   - Adoption evidence is a per-minute counter series with `30 d` retention and per-cohort rollups; it stays in the metrics store and is read by the `VERIFY_SERVING` gate, not joined in SQL.
>   - Private keys, the manifest signing key, and certificate or chain bytes live in Vault, the HSM, and object storage behind `key_ref`, `chain_ref`, and `manifest_ref`.
>   - Deviation from the companion: `certificate_versions`, `target_groups`, and `ca_rollovers` are added because the state table and the fencing deep dive name them; `certificate_deployments` references the version row by key instead of a free `certificate_version_ref`, gains `assignment_hash`, and `certificate_target_acks` gains `sequence` and `health`.

**What this says in plain words**
Three notes about what is deliberately absent from the schema.

1. **Adoption evidence is in the metrics store.** Restated here because the `VERIFY_SERVING` gate crosses that boundary, which is a fact about the system's shape rather than a schema detail.
2. **No secret or large bytes are in the database.** Three reference columns — `key_ref`, `chain_ref`, `manifest_ref` — each pointing at the system that actually holds the thing.
   - Worked example of the property: a full dump of this database yields references, not material. The private keys are in Vault, the certificate bytes are in object storage, and the references are useless without access to those.
3. **The deviation list.** Every difference from the shared API companion schema, enumerated.
   - Worked example of why writing this out matters: a reader comparing the two schemas will find discrepancies. Without this note they cannot tell a deliberate divergence from a mistake. With it, each difference has a stated reason — these three tables were added because the deep dive needs them; `assignment_hash`, `sequence`, and `health` were added because the ack protocol needs them.

---

> - Proposed service conventions
>   - Manifest: `{generation, certificateVersionRef, chainHash, trustBundleRef, notBefore, notAfter, signature}`, signed by a service key in Vault that is not a CA key
>     - Agents pin its public key at install.
>   - The reference DDL above adapts the [API companion](../../1sde/1.backend/2.rest102.md#pki-2) tables for bundles, deployments, and acks; the deviation bullet lists every difference.
>   - Documented OCI behavior: a CA bundle holds root and intermediate certificates in PEM, may include CAs not managed by the service, and a chosen version is marked current.

**The manifest, field by field**

- `generation` — the fence.
- `certificateVersionRef` — which certificate version.
- `chainHash` — the expected hash of the chain artifact, which is what makes CDN delivery verifiable.
- `trustBundleRef` — which bundle to verify against.
- `notBefore` / `notAfter` — the validity window, so an agent can sanity-check before loading.
- `signature` — over all of it.

**"Signed by a service key in Vault that is not a CA key"**

- The separation to understand. The manifest is signed to prove it came from the control plane. That is a *different* job from signing certificates, and it uses a different key.
- Worked example of why they must be separate: if the CA key signed manifests, then anything that could induce a manifest signature would have the CA key operating on attacker-influenced input, and a compromise of the manifest-signing path would be a compromise of the CA itself. Separate keys mean a manifest-signing compromise lets an attacker publish bogus assignments — bad, and recoverable — rather than mint certificates.
- **"Agents pin its public key at install."** The agent knows the manifest signing key from the moment it is installed, so it never has to fetch and trust one at runtime. Worked example of what pinning prevents: an attacker who can intercept the agent's first fetch cannot substitute their own signing key, because the agent is not looking for one.

**Documented OCI behavior**

- A CA bundle holds roots and intermediates in PEM, **may include CAs not managed by the service**, and a chosen version is marked current.
- The middle clause is the useful one. Worked example: a fleet must trust both this service's CAs and a partner organization's CA. The bundle can hold both, which means the bundle is the *fleet's complete trust policy*, not merely a list of what this service issued.

---

> - Version stages are documented in the [OCI SDK model](https://docs.oracle.com/en-us/iaas/tools/java/latest/com/oracle/bmc/certificatesmanagement/model/VersionStage.html).
>   - The deployment states and gates in this answer remain proposed behavior.

**What this says in plain words**
The certificate version stages are documented platform behavior. The deployment states, the ack protocol, and the rollover gates are this design's own invention.

**Why the line is drawn explicitly**
So a reader — or an interviewer — can tell which claims rest on documentation and which are proposals. Worked example of why it matters: asserting that the platform has a `CONVERGED` deployment state would be wrong and checkable. Saying "the stages are documented; the deployment machinery is what I am proposing" is accurate and makes the proposal easier to discuss on its merits.

## Renewal and deployment fencing — IC3 baseline

Why two thirds, three layers of idempotency, the two key-custody modes, generation fencing, the rollout policy, the agent pre-flight, and the acknowledgement procedure.

---

> - Why `2/3` lifetime
>   - A `90 d` leaf renews on day `60`, leaving `30 d` to detect and fix a consumer that did not switch.
>   - Thirty days is the nominal margin at the planned renewal instant.
>   - The actual outage budget is the minimum remaining validity across active leaves, chains, and required status evidence.
>   - Retries back off until day `75`
>   - Then the on-call is paged with `15 d` of margin.
>   - `±5%` jitter stops a fleet issued on one day from renewing in lockstep against the HSM.

**What this says in plain words**
Renew at day 60 of 90. The 30 days that remain are the incident-response budget, and the third bullet corrects a comfortable misreading of it.

**The escalation timeline, which is the useful part**

- **Day 60** — renewal is attempted. 30 days of margin remain.
- **Days 60 to 75** — retries with backoff. Nobody is woken up; the system is trying.
- **Day 75** — on-call is paged, with 15 days still on the clock.
- Worked example of why paging at 75 rather than 88: 15 days survives a holiday, a person on leave, and a dependency that needs another team to fix. Paging at day 88 gives two days, which survives nothing.

**The correction in bullet three, which is the real content**

> The actual outage budget is the minimum remaining validity across active leaves, chains, and required status evidence.

- The comfortable reading is "we have 30 days". The accurate reading is "we have however long the *nearest* thing has, across three different kinds of material".
- **Three kinds, each of which can be the binding one:**
  1. **Leaves** — the certificates themselves.
  2. **Chains** — the intermediate CA certificates. Worked example: a leaf valid for another 40 days chained to an intermediate that expires in 9. The chain fails in 9 days, and no leaf renewal fixes it.
  3. **Required status evidence** — a CRL or stapled OCSP response, for verifiers that demand freshness. Worked example: a strict verifier with a 24-hour freshness requirement stops accepting a perfectly valid certificate when its revocation evidence goes stale, regardless of the certificate's own expiry.
- Worked example of the whole point: a dashboard showing "minimum leaf validity: 30 days" looks healthy while an intermediate expires next Tuesday. The budget is the minimum across all three, and measuring only leaves measures the wrong thing.

**`±5%` jitter**

- Worked example of what it prevents: 1,000,000 certificates issued during one migration weekend all reach day 60 on the same day. Without jitter that is 1,000,000 renewals in 24 hours instead of the steady 16,700 — about 12 per second against an HSM shared with everything else. With ±5% of 90 days, renewals spread across a 9-day window and the rate stays flat.
- Same reasoning as jitter in retry backoff: independent clients that agree on a schedule stop being independent.

---

> - Three layers of idempotency
>   - The scheduler predicate selects only `CURRENT` versions with no `version_num + 1` row.
>   - The queue deduplicates on `renew:{cert_id}:{version_num}`.
>   - The worker locks the certificate row and re-checks the successor before signing.
>   - Durable unique renewal intent and fenced publication provide correctness.
>   - Scheduler filtering and queue deduplication only reduce redundant work.

**What this says in plain words**
Three defences against issuing the same renewal twice — and the last two bullets say which of them you are actually allowed to rely on.

**The three layers**

1. **The scheduler predicate.** Only select `CURRENT` versions that have no `version_num + 1` row. Cheapest filter, applied furthest out.
2. **Queue deduplication** on `renew:{cert_id}:{version_num}`. Suppresses duplicate messages.
3. **The worker's lock and re-check.** Under the certificate row lock, confirm the successor still does not exist, then sign.

**The distinction that matters**

> Durable unique renewal intent and fenced publication provide correctness.
> Scheduler filtering and queue deduplication only reduce redundant work.

- Layers 1 and 2 are **optimizations**. Layer 3, plus the primary key on `(tenant_id, cert_id, version_num)`, is the **guarantee**.
- Worked example of why the queue cannot be the boundary: queue deduplication is time-bounded and best-effort. A 5-minute dedup window suppresses a duplicate at minute 3 and passes one at minute 7. It also does nothing across a queue failover or an acknowledgement-timeout redelivery.
- Worked example of the guarantee holding anyway: two messages for `renew:cert-42:7` both survive to workers. Both take the certificate row lock in turn. The first re-checks, finds no version 8, signs, and inserts version 8. The second acquires the lock, re-checks, finds version 8, and stops. Even if it did not stop, the primary key would reject its insert.
- The general principle, worth saying out loud: **name which layer is the correctness boundary.** A design with three defences and no stated boundary is a design where nobody knows which one may be removed safely.

---

> - Managed versus customer-held key
>   - Managed: the worker generates a new key pair in the tenant's Vault key or at the agent
>     - A certificate renewal is also a key rotation.
>   - Customer-held: the service cannot recreate the key from a certificate
>     - Renewal waits in `AWAITING_CSR`, notifies the owner, and pages at day `75`.
>   - Persist PENDING with serial, issuer key version, and exact TBS before signing, as in s2.01.
>   - Resume that intent after a crash rather than allocate an untracked replacement.

**What this says in plain words**
Two custody modes with genuinely different renewal behavior, and the second cannot be fully automated.

**Managed keys**

- The service generates a new key pair on renewal, so **"a certificate renewal is also a key rotation."**
- Worked example of why that is a benefit worth naming: a key that has been in use for a year has had a year of exposure — memory dumps, backups, a host that was once compromised. Rotating the key on every renewal means no key lives longer than one certificate lifetime, and it happens automatically as a side effect of a process that already runs.
- Worked example of the cost: the new key must reach wherever the certificate is used, which is why this mode needs either a Vault-held key or generation at the agent itself.

**Customer-held keys**

- **"The service cannot recreate the key from a certificate."** A certificate contains the *public* key. Nothing in it permits reconstructing the private key — that is the entire basis of public-key cryptography.
- So renewal cannot proceed without the customer producing a new CSR. The state is **`AWAITING_CSR`**: not failed, not in progress, waiting on someone else.
- Worked example of why a distinct state is required: if a customer-held renewal simply sat in the ordinary in-progress state, it would look like a slow renewal and retries would accomplish nothing. `AWAITING_CSR` says the blocker is external, which changes what the system does — it notifies the owner rather than retrying, and it pages at day 75 like any other stalled renewal.
- The consequence to state in an interview: **automatic renewal is only as automatic as the key custody allows.** "Everything renews automatically" is not true for customer-held keys, and claiming it invites the counterexample.

**The two lines borrowed from s2.01**

- **"Persist PENDING with serial, issuer key version, and exact TBS before signing."** The issuance intent is written down before any signature exists.
- **"Resume that intent after a crash rather than allocate an untracked replacement."**
  - Worked example of what allocating a replacement would do: the worker crashes after the HSM signs but before the database records it. If the retry allocates a *new* serial, the first signature's certificate is valid, in the world, and has a serial nobody recorded — which means it can never be revoked, because revocation identifies a certificate by its serial. Resuming the same intent means every signature that can exist corresponds to a row you can find.

---

> - Generation fencing
>   - Each target group has a monotonically increasing `generation`, bumped under the group row lock when a deployment is created.
>   - The agent ignores a manifest with a lower generation than it applied, which covers a stale CDN or lagging replica.
>   - The server accepts an acknowledgment only for the exact current generation and assigned content hash.
>   - Future generations and unassigned targets are rejected too.
>   - Rollback is a new, higher generation pointing at an older version
>   - A generation never decrements.
>   - Exact failure prevented: a resumed old orchestrator must pass both expected generation and current owner epoch before allocating a new deployment.
>   - A row lock alone serializes writers but does not identify a stale owner.

**What this says in plain words**
One increasing number per target group, checked in both directions — by the agent when receiving, and by the server when acknowledging.

**Why a fence is needed at all**
Because in a distributed system, old messages arrive late. A stale manifest from a CDN edge, a read from a lagging database replica, a resumed process acting on a two-minute-old view of the world — all of these deliver instructions that *were* correct and no longer are.

**The four checks**

1. **The agent ignores a lower generation than it applied.**
   - Worked example: the agent applied generation 12. A CDN edge still holds the manifest for generation 11 and serves it on the next poll. Without the check, the agent would downgrade itself to an older certificate. With it, the stale manifest is discarded and the agent keeps generation 12.
   - This also covers a lagging read replica serving an old assignment.
2. **The server accepts an ack only for the exact current generation and content hash.**
   - Both, because they answer different questions. The generation says *which deployment*; the hash says *exactly what content*.
   - Worked example: an agent acks generation 12 with a hash that does not match what generation 12 assigned. Something fetched the wrong artifact. Counting that ack toward convergence would record success for content nobody deployed.
3. **Future generations and unassigned targets are rejected too.**
   - **Future** generations: an ack for generation 13 when the current is 12 is impossible under correct operation, so it indicates a bug or a forged message.
   - **Unassigned targets**: a target acking a deployment it was never assigned. Worked example of the attack this blocks: a compromised agent floods acks for targets across the fleet, making an unconverged deployment look converged and causing an old CA to be retired early. Rejecting acks from targets not in the assignment makes that impossible.
4. **Rollback is a new, higher generation pointing at an older version; a generation never decrements.**
   - The consequence of check 1. Worked example of why decrementing would be fatal: half the fleet is on generation 12 and the deployment is bad. A rollback issued *as* generation 11 would be ignored by exactly those agents that need it, because they already applied 12. Issuing the previous content at generation 13 means everyone accepts it.
   - The mental model: there is no "undo". There is only "deploy something else, later".

**The last two bullets, which are the sharpest part**

> Exact failure prevented: a resumed old orchestrator must pass both expected generation and current owner epoch before allocating a new deployment.
> A row lock alone serializes writers but does not identify a stale owner.

- The second sentence is the insight, and it is a general one about locks.
- Worked example of the failure a lock does not prevent:
  1. Orchestrator A holds the lease and is creating a deployment for group `G`.
  2. A stalls for two minutes — a garbage-collection pause, a hypervisor freeze, a network partition.
  3. A's lease expires. Orchestrator B takes over at owner epoch 8, creates deployment generation 13, and the fleet starts adopting it.
  4. A wakes up. From A's point of view nothing happened; it is still mid-operation. It takes the group row lock — successfully, because B is not holding it — and creates a deployment.
  - The lock was acquired correctly. The write is serialized correctly. And it is still wrong, because A is acting on a view of the world that is two minutes old and has been superseded.
- **A lock answers "is anyone else writing right now?" It does not answer "am I still the one who should be writing?"** Only a fencing token — here the `owner_epoch` — answers the second question.
- With the epoch check, A's compare-and-set is against epoch 7, the row holds 8, and the write is refused. A discovers it is no longer the owner and stops.
- This is the single most transferable idea in the section: **whenever a lease can expire while its holder is still running, a lock is insufficient and a monotonic owner token is required.**

---

> - Rollout policy
>   - Canary one target, then `10%`, `50%`, `100%` of the group.
>   - Each step waits for `LOADED` acks and an unchanged handshake error rate for `10 min`.
>   - Abort creates a rollback generation automatically
>   - The `PREVIOUS` version is still valid, so the fallback exists by construction.

**What this says in plain words**
Four waves, each gated on two signals, with automatic rollback.

**The wave sizes, and why they are shaped that way**

- One target, then 10%, then 50%, then 100%. Each step is roughly an order of magnitude larger than the last.
- Worked example of the first step's value: a certificate with a mismatched private key fails on the very first target. One target is broken, for 10 minutes, and the rollout stops. The same fault deployed fleet-wide breaks 200,000 targets.
- Worked example of why 10% before 50%: some faults only appear at scale or in specific environments. A 10% wave spanning several availability domains and client cohorts exercises variety that one canary cannot.

**The two gates per step**

1. **`LOADED` acks** — targets confirm they installed it.
2. **An unchanged handshake error rate for 10 minutes** — traffic confirms it works.
- Worked example of why both: an agent can honestly ack `LOADED` and still serve a chain that certain clients reject. The ack is a claim about the agent; the error rate is evidence about clients. Only the second catches a chain that is well-formed but incompatible with some verifier in the fleet.
- Ten minutes is chosen to be long enough to accumulate signal on a low-traffic target and short enough that a four-wave rollout completes in under an hour.

**Automatic abort, and the sentence that makes it safe**

> The `PREVIOUS` version is still valid, so the fallback exists by construction.

- Rollback is not a recovery procedure that might fail. The previous certificate has not been revoked and has not expired — it is sitting there, valid, because that is what `PREVIOUS` means.
- Worked example of the alternative: a design that deprecated the old version as soon as the new one was deployed would have nothing to roll back to. The abort path would have to *issue* something, during an incident, through the same pipeline that just failed.
- "By construction" is the phrase to use: the fallback exists as a structural property of the version model, not as a thing someone remembered to arrange.

---

> - Agent pre-flight
>   - Before loading, the agent builds the path from the new leaf to an anchor in the bundle version named by the manifest.
>   - A failure acks `FAILED` with `CHAIN_UNTRUSTED` and keeps serving the old version.
>   - This is the local enforcement of trust-before-leaves
>   - It turns a fleet outage into one failed ack.

**What this says in plain words**
Before loading anything, the agent checks that the new certificate actually chains to a CA it trusts. If it does not, the agent refuses and keeps working.

**What "builds the path" means**
Path building is the process a TLS verifier goes through: start at the leaf, find the certificate that signed it, find what signed *that*, and continue until reaching a trust anchor in the bundle. The agent does exactly what a client would do, in advance.

**Why this is the most valuable four lines in the section**

> It turns a fleet outage into one failed ack.

- Worked example of the failure without pre-flight: a new leaf is signed by a CA that has not yet been distributed to the trust bundles. The deployment goes to 200,000 targets. Every one loads it. Every subsequent handshake, everywhere, fails with `unknown issuer`. The rollout dashboard shows 100% `LOADED` — the agents did exactly what they were told — while the fleet is down.
- Worked example with pre-flight: the first canary target builds the path, cannot reach an anchor, acks `FAILED` with `CHAIN_UNTRUSTED`, and keeps serving its old certificate. The rollout halts at one target. Nothing broke.
- **"Local enforcement of trust-before-leaves."** The rollover state machine orders things so that trust is published before leaves switch. The pre-flight is the same rule enforced *at each target*, independently — so that even if the orchestration is wrong, the individual agent refuses.
- The general pattern: **an ordering invariant enforced only by the orchestrator is one bug away from a fleet outage; the same invariant re-checked locally by each actor fails safe.**
- And the error code is specific. `CHAIN_UNTRUSTED` tells the operator exactly what is wrong — the bundle does not contain the issuer — rather than a generic failure that requires investigation.

---

> - Acknowledgment flow
>   - Validate the target assignment before counting application state.
>   - Track health independently so a loaded target can later report failure.

**What this says in plain words**
Two rules: check the ack is legitimate before it counts, and keep listening after it succeeds.

- **"Validate before counting"** — the generation, hash, and assignment checks all run before the observation affects any rollout count.
- **"Track health independently so a loaded target can later report failure."**
  - Worked example: a target acks `LOADED` at 09:00, and at 09:40 a particular client cohort starts failing handshakes against it. A one-shot ack model recorded success at 09:00 and stopped caring. An independent health field lets the target report degradation, which can pause the rollout before the next wave.

---

> ```text
> ACK(deployment, target, generation, assignment_hash, sequence, load_state, health):
>     transaction:
>         lock deployment and target observation
>         require authenticated target belongs to deployment
>         require generation == current assigned generation
>         require assignment_hash == target's current assignment hash
>         if sequence <= last_observation_sequence: return stored observation
>         persist sequence, load_state, health, observed_at
>         derive rollout counts from current-generation target rows
>         pause on unhealthy observation according to rollout policy
>         converge only when all required assignments and health gates pass
>         append audit/outbox with the observation
> ```

**What this is**
The whole acknowledgement procedure, in one transaction. Read it as: four checks, one replay guard, then the writes.

**Line by line**

- `lock deployment and target observation` — both rows, so counts derived below cannot race with a concurrent ack.
- `require authenticated target belongs to deployment` — the identity check. The authenticated agent must be the target it claims to be, and that target must be in this deployment's assignment.
  - Worked example: a compromised agent acking on behalf of 500 other targets is stopped here, before any count moves.
- `require generation == current assigned generation` — not merely "not lower". Exactly equal, which rejects both stale and impossible-future acks.
- `require assignment_hash == target's current assignment hash` — the content check.
- `if sequence <= last_observation_sequence: return stored observation`
  - The replay guard, and note what it returns: **the stored observation**, not an error.
  - Worked example of why returning the stored value is right: an agent retries an ack because it never saw the response. The retry is a replay. Returning an error would make the agent think something is wrong and possibly retry harder; returning the recorded state tells it the truth — this was already processed, and here is the result.
- `persist sequence, load_state, health, observed_at` — the write, including the timestamp that makes silence detectable later.
- `derive rollout counts from current-generation target rows`
  - **Derived, not incremented.** Worked example of why that matters: a counter incremented on each ack drifts the moment any ack is double-processed or any row is corrected by hand. Deriving the counts from the rows means the dashboard cannot disagree with the data, ever.
  - "from current-generation target rows" scopes the derivation so acks belonging to a superseded generation do not contaminate the current count.
- `pause on unhealthy observation according to rollout policy` — the automatic stop, driven by the policy attached to the deployment rather than by hard-coded logic.
- `converge only when all required assignments and health gates pass` — **"all required"**, which is where exemptions enter: an exempted target is not required, and silent targets are neither exempt nor passing, so they block.
- `append audit/outbox with the observation` — inside the transaction, so the audit record and the event cannot exist without the state change, or vice versa.

## CA rollover and adoption evidence — IC3 baseline

The five-phase state machine with its gates, transition rules, cross-signing and its caveats, the evidence ladder, the silent cohort, consumer static stability, rollback limits, and the exact failure this whole section exists to prevent.

---

> ```text
> PUBLISH_TRUST     bundle b+1 = old CA + new CA (+ cross-cert); deploy to every group that verifies this CA
>   gate ->         every required verifier cohort loaded the exact assignment; exclusions explicitly approved
> VERIFY_TRUST      one canary leaf under the new CA on a canary group; old chain keeps serving elsewhere
>   gate ->         canary handshake error-rate delta < 0.1% for 1 h across client cohorts
> SWITCH_LEAVES     re-issue every CURRENT leaf under the new CA in jittered batches (normal renewal + deployment)
>   gate ->         every leaf under the old CA re-issued, or exempt with an owner and an expiry
> VERIFY_SERVING    handshakes by issuer SKI; old-CA serials must reach zero
>   gate ->         zero old-chain handshakes for 72 h in every cohort, not in the aggregate
> RETIRE_OLD_TRUST  bundle b+2 = new CA only; deploy; old CA key remains authorized only for required CRL signing until its last leaf expires
>   end ->          old CA version DEPRECATED; key disabled, then scheduled for deletion after the waiting period
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
Five phases, each with a gate that must pass before the next begins. Read the phase names as a sentence and the whole strategy is there: publish trust, verify trust, switch leaves, verify serving, retire old trust.

**The single most important property of this ordering**
Trust is published **first** and retired **last**. Everything risky happens in the middle, while both anchors are trusted.

- Worked example of the ordering reversed: switch the leaves to the new CA on Monday and distribute the new trust bundle afterwards. Every client that has not yet received the bundle rejects every new certificate with `unknown issuer`. That is a fleet-wide outage, starting the instant the first leaf is deployed.
- With this ordering there is a long window — days or weeks — during which both the old and new CA are trusted everywhere. A leaf can move to the new CA at any point in that window and every client accepts it.

**Phase 1 — `PUBLISH_TRUST`**

> bundle b+1 = old CA + new CA (+ cross-cert); deploy to every group that verifies this CA
> gate -> every required verifier cohort loaded the exact assignment; exclusions explicitly approved

- A new bundle version holding **both** CAs, plus the cross-certificate. Nothing has changed about what is being *served* — only about what is *trusted*.
- **"Every group that verifies this CA"** is a broader set than "every group that uses certificates from this CA", and the difference is where rollovers go wrong.
  - Worked example: service `A` presents certificates from this CA; service `B` never does, but it *connects to* `A` and therefore must trust the CA. If the bundle goes only to `A`'s group, `B` has no idea the new CA exists and rejects `A`'s new certificate later. The set to enumerate is verifiers, not presenters.
- **The gate: "every required verifier cohort loaded the exact assignment; exclusions explicitly approved."**
  - "Loaded the exact assignment" — not merely acked, but acked with the matching assignment hash.
  - "Exclusions explicitly approved" — anything skipped is a named decision, not an omission.

**Phase 2 — `VERIFY_TRUST`**

> one canary leaf under the new CA on a canary group; old chain keeps serving elsewhere
> gate -> canary handshake error-rate delta < 0.1% for 1 h across client cohorts

- One certificate, under the new CA, on one canary group. The rest of the fleet is untouched.
- This is the **test that the trust distribution actually worked**, using real traffic rather than acks.
- Worked example of what it catches that phase 1's gate cannot: every target acked that it loaded bundle `b+1`. But a particular client runtime cannot build a path to the new CA — its path builder gives up on the first chain it constructs. The acks were all honest; the clients still fail. Only real handshakes reveal it, and here they reveal it on one canary rather than on everything.
- **"Error-rate delta < 0.1%"** — a delta, not an absolute. Worked example of why: a healthy fleet already has a baseline handshake error rate from timeouts, aborted connections, and misconfigured clients. Requiring zero errors would never pass. Requiring the rate not to *move* is the measurable question.
- **"Across client cohorts"**, not in aggregate. Worked example: an aggregate delta of 0.05% could be one client cohort — the Java 8 services, say — failing at 40% while everything else is unaffected. The aggregate hides it; per-cohort does not.
- **One hour** of soak, which is short because only one target is at risk.

**Phase 3 — `SWITCH_LEAVES`**

> re-issue every CURRENT leaf under the new CA in jittered batches (normal renewal + deployment)
> gate -> every leaf under the old CA re-issued, or exempt with an owner and an expiry

- The bulk phase, and the one the capacity section sized: hours to days, rate-limited, jittered.
- **"normal renewal + deployment"** is a good design note: switching a leaf to the new CA uses the *same* pipeline as an ordinary renewal. No special-case code path exists for rollovers.
  - Worked example of why that matters: a bespoke rollover issuance path would be exercised once every few years, which is exactly the code most likely to be broken when needed. Reusing the renewal path means the rollover runs on machinery that executes 16,700 times a day.
- **The gate: every leaf re-issued, or exempt with an owner and an expiry.** No leaf is simply left behind.

**Phase 4 — `VERIFY_SERVING`**

> handshakes by issuer SKI; old-CA serials must reach zero
> gate -> zero old-chain handshakes for 72 h in every cohort, not in the aggregate

- The evidence phase. The question is not "did we deploy?" but "is anyone still using the old CA?"
- **By issuer SKI**, because the question is about the CA, not the certificate. Worked example of why the serial alone is insufficient: a target could be serving a brand-new leaf serial that is still chained to the *old* CA. The serial looks like progress; the SKI shows it is not.
- **72 hours**, which is much longer than the 1-hour canary soak, and deliberately.
  - Worked example of why three days: some traffic is not continuous. A batch job runs nightly; a partner integration connects weekly; a disaster-recovery failover exercises paths that are otherwise idle. A 1-hour window would see zero old-chain handshakes simply because nothing that uses the old chain happened to run.
  - Seventy-two hours covers at least three nightly cycles and a weekend boundary.
- **"In every cohort, not in the aggregate"** — the same rule as phase 2, and for the same reason. Worked example: aggregate old-chain handshakes at zero, while one cohort has simply stopped reporting. In aggregate that is indistinguishable from success.

**Phase 5 — `RETIRE_OLD_TRUST`**

> bundle b+2 = new CA only; deploy; old CA key remains authorized only for required CRL signing until its last leaf expires
> end -> old CA version DEPRECATED; key disabled, then scheduled for deletion after the waiting period

- A third bundle version, holding only the new CA. This is the irreversible step — after it, nothing trusts the old CA.
- **"Old CA key remains authorized only for required CRL signing until its last leaf expires."**
  - Worked example of why the key cannot simply be destroyed: a leaf issued under the old CA six weeks ago is still valid for another six weeks and might be compromised tomorrow. Revoking it means listing its serial on a CRL signed by the *old* CA — nothing else can sign that list, because verifiers check a CRL's signature against its issuer.
  - "Restricted to CRL signing" means the key's permissions are narrowed to exactly that. It can no longer issue certificates, so a compromise of the old key at this point is far less dangerous than it was.
- **The ending, in three steps with a gap between each:** deprecate the version, disable the key, then schedule deletion after a waiting period.
  - Worked example of why deletion waits: deleting a key is the one action in this whole system with no recovery at all. A waiting period means a mistake discovered in week two is still fixable, because the key is disabled rather than gone.

---

> - Transition rules
>   - Every phase and gate result is persisted on the rollover row
>   - `advance` is a compare-and-set on `expectedPhase`.
>   - Phases move forward only
>   - "back" means a new bundle or deployment generation that restores the previous phase's contents.
>   - Restoring an old trust anchor requires an unexpired, uncompromised certificate accepted by current policy.
>   - Deleting its private key prevents further signing, not mathematical verification of existing signatures.

**What this says in plain words**
Four rules, and the last two are a precise correction of a common misunderstanding.

1. **Every phase and gate result is persisted.** The evidence for each advance is recorded, so the decision is auditable long after the dashboards have rolled over.
2. **`advance` is a compare-and-set on `expectedPhase`.** Two operators cannot advance twice.
3. **Phases move forward only; "back" means a new generation restoring the previous contents.**
   - The same idea as rollback being a forward deployment, one level up. There is no reverse gear; there is only "deploy the earlier contents, later".
4. **"Restoring an old trust anchor requires an unexpired, uncompromised certificate accepted by current policy."**
   - Three independent conditions, and any one of them can make restoration impossible.
   - **Unexpired** — a CA certificate that has passed its own `notAfter` cannot be re-trusted; verifiers reject an expired anchor.
   - **Uncompromised** — if the rollover was caused by a compromise, the old anchor must never come back.
   - **Accepted by current policy** — worked example: the old CA is RSA-2048 and the fleet's verifiers have since been configured to require 3072-bit minimums. The certificate is unexpired and uncompromised, and clients still refuse it.
5. **"Deleting its private key prevents further signing, not mathematical verification of existing signatures."**
   - The correction worth understanding properly, because the intuition is wrong.
   - A signature is verified with the **public** key. Destroying the private key removes the ability to create *new* signatures; it does nothing to signatures that already exist.
   - Worked example: the old CA's private key is deleted on Monday. A certificate it signed last year, presented on Tuesday, still verifies perfectly — provided the CA's *certificate* is in the client's trust bundle. The private key was never involved in verification.
   - Which means the thing that actually ends trust in the old CA is **removing its certificate from bundles** (phase 5), not deleting the key. Deleting the key is about preventing future issuance. Two different controls, often confused.

---

> - Cross-signing
>   - The old CA signs a certificate for the new CA's public key, so one key carries two issuer signatures.
>   - Test alternative paths on representative verifiers.
>   - A cross-certificate does not guarantee every path builder can reach either anchor.
>   - AKI/SKI must be correct on both intermediates or path building picks the wrong parent and reports `unknown issuer`.
>   - The cross-certificate leaves the served chain only after `RETIRE_OLD_TRUST`.
>   - Compatibility tests cover path length, name constraints, algorithm support, intermediate delivery, and anchor expiry.

**What this says in plain words**
Cross-signing lets one certificate chain to either the old or the new anchor. It works — when the client's path builder finds the alternative, which is not guaranteed.

**The mechanism**

- The old CA signs a certificate for the **new CA's public key**. The new CA's key now has two certificates: one from itself or its parent, and one from the old CA.
- Worked example of what that buys: a client that trusts only the old CA is presented with a leaf signed by the new CA. Ordinarily it rejects it. With the cross-certificate available, it can build: leaf → new CA certificate signed by old CA → old CA. Every link verifies using only the anchor it already has.
- One leaf works for both old-trust and new-trust clients, which is what makes a gradual transition possible at all.

**The caveats, which are the real content**

1. **"A cross-certificate does not guarantee every path builder can reach either anchor."**
   - Worked example of a path builder that fails: an implementation constructs exactly one chain — the first it can assemble — and if that chain does not end at a trusted anchor, it gives up without backtracking to try the cross-signed alternative. The correct path exists and the client never looks for it.
   - This is not hypothetical; it is what broke a large population of clients during the most famous public root rotation, discussed in the references.
2. **"AKI/SKI must be correct on both intermediates or path building picks the wrong parent and reports `unknown issuer`."**
   - **SKI** — Subject Key Identifier, a hash of *this* certificate's public key.
   - **AKI** — Authority Key Identifier, naming the key that signed this certificate.
   - Together they are how a path builder links a certificate to its parent: match this certificate's AKI against candidate parents' SKIs.
   - Worked example of getting it wrong: the cross-certificate carries an AKI that does not match the old CA's SKI. The path builder cannot find the parent, concludes the chain is incomplete, and reports `unknown issuer` — even though the correct certificate is sitting right there in the chain the server sent.
   - The frustrating part: the error message says "unknown issuer", which points at trust configuration, when the actual fault is a mismatched identifier field. Knowing to check AKI/SKI when a cross-signed chain fails is genuinely useful.
3. **"The cross-certificate leaves the served chain only after `RETIRE_OLD_TRUST`."**
   - It stays in the chain servers present until old trust is gone, because until then some client may still need it.
   - Worked example of removing it early: a client that only has the old anchor and no longer receives the cross-certificate has no path, and fails — even though nothing about the trust bundles changed.
4. **"Compatibility tests cover path length, name constraints, algorithm support, intermediate delivery, and anchor expiry."**
   - Five things that can independently break a cross-signed chain:
     - **Path length** — the old CA's `pathLenConstraint` may forbid it from signing another CA at all.
     - **Name constraints** — the old CA may be constrained to names that the new hierarchy's certificates fall outside of.
     - **Algorithm support** — an old client may not support the new CA's signature algorithm.
     - **Intermediate delivery** — the server must actually send the cross-certificate; a client that never receives it cannot use it.
     - **Anchor expiry** — the old anchor must outlive the transition, which is the failure that broke the public web in 2021.
   - **"Test alternative paths on representative verifiers"** is the instruction that ties them together: this is a compatibility matrix to run against your real client population, not a property to assume.

---

> - Evidence ladder
>   - Downloaded: the agent fetched and hash-verified the artifact
>     - Proves distribution.
>   - Loaded: the agent acked after a hot swap
>     - Proves the target can serve.
>   - Serving: counters show the new serial at servers and the new issuer SKI at clients
>     - Only this proves traffic moved.
>   - A deployment `CONVERGED` on acks but showing old serials means an agent loaded a context the listener is not using.

**What this says in plain words**
Three rungs of increasing strength, and each proves strictly less than people assume.

1. **Downloaded — proves distribution.** The bytes arrived and hashed correctly. It says nothing about whether they work.
2. **Loaded — proves the target *can* serve.** The agent installed it into a TLS context without error. It says nothing about whether anything actually used it.
3. **Serving — proves traffic moved.** Real handshakes report the new serial and the new issuer SKI.
   - Note this rung is measured at **both ends**: the new serial at servers, and the new issuer SKI at clients.
   - Worked example of why both: the server knows what it presented. Only the client knows whether it *accepted* it. A server happily presenting a chain that some client cohort rejects looks perfect from the server side.

**The last line, which is the payoff**

> A deployment `CONVERGED` on acks but showing old serials means an agent loaded a context the listener is not using.

- A specific, diagnosable fault, not a general warning.
- Worked example: a proxy has several listeners. The agent builds a new TLS context and installs it — honestly, successfully — into a context object that the listener accepting production traffic does not reference. Every ack is truthful. Every handshake still presents the old certificate.
- Nothing in the acknowledgement path can detect this, because nothing in the acknowledgement path observes traffic. Only counters do.
- The general principle: **acknowledgements report what a process believes it did; counters report what actually happened. For anything irreversible, require the second.**

---

> - Silent cohort
>   - A registered target with no ack or counters for `24 h` is `silent`, not done.
>   - A gate cannot pass with unknowns
>   - An exemption names an owner, a reason, and an expiry, and is audited.
>   - Concrete values: CA overlap `≥ 30 d`, serving window `72 h`, canary soak `1 h`.

**What this says in plain words**
Targets that stop talking are their own category, they block gates, and the only way past them is a named exemption.

**"A gate cannot pass with unknowns"**

- The fail-safe rule, stated as plainly as it can be.
- Worked example of the alternative: 199,000 targets report zero old-chain handshakes and 1,000 report nothing. Treating silence as success retires the old CA and breaks whichever of those 1,000 still needed it — and they were silent *because* their agent was old, which is precisely the population most likely not to have converged.
- Worked example of the rule working: the gate fails, the `409 GATE_NOT_MET` response lists the 1,000 silent targets, and someone finds that they are all in one availability domain whose agent deploy failed three weeks ago.

**"An exemption names an owner, a reason, and an expiry, and is audited"**

- Three fields, each doing distinct work.
  - **Owner** — a person accountable for the decision. Worked example of its absence: an automated skip-after-timeout breaks thirteen appliances and nobody can say who chose that.
  - **Reason** — what was known at the time.
  - **Expiry** — so a transitional exception does not become permanent by inattention. When it lapses, the target reappears as a gate failure and the question is asked again.

**The three concrete values**

- **CA overlap ≥ 30 days** — how long both CAs remain trusted. Much longer than the 7-day certificate overlap elsewhere in this group, because a CA rollover touches every verifier in the fleet rather than the consumers of one certificate.
- **Serving window 72 hours** — the `VERIFY_SERVING` soak.
- **Canary soak 1 hour** — the `VERIFY_TRUST` soak.
- Worked example of the ratio: 30 days of overlap against 72 hours of required silence means there is roughly a ten-fold margin between "we have proof nothing uses the old CA" and "the old CA stops being trusted".

---

> - Static stability of consumers
>   - The agent persists manifest, artifacts, and bundle on disk and loads them at boot before any network call.
>   - A `5xx` or timeout on poll leaves the current context untouched
>   - The agent backs off with jitter.
>   - The cache extends availability, never validity
>   - The outage countdown is the earliest `notAfter` in the fleet.
>   - Defeater to volunteer: a consumer that fetches on boot with no local copy dies in a control-plane outage, so last-known-good ships in the image or persists on disk.

**What this says in plain words**
The agent works without the control plane, and the two middle bullets state exactly how far that goes.

1. **"Persists manifest, artifacts, and bundle on disk and loads them at boot before any network call."**
   - All three, and **before any network call** is the ordering that matters. The agent reaches a serving state first and talks to the control plane second.
2. **"A `5xx` or timeout on poll leaves the current context untouched."**
   - Failure means *do nothing*, which is the correct response and not the obvious one.
   - Worked example of the wrong behavior: an agent that clears its context or falls back to some default on a failed poll would turn a transient control-plane blip into a fleet-wide TLS outage. Keeping the existing context means a failed poll is invisible to traffic.
3. **"The cache extends availability, never validity."**
   - The sentence that bounds the entire static-stability claim, and it is worth saying out loud in an interview.
   - Worked example: a cached certificate whose `notAfter` was yesterday is cached and useless. The cache means the agent does not need the control plane to *keep serving what it has*; it does not and cannot extend how long what it has remains valid.
4. **"The outage countdown is the earliest `notAfter` in the fleet."**
   - Which makes the control-plane outage's severity a computable number rather than a judgement.
   - Worked example: hour 6 of an outage with the earliest `notAfter` 26 days away is a serious incident with no deadline. The same outage with the earliest `notAfter` 4 hours away is an emergency requiring certificates to be issued by any means available.
5. **The defeater to volunteer: cache on boot.**
   - Worked example: everything is held in memory and fetched at startup. The control plane goes down at 02:00. At 02:30 an autoscaling event starts a new instance, which has no local copy, cannot reach the control plane, and cannot serve TLS. The fleet was statically stable right up until anything restarted — which, across 200,000 targets, is continuously.
   - Two fixes, both about having a copy that predates the outage: ship last-known-good in the image, or persist the cache to disk.

---

> - Rollback limits
>   - A leaf rollback works while the previous version is unexpired and unrevoked.
>   - A bundle rollback requires a still-valid and uncompromised old trust anchor.
>   - Private-key availability is needed for future signatures and CRLs, not for verifying existing signatures.
>   - After `RETIRE_OLD_TRUST`, re-adding the old CA is a new bundle version, which is a forward rollover in the other direction.
>   - Last-known-good caching never revives a revoked or expired trust state.

**What this says in plain words**
Five statements about what rollback can and cannot do. The theme: rollback is not time travel, it is a forward deployment of older content, and older content has to still be usable.

1. **Leaf rollback works while the previous version is unexpired and unrevoked.** Cheap and reliable — this is the automatic abort path, and `PREVIOUS` exists precisely for it.
2. **Bundle rollback requires a still-valid and uncompromised old anchor.** Strictly harder, because the anchor must satisfy conditions the leaf does not.
3. **Private-key availability is for future signatures and CRLs, not for verification.** The correction from the transition rules, restated because it is the thing people get wrong twice.
4. **"After `RETIRE_OLD_TRUST`, re-adding the old CA is a new bundle version, which is a forward rollover in the other direction."**
   - The honest framing. Once the old CA has been removed from every bundle, getting back to it is not an undo — it is a full rollover campaign, with its own publish, verify, and soak phases, taking the same days or weeks.
   - Worked example of the practical consequence: `RETIRE_OLD_TRUST` is the point of no cheap return. That is why it is the last phase, and why the 72-hour gate in front of it is the strictest in the machine.
5. **"Last-known-good caching never revives a revoked or expired trust state."**
   - The cache holds bytes, not authority. Worked example: an agent holds an old bundle containing a CA that has since been revoked. Loading it does not make that CA trustworthy again — every verifier checking revocation rejects the chain, and the certificate expired anyway if that is what happened.

---

> - Exact failure
>   - Switching leaves before trust is loaded yields `unknown issuer` at every client lacking the new CA.
>   - The failure appears one hop away from the server that received the certificate, which is why client-side counters and the agent pre-flight both exist.
>   - Do not depend on automatic AIA fetching across client runtimes.
>   - Test the configured JVM and OpenSSL clients with exactly the chain delivered by the server.

**What this says in plain words**
The single failure this whole section is built to prevent, plus the observation that makes it hard to detect.

1. **"Switching leaves before trust is loaded yields `unknown issuer` at every client lacking the new CA."**
   - Worked example with numbers: 200,000 targets, 40,000 of which have not yet loaded bundle `b+1`. Leaves switch at 09:00. Every connection *to* those 40,000 targets' peers — or rather, every connection from clients that lack the new CA — fails immediately. That is 20% of the fleet's traffic failing at once, and the rollback takes as long as a deployment wave.
2. **"The failure appears one hop away from the server that received the certificate."**
   - The observation that makes this failure genuinely hard, and it is worth dwelling on.
   - The server that loaded the new certificate is *fine*. Its own health checks pass. Its logs show successful loads. Its metrics show it serving.
   - The failure is at the **client**, which is a different process, on a different host, owned by a different team, reporting to a different dashboard.
   - Worked example of the resulting confusion: team A deploys a certificate, sees green. Team B's service starts failing to reach team A's. Team B investigates their own code first, because nothing on their side changed. The connection between the two events is not obvious from either side.
   - **"Which is why client-side counters and the agent pre-flight both exist."** Two independent answers to the same problem:
     - **Client-side counters** make the failure visible where it happens.
     - **Agent pre-flight** stops the deployment before any client sees it.
3. **"Do not depend on automatic AIA fetching across client runtimes."**
   - **AIA** is the Authority Information Access extension, a URL inside a certificate pointing at its issuer's certificate. Some clients will fetch a missing intermediate from that URL automatically.
   - Worked example of why relying on it is a mistake: OpenSSL historically does not do it at all, many JVM configurations do not, and any client in a network without outbound access to that URL cannot. A chain that works in testing because the tester's client fetched a missing intermediate will fail in production where nothing does.
   - The rule this implies: **the server must send the complete chain.** Do not rely on the client to fill in gaps.
4. **"Test the configured JVM and OpenSSL clients with exactly the chain delivered by the server."**
   - Three precise requirements in one line: the *configured* clients (with your actual trust stores and options, not defaults), the *specific runtimes* that matter, and *exactly* the bytes the server sends.
   - Worked example of a test that proves nothing: verifying the chain with `openssl verify` on a laptop, using the system trust store and a locally assembled chain file. That tests a chain nobody will ever be sent, against a trust store nobody in production has.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| `PUBLISH_TRUST` | Distributing a bundle holding both old and new CAs. | First phase; trust always precedes leaves. |
| `VERIFY_TRUST` | One canary leaf under the new CA, watched for an hour. | Tests distribution with traffic, not acks. |
| `SWITCH_LEAVES` | Re-issuing every leaf under the new CA in jittered batches. | Uses the ordinary renewal path, not a special one. |
| `VERIFY_SERVING` | Requiring zero old-chain handshakes for 72 hours per cohort. | The strictest gate, in front of the irreversible step. |
| `RETIRE_OLD_TRUST` | Publishing a bundle with only the new CA. | The point of no cheap return. |
| Verifier set | Everything that validates this CA, not only what presents it. | The set a trust bundle must reach. |
| Error-rate delta | The change in handshake errors, not the absolute rate. | A healthy fleet has a nonzero baseline. |
| Per-cohort gate | Requiring the condition in every cohort, not in aggregate. | An aggregate hides one cohort failing badly. |
| 72-hour soak | The serving window covering nightly and weekly traffic. | An hour would miss a batch job that runs at 02:00. |
| CRL-signing-only restriction | Narrowing a retired CA key's permissions. | Its leaves may still need revoking. |
| Disable then delete | Two steps with a waiting period between them. | Deletion is the one action with no recovery. |
| Forward-only phases | "Back" meaning a new generation restoring old contents. | There is no reverse gear, only later deployments. |
| Anchor restoration conditions | Unexpired, uncompromised, and accepted by current policy. | Any one failing makes restoration impossible. |
| Key deletion versus trust removal | Preventing new signatures versus ending trust. | Verification uses the public key; deletion does not affect it. |
| Cross-certificate | The old CA's certificate for the new CA's public key. | Lets one leaf chain to either anchor. |
| Path builder | The client component assembling a chain to a trust anchor. | May not backtrack to find the cross-signed path. |
| SKI | Subject Key Identifier: a hash of this certificate's public key. | Half of how a path builder links child to parent. |
| AKI | Authority Key Identifier: names the key that signed this certificate. | A mismatch reports `unknown issuer` misleadingly. |
| Compatibility matrix | Path length, name constraints, algorithms, delivery, anchor expiry. | Five independent ways a cross-signed chain breaks. |
| Evidence ladder | Downloaded, Loaded, Serving — three rungs of proof. | Only the third proves traffic moved. |
| Loaded-but-not-serving | An agent installing a context the listener does not use. | Acks are truthful; handshakes still show the old serial. |
| Silent target | Registered, but no ack or counters for 24 hours. | Blocks gates; never counted as done. |
| Exemption | A named owner, a reason, and an expiry, audited. | The only way past a silent target. |
| CA overlap ≥ 30 days | How long both CAs stay trusted. | Far longer than a leaf overlap; every verifier is involved. |
| Load before network call | The agent reaching a serving state from disk at boot. | Survives a restart during a control-plane outage. |
| Availability not validity | The cache extending reachability, never expiry. | A cached expired certificate is cached and useless. |
| Outage countdown | The earliest `notAfter` in the fleet. | Turns outage severity into a number. |
| Cache-on-boot defeater | Fetching at startup with no local copy. | The fleet is stable until anything restarts. |
| AIA | Authority Information Access: a URL for fetching a missing issuer certificate. | Unreliable across runtimes; send the full chain instead. |
| One-hop-away failure | The server looks healthy; the client fails. | Why client-side counters and pre-flight both exist. |

## Failure, operations, and security

A nine-row failure table, six observations, retry discipline, tenant isolation, and rollout safety.

---

> | Failure | Blast radius | Detection | Degraded behavior | Recovery |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Scheduler leader dead | Renewal backlog | Intent age and nearest validity deadline | Existing material remains usable only until its actual expiry/status deadline | Fenced failover and durable-intent catch-up |
> | Duplicate renewal enqueue | One certificate | Dedup hit counter | Row lock and successor check make the second a no-op | None; alert if dedup misses rise |
> | Vault or HSM unavailable | New issuance and rollover | `Sign` error rate | Serving continues; renewals back off; expiry countdown | Vault fails over across availability domains; page at `14 d` |
> | Consumer never adopts | That target's clients | Ack missing past SLA; counters show old serial | Deployment stalls; old version still valid | Inspect agent; fix or exempt with expiry |
> | Leaf switched before trust (operator override) | One service's clients | `CHAIN_UNTRUSTED` acks; client handshake errors | Agent refuses to load; keeps old version | Rollback generation; re-run `PUBLISH_TRUST` |
> | Two orchestrators after lease expiry | One target group | `409 STALE_GENERATION` rate | Stale acks rejected; stale bump loses the lock | Renew lease; reconcile deployments |
> | Corrupt or stale bundle artifact | Polling targets | `sha256` mismatch at agent | Agent keeps last-known-good | Republish; purge CDN; new bundle version |
> | Control plane or metadata store outage | Lifecycle operations | Availability and remaining-validity alarms | Cached consumers continue only within their actual certificate/status validity | Restore authority and resume incomplete intents |
> | Old CA retired while an offline appliance serves the old chain | That appliance's clients | Per-cohort serving evidence | Clients without old trust reject it | Re-add old CA in a new bundle if unexpired; else re-issue the appliance leaf |

**What this says in plain words**
Nine failures. Read the fourth column first — in almost every row, the degraded behavior is "everything keeps serving", which is the design working.

**Row by row**

- **Scheduler leader dead.**
  - Blast radius is a backlog, not an outage. The detection is the pair from the architecture section: intent age *and* the nearest validity deadline.
  - **"Existing material remains usable only until its actual expiry/status deadline"** — the careful phrasing that avoids overclaiming. Nothing breaks now; everything breaks eventually, on a schedule you can compute.
  - "Fenced failover" — the replacement leader takes a new owner epoch, so a revived old leader cannot write.
- **Duplicate renewal enqueue.**
  - **Recovery: "None."** The most reassuring cell in the table. The design already handles it; there is nothing to do.
  - "Alert if dedup misses rise" is the subtlety. Worked example: dedup misses climbing from 2 a day to 400 a day means something upstream is malfunctioning — a queue redelivering aggressively, a scheduler double-running. The row lock still catches every one, so nothing breaks, and the rising counter is the only visible symptom of a real problem.
- **Vault or HSM unavailable.**
  - "Serving continues" — the data plane never touches the HSM.
  - "Expiry countdown" is listed as the degraded behavior because that is genuinely what is happening: nothing is failing, a clock is running.
  - Recovery notes availability-domain failover and the 14-day page, so the outage has both a fix and a deadline.
- **Consumer never adopts.**
  - Two detections, and they are different in kind: a missing ack (the target is not reporting progress) and counters showing the old serial (the target is actively still using the old certificate). Worked example of why both: an agent could ack `LOADED` and still serve the old chain — the loaded-but-not-serving fault — in which case only the counters reveal it.
  - Recovery is "fix or exempt with expiry", which is the only two options the gate model permits.
- **Leaf switched before trust (operator override).**
  - Note the parenthetical: this can only happen if someone deliberately bypasses the ordering. The state machine does not permit it.
  - **Degraded behavior: "Agent refuses to load; keeps old version."** The pre-flight doing its job. An operator error that would have been a fleet outage becomes a set of `CHAIN_UNTRUSTED` acks.
  - Recovery: roll back the generation and re-run `PUBLISH_TRUST` — do the phases in the right order this time.
- **Two orchestrators after lease expiry.**
  - The stalled-owner scenario from the fencing section. Detection is the `409 STALE_GENERATION` rate, which is a metric on a thing that should almost never happen.
  - "Stale acks rejected; stale bump loses the lock" — both halves of the fence, working.
- **Corrupt or stale bundle artifact.**
  - Detection is a hash mismatch **at the agent**, before anything is loaded.
  - Worked example: a CDN edge serves a truncated bundle. The file parses as valid PEM but is missing the last CA certificate. Without the hash check the agent loads it and silently stops trusting one issuer, which surfaces hours later as unexplained handshake failures. With the check, the artifact is rejected and last-known-good is kept.
  - Recovery includes "purge CDN" and "new bundle version" — because a bad artifact may be cached at many edges, and a new version with a new hash routes around all of them.
- **Control plane or metadata store outage.**
  - **"Cached consumers continue only within their actual certificate/status validity"** — the same careful phrasing as row one. The cache extends availability, never validity.
- **Old CA retired while an offline appliance serves the old chain.**
  - The scenario the silent-cohort rule exists to prevent, listed as a failure because it will eventually happen anyway.
  - **Two recoveries, and the branch is the interesting part:**
    - **If the old CA certificate is unexpired:** re-add it in a new bundle version. Expensive — a full rollover in the other direction — but possible.
    - **If it has expired:** there is no path back. The only fix is re-issuing the appliance's leaf under the new CA, which requires touching the appliance.
  - Worked example of why the branch matters operationally: it means the answer to "we retired too early" depends entirely on whether the old CA certificate is still within its own validity window. That is a date someone should know before pressing the button.

---

> - Observe
>   - Certificates expiring within `14 d` and `7 d`, by tenant and CA.
>   - Renewal success rate, scheduler lag, dedup hits.
>   - Convergence time, failed acks by error code, silent target count.
>   - Handshake counters by issuer SKI and serial
>   - Old-chain share during a rollover.
>   - Rollover phase age
>   - A phase older than its planned window pages.

**What this says in plain words**
Six groups of metrics, arranged from "are we about to have an outage" down to "is the rollover stuck".

1. **Expiring within 14 and 7 days, by tenant and CA.**
   - Two thresholds, because they mean different things. Fourteen days is "investigate"; seven days is "this is now urgent".
   - **By tenant and CA** is the dimension that makes it actionable. Worked example: 400 certificates inside 14 days sounds alarming. Broken down, all 400 belong to one CA in one tenant — which is one stuck renewal path, not four hundred problems.
2. **Renewal success rate, scheduler lag, dedup hits.**
   - **Scheduler lag** is the liveness metric that a dead scheduler cannot fake, and the reason it is listed alongside success rate: a dead scheduler has a perfect success rate.
   - **Dedup hits** is the early-warning signal from the failure table.
3. **Convergence time, failed acks by error code, silent target count.**
   - **Convergence time** — how long a deployment takes to reach every target. Worked example of watching it drift: a normal convergence of 40 minutes creeping to 6 hours means agents are polling less often than believed, or a cohort is struggling. Nothing is failing; the safety margin is eroding.
   - **Failed acks by error code** — `CHAIN_UNTRUSTED` and a generic load failure are completely different problems, and grouping them would hide that.
   - **Silent target count** — the number that blocks gates, tracked continuously rather than only when a gate fails.
4. **Handshake counters by issuer SKI and serial, and old-chain share during a rollover.**
   - "Old-chain share" is the single number an operator watches during `VERIFY_SERVING`: what fraction of handshakes still use the old CA. It should fall toward zero and stay there for 72 hours.
5. **Rollover phase age, paging when a phase outlives its planned window.**
   - The metric that catches a *stalled* rollover, which is otherwise invisible.
   - Worked example: `SWITCH_LEAVES` is planned for three days and has been running for eleven. Nothing has failed — batches are progressing, just far more slowly than expected, perhaps because the HSM is busier than planned. Without a phase-age alarm, nobody notices until the CA overlap window starts running out.

---

> - Retry discipline
>   - Exponential backoff with jitter on `Sign`, queue, and poll paths.
>   - Renewal retries are bounded by the day `75` page, not by attempt count.
>   - `409 STALE_GENERATION` and `CHAIN_UNTRUSTED` are corrective, never retried blindly.
>   - A generation bump that loses the row lock re-reads and rebases rather than retrying the same value.

**What this says in plain words**
Four rules, and the second and fourth are specific to this design rather than generic retry advice.

1. **Exponential backoff with jitter on all three paths.** Standard, applied consistently.
2. **"Renewal retries are bounded by the day `75` page, not by attempt count."**
   - The interesting reframing. Most retry policies bound by attempts or by elapsed time from the first attempt. Here the bound is a **calendar date derived from the certificate's expiry**.
   - Worked example of why that is the right bound: a renewal failing at day 60 could retry 10 times over an hour and give up, or retry patiently for 15 days. The second is correct, because the certificate is still perfectly valid and there is no urgency until the margin shrinks. Attempt counts have no relationship to the actual deadline; the expiry date does.
   - The general principle: **when the work has a real-world deadline, bound retries by the deadline, not by a count.**
3. **"`409 STALE_GENERATION` and `CHAIN_UNTRUSTED` are corrective, never retried blindly."**
   - **`STALE_GENERATION`** means the world moved on. Retrying the same request sends the same stale generation and fails identically. The correction is to re-read current state.
   - **`CHAIN_UNTRUSTED`** means the bundle does not contain the issuer. Retrying the load performs the identical verification against the identical bundle and fails identically. The correction is to publish the trust that is missing.
   - Both are failures with a known cause, and a known cause needs the cause addressed rather than the attempt repeated.
4. **"A generation bump that loses the row lock re-reads and rebases rather than retrying the same value."**
   - The precise version of rule 3 for the one operation where it is easy to get wrong.
   - Worked example: an orchestrator reads `current_generation = 12`, intends to write 13, and loses the race to another writer that already wrote 13. Retrying "write 13" fails forever, because 13 now exists. **Rebasing** means re-reading the row — now 13 — and writing 14.
   - The distinction is between retrying an *operation* and retrying a *value*. The operation ("create the next deployment") is still wanted; the specific value computed from stale state is not.

---

> - Tenant isolation
>   - Every certificate, bundle, deployment, and target lives in a compartment
>   - IAM policy statements gate create, deploy, and rollback.
>   - Agent identity is a resource principal mapped to one target
>   - It reads its own assignment and writes its own ack only.
>   - Quotas cap deployments in flight and targets per group per tenant.
>   - A tenant's token receives `404` on another tenant's identifiers, not `403`, to avoid existence leaks.

**What this says in plain words**
Four isolation mechanisms. The second is the one specific to this design.

1. **Compartments and IAM policy.** Everything lives in a tenant compartment, and policy gates the three mutating verbs: create, deploy, rollback.
   - Worked example of why rollback is listed separately: rollback is a privileged operation with fleet-wide effect, and someone permitted to create certificates should not automatically be permitted to roll the fleet back.
2. **"Agent identity is a resource principal mapped to one target; it reads its own assignment and writes its own ack only."**
   - A **resource principal** is a platform-issued identity a workload holds by virtue of being a recognized resource — no credential to distribute or rotate.
   - **Mapped to one target** is the containment. Worked example of what a compromised agent can and cannot do: it can read its own assignment (public material) and write its own ack. It cannot enumerate the fleet, cannot read another target's assignment, and cannot ack on another target's behalf — which is precisely the attack that would fake convergence and cause an early CA retirement.
3. **Quotas on deployments in flight and targets per group.**
   - Two dimensions, because they exhaust different things. Worked example: 10,000 simultaneous in-flight deployments would swamp the ack path and the orchestrator; one group with 5,000,000 targets would make every convergence query enormous. Capping one leaves the other open.
4. **`404` not `403` across tenants.** The existence-leak rule, identical to the rest of the group.

---

> - Rollout and rollback safety
>   - Manifest schema changes are additive
>   - Old agents ignore unknown fields.
>   - A new orchestrator must accept acks from the previous agent version.
>   - Bundle changes are canaried like leaf changes because a bad bundle has the widest blast radius in the system.
>   - Chaos test: isolate agents for 24 h after checking the entire test cohort has > 24 h remaining required validity.
>   - Include separate near-expiry cases that must follow the declared fail policy.

**What this says in plain words**
Five rules for changing the system safely. The third is the one that is easy to skip and expensive to skip.

1. **"Manifest schema changes are additive; old agents ignore unknown fields."**
   - The frozen-contract rule from the architecture section, stated as a deploy-time discipline.
   - Worked example of violating it: version 5 of the control plane renames a manifest field. Every agent older than version 5 fails to parse, refuses the deployment, and stops receiving certificate updates — silently, since each keeps serving its last-known-good, until those certificates expire weeks later.
2. **"A new orchestrator must accept acks from the previous agent version."**
   - Compatibility in the other direction, which is easier to forget. New server code must keep reading old client messages.
   - Worked example: the ack payload gains a required field in orchestrator version 6. Agents still on version 5 send acks without it, the orchestrator rejects them, and every deployment stalls at 0% convergence while every agent is in fact working perfectly.
3. **"Bundle changes are canaried like leaf changes because a bad bundle has the widest blast radius in the system."**
   - The sentence with the sharpest reasoning in this list.
   - Worked example of the asymmetry: a bad *leaf* breaks the one service that presents it. A bad *bundle* — one missing a CA that is still in use — breaks every connection that target verifies, to every peer, all at once. Bundles are small, boring files, and they are the most dangerous artifact in the system.
   - The instinct to treat a trust bundle as configuration rather than as a deployment is exactly the instinct this rule corrects.
4. **The chaos test: isolate agents for 24 hours, having first checked the cohort has more than 24 hours of remaining required validity.**
   - **"Required validity"**, not just certificate validity — the chain and the status evidence count too, which is the outage-budget correction from the fencing section applied to the test's precondition.
   - Worked example of the precondition mattering: running the test against a cohort whose status evidence expires in 6 hours produces a failure at hour 6 that says nothing about static stability. The test result would be uninterpretable.
5. **"Include separate near-expiry cases that must follow the declared fail policy."**
   - The deliberately-failing test. What happens when cached material *does* expire during an isolation?
   - The point is that the behavior is **declared in advance**. Worked example: does the agent fail closed, refusing connections, or fail open, presenting an expired certificate and letting clients decide? Both are defensible in different contexts. The unacceptable answer is that nobody knows until it happens in production.

## Alternatives and IC4 stretch

A five-row option table, four stretch scenarios, the weakest assumption, and three over-engineering warnings.

---

> | Option | What it is | Strength | Weakness | Choose when |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Push distribution | Control plane writes material to each consumer | Fast propagation | Must reach every host; a push outage halts rotation; no static stability by default | Small fleet inside one network |
> | Pull with ETag | Agent polls a signed manifest and fetches by hash | Statically stable; CDN-scalable; agent authenticates itself | Propagation bounded by poll interval | Baseline; hint with events |
> | Hard cutover | Swap CA and leaves in one step | Simple to explain | Every cached client breaks at once | Never in production |
> | Overlap with cross-signing | Alternate certified path for the new key | Can bridge compatible old-anchor clients | Path-builder and constraint compatibility; not safe after old-key compromise | Planned rollover after testing every required client cohort |
> | Short-lived leaves | `24 h` certificates; expiry replaces leaf rollover | Leaves roll over by themselves within a day | Issuance becomes a hot path with its own SLO | Service mesh with automated identity |

**What this says in plain words**
Five options across two decisions: how material reaches consumers, and how a CA transition is made safe.

**Decision one: push or pull**

- **Push distribution.** The control plane writes to each consumer.
  - Strength: fast. Worked example: a new certificate reaches every host in seconds rather than within a poll interval.
  - Three weaknesses, and they compound:
    - **"Must reach every host."** The control plane needs a route to 200,000 hosts, through whatever firewalls and network segments exist, and must know which are alive.
    - **"A push outage halts rotation."** If the push path is down, nothing propagates. There is no fallback, because push *is* the mechanism.
    - **"No static stability by default."** A pushed consumer has no reason to keep a local copy — it expects to be told. Worked example: a host restarts during a push outage, has nothing on disk, and cannot serve.
  - "Choose when: small fleet inside one network." Honest. At small scale push is genuinely simpler.
- **Pull with ETag (the baseline).**
  - Three strengths: statically stable (the agent keeps a local copy by necessity), CDN-scalable (identical bytes for everyone), and **"the agent authenticates itself"**.
    - That third one is easy to miss. Worked example of the difference: with push, the control plane must authenticate *to* each consumer, meaning credentials for 200,000 endpoints. With pull, each agent presents its own resource principal and the control plane authenticates *it* — one direction, using identity the platform already issues.
  - Weakness: propagation is bounded by the poll interval. Resolved by events as hints, which is why the "choose when" cell says "hint with events".

**Decision two: how a CA transition is made safe**

- **Hard cutover.** Swap CA and leaves in one step. **"Never in production."**
  - The table includes it precisely because it is the instinct to name and reject. Worked example: every client holding a cached bundle without the new CA fails at the instant of the swap, and the population of such clients is exactly the one you cannot enumerate.
- **Overlap with cross-signing (the chosen approach).**
  - Strength: bridges clients that still hold only the old anchor.
  - Two weaknesses, and the second is severe:
    - **Path-builder and constraint compatibility** — the caveats from the rollover section.
    - **"Not safe after old-key compromise."** Worked example of why: cross-signing means the new CA's authority is vouched for by the old CA. If the old key is in an attacker's hands, that vouching is worthless — the attacker can produce the same signature for a CA of their own choosing. A compromise-driven rollover must distribute the new anchor out of band, not bridge it from the compromised one.
  - So the "choose when" cell says **planned** rollover, after testing every required client cohort. Cross-signing is a tool for scheduled transitions, not for incident response.
- **Short-lived leaves.** 24-hour certificates, where expiry replaces leaf rollover entirely.
  - Strength: **"leaves roll over by themselves within a day."** Worked example: `SWITCH_LEAVES` disappears as a phase. Change the issuing CA and within 24 hours every certificate has naturally been reissued under it, because every certificate is reissued daily regardless.
  - Weakness: **"issuance becomes a hot path with its own SLO."** Worked example of the shift: at 90-day lifetimes, issuance running at 0.2 per second can be unavailable for hours with no consequence. At 24-hour lifetimes, an issuance outage longer than the renewal margin expires the fleet. A background correctness workload becomes a production dependency.
  - The trade in one line: short lifetimes remove the rollover problem and replace it with an availability problem.

---

> - IC4 stretch — stronger requirement: root rollover across verifiers you do not control
>   - Customer devices, Oracle wallets, and JVM `cacerts` run no agent, so serving evidence is incomplete by construction.
>   - Build a verifier inventory from LB and gateway registries and client cohorts before the rollover, not during.
>   - Retire old trust only when every mandatory verifier cohort is covered or an explicit owner accepts the remaining outage risk.
>   - A fleet-wide percentage alone cannot prove compatibility.

**What this says in plain words**
Everything in this design depends on agents reporting. Roll a root, and a large part of the verifier population has no agent at all.

1. **"Serving evidence is incomplete by construction."**
   - Not incomplete because of a bug or a gap in coverage — incomplete *structurally*, because those verifiers cannot be instrumented.
   - Three named populations, each a different problem:
     - **Customer devices** — outside the organization entirely. No agent, no telemetry, no deploy mechanism.
     - **Oracle wallets** — Oracle's credential store, read by database clients. Updated by whoever administers that database, on their schedule.
     - **JVM `cacerts`** — the Java trust store, baked into a JDK installation. Worked example: a service running on a JDK image built eighteen months ago has an eighteen-month-old trust store, and updating it means rebuilding and redeploying that service.
2. **"Build a verifier inventory before the rollover, not during."**
   - The same instruction as the root-compromise case in the sibling question, for the same reason: the failures are always verifiers nobody knew existed, and finding them under incident pressure is the worst possible time.
   - **From LB and gateway registries and client cohorts** — sources that know who connects, since the verifiers themselves cannot be asked.
   - Worked example of what the inventory finds: a partner integration connecting from outside, a hardware appliance in a datacenter nobody has logged into for three years, and a client library with a certificate bundle compiled in. None of them appear in any deployment system.
3. **"Retire old trust only when every mandatory verifier cohort is covered, or an explicit owner accepts the remaining outage risk."**
   - Two acceptable end states, and both are decisions. There is no "the numbers look good enough".
4. **"A fleet-wide percentage alone cannot prove compatibility."**
   - The reasoning: 99.8% coverage sounds excellent and says nothing about *which* 0.2%. Worked example: the missing 0.2% is one payment partner's integration. The percentage is wonderful; the outage is a payments outage.
   - This is the per-cohort rule from the gates, restated for a population that cannot be measured directly — which makes it stricter, not looser.

---

> - IC4 stretch — 10× load
>   - `10M` certificates at `24 h` lifetime renew about `6.9k` per minute
>   - HSM `Sign` becomes the bottleneck.
>   - Delegate per-node issuers with short-lived, name-constrained sub-CAs so the HSM signs one sub-CA per node per day, not one leaf per pod.
>   - Serve assignments from regional replicas and CDN-cached manifests
>   - Batch acks per node.

**What this says in plain words**
Ten times the certificates at a twentieth of the lifetime changes the problem from correctness to throughput.

**The arithmetic**
10,000,000 certificates ÷ 24 hours = 10,000,000 ÷ 1,440 minutes ≈ 6,900 per minute ≈ 115 per second.

- Against an HSM signing at "order of hundreds per second", 115 per second is most of the device's entire capacity, permanently, with nothing left for anything else.

**The three responses**

1. **Delegate per-node issuers with short-lived, name-constrained sub-CAs.**
   - The change that alters the arithmetic rather than buying more hardware.
   - Worked example: 1,000 nodes running 10,000 pods each. Signing every leaf is 10,000,000 HSM operations a day. Instead the HSM signs **one sub-CA per node per day** — 1,000 operations — and each node's agent issues its own pods' certificates locally, in microseconds.
   - **Name-constrained** is what keeps it safe: the node's sub-CA carries X.509 Name Constraints limiting it to that node's namespace, so a compromised node can only mint identities it could already impersonate.
   - **Short-lived** is the second control: the sub-CA expires in a day, so a stolen node sub-CA key is useless within 24 hours.
   - The general pattern: **delegation of signing authority under constraints** — move the hardware off the per-request path by having it authorize a local issuer instead of each item.
2. **Serve assignments from regional replicas and CDN-cached manifests.**
   - The read path at 10×. Worked example: 2,000,000 targets polling means the assignment endpoint must not be a single regional database. Regional replicas serve reads locally, and manifests — which change rarely — cache at the CDN.
3. **Batch acks per node.**
   - The write path. Worked example: 10,000 pods per node each acking individually is 10,000 writes from one host. One batched ack per node carrying all 10,000 statuses is a single request, and the reduction is the same order as counters-not-events in the capacity section.

---

> - IC4 stretch — cost and blast radius
>   - Cell the orchestrator and evidence store per region and per CA
>   - One CA's rollover cannot stall another's renewals.
>   - Two-tier hierarchy keeps root rollover rare
>   - Most rollovers touch one subordinate and its `100k` leaves.
>   - Evidence retention dominates storage cost
>   - Keep raw counters `30 d` and rollups `7 y`.

**What this says in plain words**
Two containment mechanisms and one observation about where the money actually goes.

1. **Cell the orchestrator and evidence store per region and per CA.**
   - **Per CA** is the unusual half, and it is the point. Worked example of the failure it prevents: CA `A` is mid-rollover, consuming orchestrator capacity and flooding the evidence store with per-cohort queries for three days. CA `B`'s ordinary renewals queue behind it. One planned maintenance activity starves an unrelated correctness workload.
   - Cells make a rollover's cost fall on its own CA only.
2. **Two-tier hierarchy keeps root rollover rare.**
   - Worked example of the difference in scope: rolling a subordinate touches its 100,000 leaves and the verifiers that trust *it*. Rolling a root touches every verifier anywhere, including the uninstrumentable ones from the previous stretch item. The two-tier structure means the second almost never happens.
3. **"Evidence retention dominates storage cost. Keep raw counters `30 d` and rollups `7 y`."**
   - The counterintuitive part, and worth checking with numbers. Worked example: certificates and bundles are a few gigabytes in total — trivial. Adoption evidence at 3,300 datapoints per second for 30 days is about 8.5 billion rows, which is the real storage line item.
   - **The two-tier retention answers two different questions:**
     - **Raw counters for 30 days** answer the operational question: is anything still on the old chain right now? That question has no meaning after a month.
     - **Rollups for 7 years** answer the compliance question: can you show that the 2024 rollover completed before the old CA was retired? That needs a summary, not per-minute granularity.
   - Worked example of the saving: rolling 30 days of per-minute counters into per-day per-cohort summaries reduces the retained volume by roughly three orders of magnitude while keeping every fact an auditor would ask for.

---

> - IC4 stretch — migration
>   - Adopt the agent on a legacy fleet in observe-only mode that reports counters without loading, so the evidence ladder exists before the first automated switch.

**What this says in plain words**
Deploy the agent first with its dangerous half turned off, so you have telemetry before you have automation.

**Why this is a good migration plan**

- The agent does two things: it *loads* certificates, and it *reports* what is being served. Observe-only mode keeps the second and disables the first.
- Worked example of the sequencing benefit: the agent is deployed fleet-wide in observe-only mode for a month. During that month it reports which chains are actually in use — which immediately reveals things nobody knew, such as three hosts still presenting a certificate from a CA that was supposedly retired last year. None of that required trusting the agent with a single change.
- **"So the evidence ladder exists before the first automated switch."** The first real deployment can be gated on evidence from day one, rather than being the thing that also tests whether evidence collection works.
- The general principle: **when adopting a system that both observes and acts, deploy the observing half first.** It is low-risk, it is immediately useful, and it means the acting half arrives into an environment where its safety gates already function.

---

> - Weakest assumption
>   - Telemetry completeness: that every verifier is registered and reports.
>   - Mitigation: reconcile the target registry against LB and gateway inventories daily
>     - Gate per cohort
>     - Every exemption expires.

**What this says in plain words**
The entire safety model rests on knowing who all the verifiers are and hearing from them. Both halves are optimistic.

**Why this is genuinely the weakest link**

- Every gate in the rollover machine asks "has everyone converged?" That question is unanswerable if the registry does not contain everyone.
- Worked example of the silent failure: the registry holds 200,000 targets. The real fleet is 200,350, because a team stood up a service outside the standard deployment path. All 200,000 registered targets report zero old-chain handshakes. The gate passes. The 350 unregistered targets break when old trust is retired — and they were invisible at every stage, because the system only ever asked about targets it knew.
- This is strictly worse than the silent-cohort problem, which is at least *visible*: a silent target is a known unknown. An unregistered target is an unknown unknown.

**The three mitigations, each closing a different hole**

1. **"Reconcile the target registry against LB and gateway inventories daily."**
   - The only defence against unregistered targets: compare the registry against systems that see traffic. Worked example: the load balancer knows about a backend the certificate registry has never heard of. Daily reconciliation surfaces it as a discrepancy.
2. **"Gate per cohort."** Already the rule, restated because it also limits the damage from partial telemetry: a cohort with no reporters is an obviously empty cohort, whereas in aggregate it is invisible.
3. **"Every exemption expires."** Stops the accumulated exemptions from quietly becoming the mechanism by which gates always pass.
   - Worked example of the drift: three rollovers, each granting a handful of permanent exemptions. By the fourth, 200 targets are permanently exempt and the gate is checking a shrinking subset of the fleet while appearing to be as strict as ever. Expiry forces each exemption to be re-justified.

---

> - Over-engineering warning
>   - Do not build push distribution for `200k` targets
>   - Pull with a CDN is simpler and statically stable.
>   - Do not try to make trust publication and leaf switch atomic
>   - The ordering is the safety property.
>   - Do not add OCSP to solve rollover
>   - Revocation is `s2.03`.

**What this says in plain words**
Three things not to build, and the second is the most interesting because it rejects something that sounds like rigor.

1. **"Do not build push distribution for `200k` targets."**
   - Worked example of what building it costs: connection state for 200,000 hosts, network reachability into every segment, liveness tracking, retry queues per host, and a fallback for hosts that are unreachable — which is a pull mechanism, so you end up building both.
   - Pull with a CDN is simpler, and it yields static stability for free rather than as an extra feature.
2. **"Do not try to make trust publication and leaf switch atomic. The ordering is the safety property."**
   - The instinct this corrects is a good one: two things must change together, so make them atomic. Here that instinct is wrong.
   - Worked example of why atomicity is both impossible and undesirable: the two changes happen on 200,000 independent machines with their own caches and schedules. There is no coordination point. But more importantly, atomicity would *remove* the overlap window — and the overlap window is what makes the transition safe. During it, both anchors are trusted, so a leaf can move at any moment and every client accepts it.
   - Trying to synchronize the two changes would replace a wide safe window with a single instant at which everything must be correct everywhere.
   - The general shape: **when a transition cannot be atomic, the answer is not a cleverer protocol — it is an ordering with an overlap, and a gate proving the first step finished.**
3. **"Do not add OCSP to solve rollover. Revocation is `s2.03`."**
   - Scope discipline. OCSP answers "has this certificate been revoked?" A rollover asks "has every verifier adopted the new CA?" They are unrelated questions.
   - Worked example of the confusion: someone proposes OCSP so clients can check certificate status during the transition. Status has nothing to do with it — the old certificates are perfectly valid throughout, and the problem is trust distribution, not revocation.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |

## Level signal — IC3 baseline vs IC4 stretch

One row per interview phase: what must land, what raises it, and the observable that separates the two.

---

> | Phase | IC3 must land | IC4 adds | Separating signal |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Requirements | Consumers, lifetimes, key custody, trust-before-leaves invariant | Names the unregistered-verifier cohort and what excluding it costs | Asks who verifies, not only who serves |
> | Estimates | Renewal rate, rollover burst against HSM throughput, poll load | Uses the burst number to size `SWITCH_LEAVES` duration and jitter | A number sets a phase length |
> | Architecture | Control/data-plane split, pull with cache, one renewal trace | Cost of each component; evidence store as a first-class owner | Every component has a mechanism and a price |
> | Deep dive | Idempotent renewal, generation fencing, five-phase rollover | Cross-sign chain mechanics, per-cohort gates, rollback limits | Explains why downloaded is not serving |
> | Operations | Failure table, static stability, expiry alarms | Silent cohorts, expiring exemptions, data-plane chaos test | Volunteers what blocks retirement before being asked |
> | Wrap | Recap and tradeoff | Names telemetry completeness as the weakest assumption | Identifies the flaw before the interviewer does |

**What this says in plain words**
A self-assessment grid. The second column is a checklist of content; the fourth is what actually distinguishes a stronger answer from a merely complete one.

**Why the fourth column is the one to score**
In every row the difference is not more material. It is a different kind of claim about the same material.

- **Row: Requirements.**
  - Must land: who the consumers are, the lifetimes, the key-custody modes, and the trust-before-leaves invariant.
  - Adds: naming the unregistered-verifier cohort and what excluding it costs.
  - Separating signal: **"Asks who verifies, not only who serves."**
  - Worked example of the difference, which is the whole question in miniature: a weaker answer enumerates the services that present certificates — the load balancers, the gateways, the sidecars — and designs distribution for them. The stronger answer notices that the set which must receive a new trust bundle is *everything that validates certificates from this CA*, which includes services that never present one, partner integrations, database clients reading wallets, and JVMs with year-old trust stores. The presenter set is a deployment list; the verifier set is a research project, and only one of them is the set that breaks.
- **Row: Estimates.**
  - Must land: the renewal rate, the rollover burst against HSM throughput, the poll load.
  - Adds: using the burst number to size the `SWITCH_LEAVES` duration and its jitter.
  - Separating signal: **"A number sets a phase length."**
  - Worked example of passing it: 100,000 leaves at a rate-limited 20 signatures per second is about 83 minutes of signing, spread with jitter across a multi-day window so routine renewals are never starved — therefore `SWITCH_LEAVES` is planned for three days, and a phase-age alarm fires if it runs past that. The estimate did not decorate the answer; it produced a plan and an alarm threshold.
  - Worked example of failing it: quoting 17 minutes of HSM time and then describing the phase as "takes a while".
- **Row: Architecture.**
  - Must land: the control-plane and data-plane split, pull with a cache, and one renewal traced end to end.
  - Adds: the cost of each component, and the evidence store as a first-class owner.
  - Separating signal: **every component states both its mechanism and its price.**
  - Worked example of the price most often omitted: the consumer agent buys static stability and costs you version skew across a 200,000-target fleet, which is why the manifest schema is a frozen contract. Saying the second half unprompted is the signal.
  - "Evidence store as a first-class owner" is the specific stretch here: listing it as a component with its own mechanism and its own failure mode — a silent cohort blocking retirement — rather than treating telemetry as a footnote.
- **Row: Deep dive.**
  - Must land: idempotent renewal, generation fencing, the five-phase rollover.
  - Adds: cross-sign chain mechanics, per-cohort gates, and rollback limits.
  - Separating signal: **"Explains why downloaded is not serving."**
  - Worked example of what that explanation sounds like: downloaded proves distribution, loaded proves the target can serve, and only counters prove traffic moved — because an agent can honestly load a TLS context that the listener accepting production traffic does not reference, in which case every ack is truthful and every handshake still presents the old certificate.
  - That is the evidence ladder, and being able to give a concrete fault that lives between two of its rungs is what distinguishes understanding it from having memorized it.
- **Row: Operations.**
  - Must land: the failure table, static stability, the expiry alarms.
  - Adds: silent cohorts, expiring exemptions, and the data-plane chaos test.
  - Separating signal: **"Volunteers what blocks retirement before being asked."**
  - Worked example: saying unprompted that a gate cannot pass with unknowns, that a registered target silent for 24 hours is `silent` rather than done, and that the only way past it is an exemption naming an owner and an expiry. It says you have thought about what happens when the fleet does not cooperate, which is a different posture from designing for the fleet that does.
- **Row: Wrap.**
  - Must land: the recap and the tradeoff.
  - Adds: naming telemetry completeness as the weakest assumption.
  - Separating signal: finding the flaw before the interviewer does.
  - Worked example of why this specific admission is credible: telemetry completeness is genuinely the weakest link, because every gate in the design asks "has everyone converged?" and that question is unanswerable if the registry does not contain everyone. Naming the real weakest assumption rather than a comfortable one is what makes the rest of the answer trustworthy.

**How to use this table in preparation**
Rehearse the whole answer, then score only the fourth column. Each entry is a yes-or-no observable rather than an impression, which is what makes it usable on yourself.

## Interview pushback and validation

Eight questions with a baseline and a stretch answer each, then nine validation scenarios.

---

> - Q1: The renewal job ran twice.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - The successor check, the queue dedup identity, and the worker's row lock each make the second run a no-op.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Name the crash between sign and insert
>     - A retry mints a new serial, so the orphan is bounded by lifetime and reconciled as revoked.

**What this question is testing**
Whether you have one defence or several, and whether you know which one is actually load-bearing.

**The baseline: three layers**
The scheduler's successor check, the queue's dedup identity, and the worker's row lock. Any of them makes the second run a no-op, and the third is the guarantee.

**What the stretch adds, and it is the harder half**

- **"Name the crash between sign and insert."** The one case the three layers do not cover.
  - Worked example: the worker takes the lock, checks the successor, calls the HSM, and receives a signature. Then it crashes before inserting the version row. A valid certificate now exists in the world and the database has no record of it.
  - The three idempotency layers all operate on database state, and the signature happened outside the database. None of them sees it.
- **"A retry mints a new serial, so the orphan is bounded by lifetime and reconciled as revoked."**
  - The honest accounting. The retry produces a *different* certificate with a *different* serial, so now there are two — one tracked, one orphaned.
  - **Bounded by lifetime** — the orphan expires on its own, in at most one certificate lifetime.
  - **Reconciled as revoked** — and before then, a reconciler finds the discrepancy and puts the orphan's serial on the CRL.
  - Worked example of why the bound is the thing to state: "there is a small window where an untracked certificate can exist" is a worry. "The worst case is one orphaned certificate, revoked by reconciliation and expiring within 90 days regardless" is a quantified residual. The second is an answer.
- Note the contrast with the sibling design in `s2.01`, which pins the serial in the intent so a retry reproduces the *same* certificate. That approach removes the orphan entirely; this one bounds it. Knowing that both exist, and what each costs, is the stretch.

---

> - Q2: Issuance succeeded.
>   - Is the rotation done?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - No
>     - The deployment converges on `LOADED` acks and version `n` is deprecated only when counters show serial `n` at zero.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - A `CONVERGED` deployment with old serials in counters means the listener is not using the loaded context.

**Why this question exists**
It is the shortest possible test of whether you understand the difference between issuing a certificate and deploying one. The correct answer begins with the word "No".

**The baseline: two separate gates**

- Convergence is on acks. Deprecation is on counters. They are different questions answered by different evidence.
- Worked example of collapsing them: treating a successful issuance as a completed rotation means deprecating version 7 the moment version 8 is signed — while 200,000 targets are still serving version 7. That is the classic rotation outage in its purest form.

**What the stretch adds**

- A specific, diagnosable fault: **`CONVERGED` on acks with old serials still in the counters.**
- Worked example: a proxy has several listeners. The agent builds a new TLS context and installs it — honestly and successfully — into a context object the listener accepting production traffic does not reference. Every ack is truthful. Every handshake still presents the old certificate.
- Nothing in the acknowledgement path can detect this, because nothing in the acknowledgement path observes traffic.
- Being able to name that fault, rather than saying "acks might be wrong", is what distinguishes having thought about the evidence ladder from having recited it.

---

> - Q3: A late ack from the previous rollout arrives after the new one started.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - It carries the old generation and is rejected with `409 STALE_GENERATION`.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - The agent also ignores manifests below its applied generation, covering a stale CDN in the other direction.

**What this question is testing**
Whether the fence works in both directions, or only in the one you thought about.

- **The baseline** handles the server side: an ack carrying an old generation is rejected.
- **The stretch** handles the agent side: a *manifest* carrying an old generation is ignored.
  - Worked example: the agent applied generation 12. A CDN edge still holds the manifest for generation 11 and serves it on the next poll. Without the agent-side check, the agent downgrades itself to an older certificate — and the server-side fence is no help, because nothing invalid was acknowledged. The agent would simply and quietly be running old configuration.
- The general shape: **a fencing token protects a boundary, and every boundary has two sides.** Checking only the side you control leaves the other open.

---

> - Q4: Why not switch leaves the same day trust is published?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Verifiers load trust on their own poll schedule
>     - The agent pre-flight refuses a leaf whose issuer is not in the loaded bundle.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - The `VERIFY_TRUST` canary catches verifiers without an agent, whose failure appears only in client error rates.

**What this question is testing**
Whether you understand that publishing and adopting are different events separated by an unknown amount of time.

**The baseline: two reasons**

1. **"Verifiers load trust on their own poll schedule."** Publishing a bundle does not deploy it. Worked example: 200,000 targets on hourly polls with jitter converge over hours in the best case and over days in the realistic one, because some hosts are asleep, some agents are wedged, and some are behind a deploy freeze.
2. **The agent pre-flight refuses a leaf whose issuer is not in the loaded bundle.** So even if you tried, the fleet would refuse — which turns an ordering mistake into failed acks rather than an outage.

**What the stretch adds, and why it is the better half**

- The pre-flight only protects targets that *run an agent*. **"The `VERIFY_TRUST` canary catches verifiers without an agent, whose failure appears only in client error rates."**
- Worked example: a partner integration, a database client reading an Oracle wallet, a JVM with a year-old `cacerts`. None of them run an agent, none of them pre-flight anything, and none of them will refuse gracefully. They will simply fail handshakes.
- And their failure appears **one hop away** — on the client side, in someone else's metrics, on someone else's dashboard.
- The canary phase exists precisely to surface this class with one certificate on one group, using real traffic, before the fleet moves.

---

> - Q5: The control plane is down for a week.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Cached material avoids a live issuance dependency.
>     - The actual outage budget is the shortest remaining leaf, chain, or status-validity window.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - The countdown is the earliest `notAfter` in the fleet
>     - A consumer with no on-disk copy that restarts is the case that breaks static stability.

**What this question is testing**
Whether "we are statically stable" is a slogan or a bounded claim.

**The baseline: the claim, and immediately its limit**

- Cached material means no live issuance dependency — that is the claim.
- **"The actual outage budget is the shortest remaining leaf, chain, or status-validity window"** — that is the limit, and volunteering it in the same breath is what makes the claim credible.
- Worked example of why all three matter: leaves valid for 40 more days, chained to an intermediate expiring in 9, with verifiers requiring status evidence refreshed every 24 hours. The budget is 24 hours, not 40 days.

**What the stretch adds**

1. **"The countdown is the earliest `notAfter` in the fleet."** Turns severity into a number. Worked example: day 3 of the outage with the earliest `notAfter` 26 days out is serious with no deadline; day 3 with the earliest 4 hours out is an emergency.
2. **"A consumer with no on-disk copy that restarts is the case that breaks static stability."** The defeater, volunteered.
   - Worked example: the fleet is stable for a week — until an autoscaling event starts a fresh instance at 02:00 on day 3. It has nothing on disk, cannot reach the control plane, and cannot serve TLS. Across 200,000 targets, restarts are continuous, so this is not an edge case; it is the normal case a week-long outage will certainly encounter.

---

> - Q6: Can we roll back after retiring the old CA?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Only while the old trust anchor remains valid, uncompromised, and permitted by current policy.
>     - Private-key deletion affects future signing, not verification of an existing signature.
>     - Rollback is a new bundle version that re-adds it.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - That is why the key stays enabled through the CRL-signing tail and deletion after the waiting period is the last irreversible step.

**What this question is testing**
Two things at once: whether you know rollback has preconditions, and whether you understand what a private key is actually for.

**The baseline: three statements**

1. **Three conditions, all required:** the old anchor must be unexpired, uncompromised, and permitted by current policy.
   - Worked example of the third failing alone: the old CA is RSA-2048, unexpired and uncompromised, and the fleet's verifiers have since been configured to require 3072-bit minimums. Re-adding it changes nothing, because clients refuse it.
2. **"Private-key deletion affects future signing, not verification of an existing signature."**
   - The correction most people need. A signature is verified with the **public** key. Destroying the private key removes the ability to create new signatures and does nothing to signatures that already exist.
   - Worked example: the old CA's private key is deleted on Monday. A certificate it signed last year, presented on Tuesday, still verifies perfectly — provided the CA's certificate is in the client's bundle. The private key was never involved.
   - Which means the thing that ends trust in the old CA is removing its certificate from bundles, not deleting its key. Two different controls, routinely confused.
3. **"Rollback is a new bundle version that re-adds it."** Not an undo — a forward rollover in the other direction, with its own publish, verify, and soak phases, taking days.

**What the stretch adds**
It connects the answer back to the design's own ordering: the key stays enabled through the CRL-signing tail, and **deletion after the waiting period is the last irreversible step**.

- Worked example of why that ordering is deliberate: as long as the key is merely disabled, a mistake is recoverable. Once it is deleted, nothing can sign a CRL for the certificates it issued, and there is no path back at all. Putting the single unrecoverable action last, behind a waiting period, is the same discipline as putting `RETIRE_OLD_TRUST` last in the phase machine.

---

> - Q7 (premise reversal): Why need consumer acks at all?
>   - The service is the source of truth
>   - Mark the deployment done when the API call succeeds.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - The API call proves desired state, not observed state
>     - The canonical rotation outage is a certificate issued and never loaded.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Acks are only the middle rung
>     - Retirement gates on serving evidence, and for verifiers without an agent the honest answer is an inventory plus a long cross-signed overlap.

**What a premise reversal is**
The interviewer attacks a component's existence rather than its design. The wrong response is to defend it defensively; the right one is to name the specific failure its absence produces.

**The baseline: one distinction and one failure**

- **"The API call proves desired state, not observed state."** The sentence to say. A successful API call proves you *wrote down what you wanted*. Nothing more.
- **"The canonical rotation outage is a certificate issued and never loaded."** The concrete failure. Worked example: version 8 is issued successfully and the deployment is marked done. Version 7 is deprecated a week later. Forty targets never loaded version 8 because their agent had been failing silently for a month, and all forty break at once — a week after the change everyone was watching.

**What the stretch adds, and it partially agrees with the interviewer**

- **"Acks are only the middle rung."** The questioner is right that acks are not the whole answer — they are just not right that the answer is fewer signals.
- Retirement gates on **serving evidence**, which is stronger than acks.
- **"For verifiers without an agent the honest answer is an inventory plus a long cross-signed overlap."**
  - The genuine limit, admitted. For the population that cannot ack and cannot report counters, there is no evidence mechanism at all. What replaces it is a pre-built inventory of who they are, and an overlap long enough that they converge on their own schedule.
  - Worked example: a partner integration updating its trust store quarterly needs an overlap measured in months, and the only way to know that is to have the partner on an inventory before the rollover starts.
- Conceding the honest limit while explaining why the mechanism still earns its place is a stronger answer than defending acks as sufficient.

---

> - Q8: The customer holds the private key.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - The service cannot recreate it, so renewal waits in `AWAITING_CSR` with a notification and a day `75` page.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Offer a managed-key migration where the next key is generated inside the tenant's Vault so later renewals are automatic.

**What this question is testing**
Whether "everything renews automatically" was a claim you examined.

**The baseline**

- **"The service cannot recreate it."** A certificate contains the *public* key; nothing in it permits reconstructing the private key. That is the entire basis of public-key cryptography, and it makes this a hard limit rather than a missing feature.
- So the renewal parks in **`AWAITING_CSR`** — a state that says the blocker is external. Worked example of why a distinct state is necessary: an ordinary in-progress state would look like a slow renewal, and retries would accomplish nothing. `AWAITING_CSR` changes the system's behavior — it notifies the owner instead of retrying, and still pages at day 75 like any other stalled renewal.

**What the stretch adds**
A path out rather than an acceptance of the limit: offer a **managed-key migration** where the *next* key is generated inside the tenant's Vault.

- Worked example of the sequencing: the customer submits one more CSR manually. That renewal is issued against a key the service now holds in the tenant's own Vault. From then on, every renewal is automatic and is also a key rotation.
- The migration costs the customer one manual step and removes the manual step forever. Offering the path, rather than documenting the exception, is the difference between describing a constraint and resolving one.

> - Validation scenarios
>   - Double enqueue and worker crash-and-retry for the same base version.
>   - Late ack with a stale generation.
>   - Manifest with a lower generation served from a stale CDN.
>   - Leaf deployed before its issuer is in the bundle
>   - Agent pre-flight path.
>   - Rollover gate with one silent target and one expired exemption.
>   - Old CA retired while a cohort still reports old-issuer handshakes.
>   - Control plane unreachable for `24 h` with an agent restart in the middle.
>   - Rollback requested after the previous version's `notAfter`.

**What this list is**
Eight tests, each mapping to a claim made earlier in the file. Every design claim has a scenario that would falsify it.

1. **Double enqueue and worker crash-and-retry for the same base version.**
   - Tests all three idempotency layers plus the orphan path from Q1. Worked example of running it: send two messages for the same base version, and kill the worker between the HSM call and the insert. The assertions are one tracked version and one orphan reconciled as revoked.
2. **Late ack with a stale generation.** Tests the server-side fence. The assertion is `409 STALE_GENERATION` and no change to convergence counts.
3. **Manifest with a lower generation served from a stale CDN.** Tests the agent-side fence — the other direction from Q3. The assertion is that the agent keeps its current context.
4. **Leaf deployed before its issuer is in the bundle; agent pre-flight path.**
   - Tests the single most valuable safety check in the design. Worked example: deploy a leaf signed by a CA absent from the bundle and confirm the agent acks `FAILED` with `CHAIN_UNTRUSTED` while continuing to serve the old certificate. A passing implementation turns a fleet outage into one failed ack.
5. **Rollover gate with one silent target and one expired exemption.**
   - Two blockers in one scenario, deliberately. The assertion is that the gate fails and the `409 GATE_NOT_MET` response names both the silent target and the lapsed exemption.
   - Worked example of the bug this catches: an implementation that treats an expired exemption as still exempt. The gate passes, and the target it was covering breaks.
6. **Old CA retired while a cohort still reports old-issuer handshakes.**
   - The scenario the entire `VERIFY_SERVING` gate exists to prevent, run deliberately to confirm the gate blocks it. The assertion is that retirement cannot proceed.
7. **Control plane unreachable for 24 hours with an agent restart in the middle.**
   - The cache-on-boot defeater as a test. The restart is the point — static stability without a restart proves very little.
   - Worked example of a passing implementation: the restarted agent reads its manifest, artifacts, and bundle from disk, loads them before any network call, and serves normally throughout.
8. **Rollback requested after the previous version's `notAfter`.**
   - Tests that rollback preconditions are enforced rather than assumed. The assertion is `409 UNRECOVERABLE`, not a deployment of an expired certificate.

**The shape worth copying**
Every scenario names an observable outcome — a specific error code, a specific ack, a specific gate result — rather than "it should work". That is what turns the list into a test plan and lets you answer "how would you know?" for any claim in the design.

## Recall card

Five one-liners and a four-sentence closing script, memorized rather than improvised.

---

> - “Immutable versions, explicit references, and a generation per target group; nothing points at latest.”

**What this says in plain words**
Three structural choices and one prohibition. The prohibition is the memorable half.

**Why "nothing points at latest" is the load-bearing clause**

- A reference to "latest" is a reference whose meaning changes without anyone changing it.
- Worked example of the failure: a deployment that said "deploy the latest certificate version" would mean version 8 when created and version 9 an hour later, so two agents fetching the same deployment at different times get different certificates. Convergence becomes unanswerable, because there is no fixed thing to converge on.
- Explicit references fix this: a deployment names version 8, forever. Immutable versions make that safe, because version 8's bytes never change. And the generation orders the deployments themselves.
- The three together mean every question of the form "what should target X be running right now?" has exactly one answer, and it is the same answer whenever you ask it.

---

> - “Renew at two-thirds lifetime so the control plane can be down a third of a lifetime before anyone notices.”

**What this says in plain words**
The two-thirds rule, stated by what it buys rather than by what it does.

**Why this phrasing is better than "renew early"**

- It expresses the margin as **tolerable control-plane downtime**, which is the quantity an interviewer actually cares about.
- Worked example at 90 days: renewal at day 60 means the issuance path can be completely unavailable for 30 days before the first certificate expires. That is the answer to "what happens if your control plane is down?", pre-computed.
- Worked example at 24 hours: renewal at 50% means 12 hours of tolerable downtime, which is why short-lived certificates use a different fraction — two thirds of a day leaves 8 hours, less than one overnight.
- The generalization worth carrying: **the renewal fraction is a downtime budget, so choose it by how long you need to be able to be broken.**

---

> - “Downloaded is not loaded, and loaded is not serving; only handshake counters retire old trust.”

**What this says in plain words**
Three rungs, each proving strictly less than the next, and only the top one gates the irreversible step.

**Why it matters**

- Worked example of the gap between rung two and rung three: an agent builds a new TLS context and installs it — honestly, successfully — into a context object the listener accepting production traffic does not reference. The ack says `LOADED` and is true. Every handshake still presents the old certificate.
- Nothing in the acknowledgement path can detect that, because nothing in the acknowledgement path observes traffic.
- The sentence to attach: **acknowledgements report what a process believes it did; counters report what actually happened.** Deployment progress may be gated on the first. Retirement, being one-way, must be gated on the second.

---

> - “Publish trust, verify trust, switch leaves, verify serving, retire old trust; forward only, cross-signed so verifiers switch on their own schedule.”

**What this says in plain words**
The whole rollover machine in one breath: five phases, a direction, and the mechanism that makes the middle survivable.

**The three things packed in here**

1. **The phase order.** Trust first, leaves in the middle, trust retirement last.
   - Worked example of the reverse: switch leaves on Monday and distribute trust afterwards. Every client without the new CA fails with `unknown issuer` from the instant the first leaf deploys.
2. **"Forward only."** No reverse gear. Going "back" is a new bundle or deployment generation restoring earlier contents — which is a forward campaign of its own.
3. **"Cross-signed so verifiers switch on their own schedule."** The clause that explains why the middle phases are safe.
   - Worked example: the old CA signs a certificate for the new CA's public key, so one leaf chains to either anchor. A verifier that has loaded the new bundle uses the new anchor; one that has not still builds a valid path to the old one. Nobody has to switch at a particular moment.
   - The caveat to have ready, because it is the standard follow-up: this depends on the client's path builder finding the alternative path, and on AKI/SKI being correct on both intermediates. Cross-signing is a tested compatibility claim, not an assumption.

---

> - “The data plane serves last-known-good from disk; the cache extends availability, never validity.”

**What this says in plain words**
Two clauses: the mechanism, and immediately its limit.

**Why the second clause is what makes the first credible**

- Worked example of the limit: a cached certificate whose `notAfter` was yesterday is cached and useless. The cache means the agent does not need the control plane to keep serving what it has; it cannot extend how long what it has remains valid.
- Which gives the number to quote when asked how long an outage is survivable: **the earliest `notAfter` in the fleet.**
- And the defeater to volunteer in the same breath: a consumer with no on-disk copy that restarts mid-outage cannot reach a serving state, so last-known-good ships in the image or persists to disk.

---

> - Wrap-up script
>   - “The design is a control plane of immutable certificate and bundle versions, an idempotent renewal scheduler, and fenced deployments that consumers pull, verify, and acknowledge.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence one: the architecture in three clauses, each naming a component and the property that makes it work.

**Why it matters**

- "Immutable versions" — nothing points at latest.
- "Idempotent renewal scheduler" — the correctness workload, running at a rate small enough that correctness is the only concern.
- "Fenced deployments that consumers **pull, verify, and acknowledge**" — three verbs covering the whole data-plane contract. Pull gives static stability, verify gives the pre-flight, acknowledge gives the evidence.

---

> -   - “The key invariant is trust before leaves, enforced by a five-phase rollover whose gates are per-cohort serving evidence.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence two: the one invariant, and the two mechanisms that enforce it.

**Why it matters**

- **"Trust before leaves"** is the whole question compressed into three words. If you say one thing about this design, say this.
- **"Five-phase rollover"** is the orchestration-level enforcement.
- **"Per-cohort serving evidence"** is what the gates check, and "per-cohort" is doing real work. Worked example: an aggregate of zero old-chain handshakes could be one cohort that simply stopped reporting. Per-cohort makes that visible as an empty cohort rather than as success.
- Worth adding if there is room: the invariant is also enforced *locally* by each agent's pre-flight, so an orchestration bug becomes one failed ack rather than a fleet outage.

---

> -   - “The main tradeoff is pull with acknowledgments over push: slower propagation, but a data plane that survives a control-plane outage and a retirement gate that proves traffic moved.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence three: what was paid and exactly what was bought.

- **Paid:** slower propagation. A change reaches the fleet over a poll interval rather than in seconds.
- **Bought:** two things, and naming both is what makes the trade sound examined.
  1. A data plane that survives a control-plane outage, because pull forces every agent to hold a local copy.
  2. A retirement gate that proves traffic moved, because acknowledgements and counters exist at all.
- Worked example of the second being non-obvious: push could be fast *and* report success. What it could not easily give is the evidence ladder, because a push model's natural success signal is "the write succeeded", which is desired state rather than observed state.

---

> -   - “My weakest assumption is telemetry completeness; I would reconcile the target registry against the load-balancer inventory before the first rollover.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence four: the flaw, and one specific first action.

**Why it matters**

- Every gate in the design asks "has everyone converged?" That question is unanswerable if the registry does not contain everyone.
- Worked example of the silent failure: the registry holds 200,000 targets; the real fleet is 200,350, because a team stood up a service outside the standard path. All 200,000 registered targets report clean. The gate passes. The 350 break when old trust is retired, and they were invisible at every stage because the system only ever asked about targets it knew.
- **Reconciling against the load-balancer inventory** is the concrete first action, and it is chosen well: the load balancer knows about backends whether or not they registered with the certificate system, so the comparison surfaces exactly the unknown unknowns.
- Ending on a specific, cheap, immediately actionable validation is a good last impression: it says the weakness was not just identified but costed.
