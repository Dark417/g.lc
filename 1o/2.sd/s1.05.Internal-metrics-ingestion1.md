---

> - Why this is hard
>   - Naive: every host posts samples straight to the time-series database and retries on failure.

**What this says in plain words**
The obvious design: each host sends its measurements directly to the database, and retries if the send fails.

**Why it matters**
It is what everyone proposes, so the interviewer is waiting to see whether you know why it fails.

- Worked example of it working fine: 50 hosts, 20 series each, one sample per minute. That is about 17 points per second, which a single database absorbs without noticing. The naive design is correct and costs nothing.

---

> -   - First break: a database slowdown backs up into `100,000` hosts, retries double-count counters, and an in-memory queue acknowledgment loses points on crash.

**What this says in plain words**
Three separate failures in one line.

1. **A database slowdown propagates to every host.**
   - Worked example: the database's write latency rises from 5 milliseconds to 2 seconds. Every one of 100,000 hosts now has a send outstanding for 2 seconds. Their buffers fill, their retry queues grow, and the monitoring agent starts consuming memory on production machines. A storage problem has become a fleet-wide problem on the machines running your actual product.
   - This is coupling: the producer's health is tied to the sink's health with nothing in between.
2. **Retries double-count counters.**
   - A counter is a running total. Worked example: a host reports that its request counter increased by 500. The send times out and is retried. If the database adds 500 twice, the counter now reads 1,000 for 500 real requests. The graph is wrong, and there is nothing in the data to reveal it.
3. **An in-memory queue acknowledgment loses points on crash.**
   - Worked example: the gateway accepts a batch, puts it on an in-memory queue, and returns success. The host deletes its copy. The gateway process is then restarted during a deploy. The queue was memory, so the batch is gone, and the only other copy was deleted because of the acknowledgment. Saying "accepted" before the data is durable is a promise the system cannot keep.

---

> -   - Second break: a consumer crashes after writing but before committing progress, rollups double-add, averaged percentiles lie, and one tenant's label explosion evicts everyone's index.

**What this says in plain words**
Four more failures, and these are the ones the design exists to solve.

1. **Crash between writing and committing progress.**
   - Worked example: the consumer reads records, writes them to the database, and then records how far it got. It crashes after the write and before recording progress. On restart it re-reads the same records and writes them again. Without protection, every aggregate they touched is now double-counted.
2. **Rollups double-add.**
   - A rollup is a pre-computed summary, such as the total per minute. Adding the same batch twice inflates it, and unlike a raw point, a rollup does not carry the identity of what went into it, so the error cannot be detected afterwards.
3. **Averaged percentiles lie.**
   - Worked example: host A's p99 latency is 100 milliseconds over 1,000,000 requests. Host B's p99 is 200 milliseconds over 10 requests. The average of the two numbers is 150 milliseconds. The true combined p99 is essentially 100 milliseconds, because B contributed 10 requests out of a million. The averaged figure is not slightly off; it is a different number with no relationship to reality.
4. **One tenant's label explosion evicts everyone's index.**
   - A series is one named measurement with one specific set of labels. Adding a label with many distinct values creates a separate series for each value.
   - Worked example: a team adds `user_id` as a label on a request counter. With 2,000,000 users, one metric becomes 2,000,000 series. The database's in-memory index, which is shared by every tenant, fills up. Every other team's queries slow down or fail because of one team's instrumentation choice.

---

> -   - Minimum primitives
> -     - A swap-buffer agent
> -     - A partitioned durable log acknowledged after quorum
> -     - Stable point identity
> -     - An atomic dedup-plus-update sink boundary
> -     - A per-tenant cardinality budget.

**What this says in plain words**
Five parts you cannot drop.

1. **A swap-buffer agent.** The agent swaps its collection buffer for an empty one under a short lock, then sends the detached one outside the lock. This is what lets it collect and send at the same time without losing points.
2. **A partitioned durable log acknowledged after quorum.**
   - A log is an append-only sequence of records. Partitioned means split into independent ordered streams for throughput. Quorum means a majority of replicas confirmed the write before it counts as accepted.
   - It is the buffer between producers and the sink, and it is durable, so acknowledging at this point is a promise that can be kept.
3. **Stable point identity.** Every point carries an identity that survives a retry, so a re-sent point is recognizable as the same point rather than a new one.
4. **An atomic dedup-plus-update sink boundary.** Recording "I already applied this batch" and applying it happen as one indivisible action.
5. **A per-tenant cardinality budget.** A cap on how many distinct series one tenant may create.

**Why it matters**
These five give you a recovery path. If you lose your place, walk them, and each leads to a concrete part of the design.

---

> - 🗣 “Acknowledge at the durable log, not the database; then make every downstream write idempotent so replay costs nothing but time.”

**What this says in plain words**
A quotable one-liner; the 🗣 marker means say it roughly verbatim. Two claims:

1. The point at which you tell a producer "accepted" is the durable log, not the final database.
2. Everything after that point is written idempotently, meaning applying it twice has the same effect as applying it once, so re-processing is free except for the time it takes.

**Why it matters**
It states the whole design philosophy in one sentence.

- Moving the acknowledgment boundary earlier decouples producers from the sink.
- Idempotent downstream writes make that safe: since replay is harmless, the system can always recover by re-reading, and no failure requires a decision about whether to risk duplicates.
- Worked example of the payoff: a 2-hour database outage becomes a backlog to drain rather than 2 hours of lost data or 100,000 hosts in distress.

---

> - Opening script
>   - “I will separate durable ingestion from query freshness and show how batching, identity, and bounded recovery fit together.”

**What this says in plain words**
The first sentence. It announces the central separation: data being safely stored and data being visible on a dashboard are two different things with two different guarantees.

**Why it matters**
Worked example of the separation: during a sink outage, ingestion continues and nothing is lost, while dashboards show data that is 2 hours stale. That is a deliberate, stated outcome rather than a failure, and announcing it up front makes every later decision legible.

---

> -   - “First I confirm metric types, the acceptable loss policy, and how stale a dashboard may be.”

**What this says in plain words**
Three questions to ask before designing.

1. **Metric types**, because each aggregates differently.
2. **Loss policy**, because whether data may be dropped before acceptance changes the agent design completely.
3. **Staleness tolerance**, because it sets the freshness target and therefore the catch-up capacity.

---

> -   - “Then I size the log, the catch-up rate, and the cardinality budget from one million points per second.”

**What this says in plain words**
Three estimates, all derived from the one headline number.

**Why it matters**
Naming the three in advance signals that the estimates will drive decisions rather than decorate the answer, which is the discipline the source's rules require.

---

> -   - “The deep dive is the agent flush race and the replay-safe sink boundary.”

**What this says in plain words**
The two deep dives, announced early: how the agent hands off a buffer without losing concurrent writes, and how the sink absorbs a re-delivered record safely.

**Why it matters**
They are the two ends of the pipeline, and they are the two places where a plausible-looking implementation is silently wrong. Pre-announcing also lets the interviewer redirect you at minute 17 rather than minute 40.

**What this says in plain words**
Announces the ending: an operational drill, multi-tenancy, and what changes if the data must not be lost at all.

**Why it matters**
The outage drill is the strongest of the three to volunteer.

## Requirements — IC3 baseline

The numbers warning, the clarifying table, functional scope with metric semantics, assumed scope, the numeric target table, invariants, and non-goals.

---

> - Numerical targets are interview assumptions unless an adjacent primary source states otherwise.

**What this says in plain words**
Every number here was chosen to make the design concrete. None came from Oracle unless a link says so.

**Why it matters**
Worked example: at 1,000,000 points per second you need a partitioned log and admission control. At 1,000 points per second the naive design is correct and the entire discussion is wasted interview time.

---

> | Question | Assumed answer | Why it changes the design |
> |---|---|---|
> | Which metric types? | Counters, gauges, histograms | Each type needs its own aggregation; one count/sum/max record is wrong |
> | Loss policy before acceptance? | Operational telemetry; bounded counted drops allowed at the agent | Permits drop-on-full spool; billing-grade data would forbid it |
> | Durability versus freshness priority? | Accepted data must survive sink outages; freshness may degrade | Forces a durable log before any sink |
> | Query workload? | Dashboards over recent bounded ranges; no full-text log search | Rollups plus raw; series fan-out caps |
> | Multi-tenant? | Yes; internal teams as tenants mapped to compartments | Tenant sits in every series key, budget, and query path |
> | Out-of-order tolerance? | Bounded, about `5 min` | Lateness window, provisional rollups, versioned repair |
> | Who owns the agent? | The platform ships the agent on every host | Producer incarnation and batch identity are ours to define |

**What this says in plain words**
Seven questions, the assumed answer, and what each decides.

- **Row: Which metric types?**
  - Three types, each aggregating differently.
  - Worked example of why one record shape is wrong: a counter's per-window value is a *delta* to be summed. A gauge's is a *sample* whose sum is meaningless, since summing memory-used across ten samples of one host produces a number with no interpretation. A histogram's is a set of bucket counts that merge by adding per bucket. Forcing all three into one count/sum/max record silently produces nonsense for two of the three.
- **Row: Loss policy before acceptance.**
  - Operational telemetry, so bounded counted drops are allowed at the agent before the data is accepted.
  - *Counted* is the crucial word: a drop that increments a visible counter is an observable degradation; a silent drop is data corruption.
  - Worked example of the contrast: for a billing record you must never drop, so the agent has to block or apply backpressure, which pushes the problem into the application being measured. That is a far more invasive design, and it is the right one only when the data justifies it.
- **Row: Durability versus freshness priority.**
  - Accepted data survives sink outages; freshness may degrade.
  - This single answer forces the durable log. Worked example: if freshness mattered more, you would write straight to the database and drop when it is slow, which is the naive design and a legitimate choice for some systems.
- **Row: Query workload.**
  - Dashboards over recent bounded ranges, not searching text.
  - Decides two things: keep rollups as well as raw points, and cap how many series one query may touch.
  - Worked example of the cap's purpose: a query matching 500,000 series over 24 hours would read billions of points. Without a cap, one dashboard can consume the entire query tier.
- **Row: Multi-tenant.**
  - Internal teams as tenants, mapped to compartments, which is Oracle's term for an account's isolation boundary.
  - Tenant identity is part of every series key, every budget, and every query path.
- **Row: Out-of-order tolerance.**
  - Points may arrive up to about 5 minutes late.
  - Worked example of why some lateness is unavoidable: a host's network is briefly down, so it spools locally and sends 3 minutes of backed-up points when it recovers. Those points are old but perfectly valid.
  - This creates the lateness window, provisional rollups, and versioned repair, all of which appear later.
- **Row: Who owns the agent?**
  - The platform ships it, which is a strong position: you define the batch identity, the producer incarnation, and the retry behavior.
  - Worked example of the contrast: if tenants wrote their own agents, you could not assume a stable batch identity, and the gateway would have to defend against arbitrary client behavior rather than cooperating with a known one.

---

> - Functional scope
>   - Accept batched points from an agent on every host.
>   - Clarify the metric semantics.
>     - Counters represent accumulated events and may reset on process restart.
>     - Gauges represent sampled current values.
>     - Histograms represent distributions through mergeable bucket counts.
>     - Do not aggregate every metric into the same count/sum/max record.
>   - Persist accepted points durably before any sink applies them.
>   - Serve bounded dashboard queries over raw points and rollups.
>   - Support authorized replay of a source range into a sink.

**What this says in plain words**
Five capabilities, with the metric semantics spelled out as part of the scope rather than left as an implementation detail.

1. **Accept batched points.** Batched, not individual, which is what makes 1,000,000 points per second only 10,000 requests per second.
2. **The metric semantics**, taken one at a time:
   - **Counters accumulate and may reset.** Worked example: a process has counted 4,000,000 requests, then restarts and its counter is 0. A naive reader sees a drop of 4,000,000 and reports a negative rate. Handling the reset requires knowing that the producer restarted, which is why the producer incarnation travels with the data.
   - **Gauges are samples.** The current value at a moment. The right aggregation is last-value, or an explicitly declared min or max policy, never a sum.
   - **Histograms merge by bucket.** Worked example: host A has 40 requests in the 0-to-10-millisecond bucket, host B has 25 in the same bucket, so the merged bucket is 65. This works only if both use the same bucket boundaries, which is what "compatible" means later.
   - **Do not force one record shape.** The line exists because the naive schema is a single table with count, sum, min and max, and it is wrong for gauges and lossy for histograms.
3. **Persist durably before any sink applies.** The acknowledgment boundary as a functional requirement.
4. **Serve bounded queries over raw and rollups.** Both, because recent debugging needs raw detail and a week-long dashboard needs summaries.
5. **Support authorized replay.** Re-processing a time range on purpose, which is a feature rather than only a recovery path. Worked example: a rollup computation had a bug for three days; fixing it means recomputing those three days from the raw source.

---

> - Assumed scope
>   - `100,000 hosts`.
>   - `100 active series/host`.
>   - One point per series every `10 s`.
>   - Dashboard queries over recent bounded time ranges.
>   - Full-text log search is separate.

**What this says in plain words**
The inputs to every estimate that follows. 100,000 × 100 ÷ 10 gives the headline 1,000,000 points per second.

**Why it matters**
Stating them as a block means the interviewer can challenge one number and you can immediately say what it changes. Worked example: if hosts have 1,000 series rather than 100, everything scales tenfold and the design moves straight to its own 10× stretch case.

> |---|---|---|
> | Sustained ingest | `1,000,000 points/s` | Partitioned log sized with headroom; per-tenant admission budget |

> | Replay log retention | `24 h` | Retention configured above outage plus catch-up plus margin |

**What this says in plain words**
Nine promises, each with a number and the mechanism that keeps it.

- **Row: Sustained ingest, 1,000,000 points per second.** Sustained, not peak.
- **Row: Ingestion availability, 99.95%.** About 21.6 minutes of allowed downtime per 30-day month, held by a stateless gateway fleet spread across fault domains plus a replicated log.
- **Row: Batch acknowledgment latency, p99 under 250 milliseconds.**
  - "Acknowledge after log quorum only; no synchronous database write" is the mechanism *and* the prohibition.
  - Worked example of why the prohibition is explicit: adding a database write to the acknowledgment path makes every producer wait on the database, which reintroduces the coupling the whole design removes. The rule is written into the requirement so it cannot be optimized away later.
- **Row: Accepted-data freshness, p99 under 30 seconds.**
  - Distinct from acknowledgment latency, and the distinction is the design's core separation. Acknowledgment means safely stored, within 250 milliseconds. Freshness means visible on a dashboard, within 30 seconds.
- **Row: Bounded dashboard query, p99 under 2 seconds.**
  - "Bounded" is doing the work: the promise applies to queries within the fan-out and point caps, not to arbitrary queries.
- **Row: Replay log retention, 24 hours.**
  - Enforced by being configured above outage plus catch-up plus margin, which is exactly what the capacity section computes.
- **Row: Lateness window, 5 minutes.** Rollups stay provisional until it closes.
- **Row: Per-tenant active-series budget, 1 million.**
  - Checked at the gateway *before* a new series is created, which is the only place it can be enforced cheaply. Once a series exists in the database's index, the memory is already spent.
- **Row: Control-plane independence, a 30-minute outage drill.**
  - The most carefully worded row in the table. Gateways keep using cached policy, but only for the 30-minute lease period. When the lease expires, a declared fail policy takes over.
  - Worked example of why the lease is bounded: without an expiry, a control plane down for a week leaves gateways enforcing week-old budgets, and a tenant whose budget was revoked keeps consuming. The lease converts indefinite staleness into a bounded window with a defined behavior at the end.

---

> - Invariants
>   - Acknowledged acceptance means the batch reached the chosen durable boundary.
>   - A consumer retry must not double-add an already applied aggregate.
>   - Tenant identity is part of every series and access path.
>   - Queue and cardinality bounds are explicit.
>   - Data before durable acceptance may be dropped only under the documented metrics policy.

**What this says in plain words**
Five statements true at every instant.

1. **Acknowledgment means durable.** Note "the chosen durable boundary": the guarantee is defined relative to a boundary you declared, which is the honest formulation. There is no absolute durability, only durability against a named set of failures.
2. **A retry must not double-add.** The correctness property that makes replay safe.
3. **Tenant identity is in every key and path.** Structural isolation rather than a check someone might forget.
4. **Bounds are explicit.** Every queue has a stated limit and every tenant has a stated series budget. Worked example of the alternative: an unbounded queue does not remove the limit, it relocates it to the moment the machine runs out of memory, which is the worst possible place for it.
5. **Pre-acceptance drops only under the documented policy.** Dropping is permitted, but only in the stated circumstances and only counted.

---

> - Non-goals
>   - Billing or metering records with a no-loss contract.
>   - Full-text log search or trace storage.
>   - Cross-tenant metric federation.

**What this says in plain words**
Three exclusions.

1. **Billing-grade records.** The drop policy makes this system unsuitable, and saying so prevents the data being reused for invoicing later.
2. **Log search and traces.** Different data shapes with different storage and query patterns.
3. **Cross-tenant federation.** Querying across tenants is excluded, which keeps the isolation story simple and absolute.

**Why it matters**
The first is the one that earns its place. Worked example of the risk it heads off: a team discovers the metrics pipeline already carries request counts and decides to bill from it. The drop policy that was acceptable for a dashboard now silently under-bills customers. Declaring it a non-goal is a written record that the data was never fit for that use.

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Counter | A metric that only increases, and resets when its process restarts. | Needs producer incarnation to interpret a reset. |
| Gauge | A metric that is a sampled current value. | Summing it produces a meaningless number. |

## Capacity and consequence — IC3 baseline

Four estimates. Each ends in the decision it forces.

---

> - Input
>   - `100,000 × 100 / 10 = 1,000,000 points/s`.
>   - At an assumed compact `50 B/point`, raw input is `50 MB/s`.
>   - Raw daily volume is `50 MB/s × 86,400 ≈ 4.32 TB/day`.
>   - Three replicated copies imply approximately `12.96 TB` for a day before indexes and overhead.
>   - Compression is workload-dependent and must be measured.
>   - Decision: the log must be partitioned and retention-bounded
>     - `24 h` of replicated raw log is on the order of `13 TB`, so retention is a capacity line item, not a default.

**What this says in plain words**
The arithmetic chain from hosts to terabytes.

1. **Points per second.** 100,000 hosts × 100 series ÷ 10 seconds = 1,000,000 points per second.
2. **Bytes per second.** At 50 bytes each, 50 megabytes per second.
   - "compact" is the qualifier. Worked example: 50 bytes assumes the series identity is a small integer reference and the value is a binary number. A format repeating full label strings on every point could be 500 bytes, making this 500 megabytes per second.
3. **Per day.** 50 megabytes per second × 86,400 seconds ≈ 4.32 terabytes per day.
4. **With three replicas.** About 12.96 terabytes for one day, before indexes.
   - Three copies is the standard replication factor for a durable log: it tolerates one replica failing while a majority still exists.
5. **Compression is unknown until measured.** Metrics compress well in principle, because consecutive values are similar, but the actual ratio depends entirely on the workload.

**Why it matters — the decision**
The log must be partitioned, and its retention is a deliberate budget line.

- **Partitioned** because 50 megabytes per second of sustained writes plus the corresponding reads exceeds what one machine should carry, and partitions are how a log spreads across machines.
- **Retention is a line item, not a default.** Worked example: 24 hours of replicated log is about 13 terabytes. Setting retention to 7 days without thinking makes that 91 terabytes. Retention is one configuration value that moves the storage bill by a factor of seven, so it must be chosen against a requirement rather than left at whatever the default is.

---

> - Batching
>   - One flush per host every `10 s` gives `10,000 batch requests/s`.
>   - In this sampling scenario, batching reduces requests, not the one million logical points.
>   - Local pre-aggregation reduces points only when the original measurements and query semantics allow it.
>   - Decision: the gateway is sized for `10,000 requests/s` of about `5 KB` each
>     - The log and sinks are sized for one million points, and no one may quote the smaller number for the larger system.

**What this says in plain words**
The most important clarification in the section, and the one candidates most often get wrong.

1. **The request rate.** 100,000 hosts ÷ 10 seconds = 10,000 requests per second.
2. **Batching reduces requests, not points.**
   - Worked example: each request carries 100 points, so 10,000 requests per second still delivers 1,000,000 points per second. The points did not go anywhere; they are travelling together.
3. **Pre-aggregation can reduce points, but only sometimes.**
   - Worked example where it works: a host records 10 request-duration samples in 10 seconds and sends one histogram with the bucket counts. Ten points became one, and no query answerable from the histogram lost accuracy.
   - Worked example where it does not: a gauge sampled every second, and a dashboard that needs per-second resolution. Averaging ten samples into one destroys exactly the detail the query needs. The aggregation is only valid if no supported query can tell the difference.

**Why it matters — the decision**
Two different components are sized from two different numbers, and the last clause is a warning about a specific mistake.

- The gateway handles 10,000 requests per second of about 5 kilobytes each.
- The log and sinks handle 1,000,000 points per second.
- Worked example of the mistake: a candidate says "batching gives us 10,000 per second, which is easy" and sizes the database for 10,000 writes per second. The database receives 1,000,000 points per second and is undersized by a hundredfold. The number was correct for one component and catastrophically wrong for another.

---

> - Recovery capacity
>   - A `2 h` sink outage creates `7.2 billion` points of backlog.
>   - A recovery rate of `1.5 million/s` leaves `0.5 million/s` for catch-up.
>   - Catch-up takes `14,400 s = 4 h` while live traffic continues.
>   - The oldest affected record waits about six hours from outage start to full recovery.
>   - Retention must cover outage plus catch-up plus operational margin.
>   - Decision: provision sink write capacity at `1.5×` live rate and set log retention to `24 h`
>     - A `2 h` outage then consumes `6 h` of the retention window and leaves margin.

**What this says in plain words**
The arithmetic every candidate skips, and the one that produces the most surprising result.

1. **The backlog.** 2 hours × 3,600 seconds × 1,000,000 points per second = 7.2 billion points.
2. **Catch-up capacity is the surplus, not the total.**
   - This is the step that trips people. The sink can write 1,500,000 per second, but live traffic is still arriving at 1,000,000 per second and must also be written. Only the difference, 500,000 per second, works on the backlog.
3. **Drain time.** 7.2 billion ÷ 500,000 per second = 14,400 seconds = 4 hours.
4. **Total elapsed: about 6 hours.** The oldest record was written 2 hours before the outage ended and waits another 4 hours to be processed.
   - Worked example of the shape: a 2-hour outage is a 6-hour incident. The recovery is twice as long as the failure, and during those 4 hours the system is degraded but appears to be "recovering fine".

**Why it matters — the decision**
Two numbers, chosen together.

- **1.5× sink capacity.** Worked example of the sensitivity: at 1.1× the surplus is only 100,000 per second, so 7.2 billion points take 72,000 seconds, which is 20 hours. The same 2-hour outage becomes a 22-hour incident, and it nearly exhausts a 24-hour retention window. A small change in headroom produces a fivefold change in recovery time, which is why this is later named the weakest assumption.
- **24-hour retention.** A 2-hour outage consumes 6 hours of it, leaving 18 hours of margin. Retention must cover outage plus catch-up plus margin, because a record that ages out of the log before being processed is lost permanently.

---

> - Series cardinality
>   - `10 million` active series can dominate TSDB memory and indexes.
>   - Bound label combinations before accepting a new series.
>   - Decision: admission control on new series is a gateway function with a per-tenant budget, because index memory is shared and cannot be reclaimed quickly.

**What this says in plain words**

1. **The count.** 100,000 hosts × 100 series each = 10,000,000 active series.
2. **Series count drives index memory.**
   - Worked example: a time-series database keeps an in-memory index mapping label combinations to series. At roughly 1 kilobyte of index per series, 10,000,000 series is about 10 gigabytes of memory used purely for lookup, before any data.
3. **Bound the label combinations before accepting.** The check happens at series creation, not at query time.

**Why it matters — the decision**
Admission control belongs at the gateway, with a per-tenant budget, and the reason given is the key part: the index is shared and cannot be reclaimed quickly.

- Worked example of the failure it prevents: a team deploys code adding `request_id` as a label. Each request creates a new series. Within minutes, millions of new series exist. They are useless, each holding a single point, and they have consumed the shared index memory that every other tenant's queries depend on.
- "cannot be reclaimed quickly" is the operational reality that makes prevention the only option. Deleting series is slow, often requiring a compaction, and until it completes every tenant is degraded. Once the memory is spent there is no fast undo, so the check has to happen before the series exists.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Point | One numeric sample for one series at one time. | The unit every estimate counts. |
| Replication factor | How many copies of each record the log keeps. | Three here, which triples storage. |
| Compression ratio | How much smaller data becomes when compressed. | Workload-dependent, so it is not assumed. |
| Retention | How long the log keeps records before deleting them. | A capacity line item, and a hard deadline for recovery. |
| Partitioned log | A log split into independent ordered streams across machines. | How it exceeds one machine's throughput. |
| Batch | Many points sent in one request. | Reduces requests, never points. |
| Pre-aggregation | Combining measurements at the source before sending. | Reduces points only when no supported query can tell. |
| Logical point | One measurement, regardless of how it is packaged for transport. | The number that sizes the log and sinks. |
| Backlog | Accepted data not yet processed by the sink. | What a sink outage accumulates. |
| Catch-up rate | The surplus capacity above live traffic, available to drain a backlog. | The number people mistake for total capacity. |
| Headroom | Capacity provisioned above the steady-state requirement. | 1.5× here, and the design's weakest assumption. |
| Retention loss | Data deleted from the log before it was ever processed. | The permanent failure retention margin exists to prevent. |
| Active series | A series currently receiving points. | The unit of index memory. |
| Index memory | Memory the database uses to map labels to series, before storing any values. | The shared resource one tenant can exhaust. |
| Cardinality explosion | A sudden large increase in distinct series, usually from a high-cardinality label. | The failure admission control prevents. |
| Admission control | Rejecting something before it consumes a resource, rather than cleaning up after. | The only workable defence when the resource cannot be reclaimed quickly. |
| Compaction | The background process that reorganizes stored data, and the slow path for reclaiming space. | Why spent index memory has no fast undo. |

## Architecture and flow — IC3 baseline

Diagram first, then each component with the mechanism it buys and the price it charges, then one batch traced end to end.

---

> ```text
> Agent -> authenticated batch gateway -> partitioned durable log
>   |                                           |
> bounded local spool              +------------+-------------+
>                                  v                          v
>                           raw-point consumer         rollup consumer
>                                  |                          |
>                                  +------ TSDB / blocks -----+
>                                                 ^
> Dashboard -> authorized query tier -> query budget / cache
>
> Series catalog / policy store (control plane) -> cached snapshots at gateways
> Permanent record errors -> quarantine / DLQ -> controlled replay
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
One write path, one read path, and two supporting paths.

**Reading the write path**

1. **Agent → authenticated batch gateway.** The host's collector sends batches; the gateway verifies who is calling.
2. **Agent → bounded local spool.** The branch downward is the agent's own disk buffer, used when the gateway is unreachable. *Bounded* means it has a size limit and a maximum age.
3. **Gateway → partitioned durable log.** The gateway appends and only then acknowledges.
4. **The log fans out to two consumers.**
   - The raw-point consumer writes individual points.
   - The rollup consumer computes windowed summaries.
   - They are separate because they have different costs and different failure consequences. Worked example: the rollup consumer falls behind during a heavy window. Raw points keep flowing, so recent debugging still works while long-range dashboards go stale. One shared consumer would make both fail together.
5. **Both write to the TSDB or storage blocks.**

**Reading the read path**
Dashboard → authorized query tier → query budget and cache. Queries do not touch the log or the consumers; they only read committed storage.

**Reading the supporting paths**

- **Series catalog and policy store**, marked control plane, feeding cached snapshots to the gateways. The arrow direction matters: policy flows out to the gateways, and the gateways do not call back on the request path.
- **Permanent record errors → quarantine → controlled replay.** A separate path for data that will never succeed, kept out of the main flow so it cannot block it.

**Why it matters**
The diagram encodes the design's two structural claims before a word is spoken: the log sits between producers and sinks so neither can stall the other, and the control plane feeds the data plane one way only.

---

> - Agent
>   - Buys concurrent-safe batching through an atomic buffer swap and a bounded spool.
>   - Cost: bounded local loss before acceptance under the documented drop policy.

**What this says in plain words**
The agent collects measurements while simultaneously sending previous ones, using a buffer swap, and keeps a size-limited disk spool for when sending fails.
The price is that data can be lost on that host before it was ever accepted.

**Why it matters**
The cost is stated plainly rather than hidden, which is the pattern every component follows here.

- Worked example of the loss: the gateway is unreachable for 20 minutes and the 64-megabyte spool fills. Further points are dropped and counted. The host's own metrics for that window have a hole, and a counter says how large the hole is.
- Acceptable because this is operational telemetry, which the requirements established. For a billing record it would not be.

---

> - Gateway
>   - Validates schema and payload bounds before durable acceptance.
>   - Applies tenant admission budgets.
>   - Acknowledges only after the log reports the required durability condition.
>   - Buys a single admission point for cardinality and tenancy.
>   - Cost: one extra hop and the `250 ms` acknowledgment budget must include the log quorum write.

**What this says in plain words**
Three jobs, one benefit, one cost.

1. **Validate schema and payload bounds.** Before anything durable happens, so malformed data never enters the log.
   - Worked example of the ordering mattering: if validation happened after the append, every consumer would have to handle malformed records forever, because the log is immutable and the bad records cannot be removed.
2. **Apply tenant admission budgets.** Ingest rate and new-series creation, checked here.
3. **Acknowledge only after durability.** The promise is made only once it can be kept.
4. **The benefit: one admission point.**
   - Worked example of why one point matters: with cardinality checked at the gateway, there is exactly one place to enforce it. If consumers also created series, a tenant could bypass the budget through any path that skipped the gateway.
5. **The cost: a hop, and the acknowledgment budget includes the quorum write.**
   - Worked example of the budget: 250 milliseconds total, of which the log's quorum write might be 20 to 50 milliseconds. Validation and routing must fit in the rest, which is generous but not unlimited.

---

> - Durable log
>   - Decouples short storage outages from ingestion.
>   - Supplies ordered partitions and replay.
>   - It has finite retention and capacity.
>   - Cost: retention bytes, partition management, and a second system to operate.

**What this says in plain words**
The log buys three things and costs three things, listed together.

1. **Decoupling.** A database outage stops the consumers, not the producers.
2. **Ordered partitions and replay.** Records within a partition have a fixed order, and any consumer can re-read from any point.
3. **Finite retention and capacity.** Stated as a property, not a caveat, because it creates a hard deadline: unprocessed data ages out and is gone.
4. **The cost: bytes, partition management, and an extra system.**
   - "a second system to operate" is the honest one. Worked example: the log needs its own capacity planning, its own upgrades, its own on-call knowledge, and its own failure modes. It is not free infrastructure, and a small deployment is better off without it, which is what the alternatives table says.

---

> - Consumers
>   - Raw-point and rollup consumers apply idempotent writes and then advance recoverable progress.
>   - Buys replay safety and independent scaling of raw versus rollup paths.
>   - Cost: at-least-once delivery, so every sink write must carry a unique application identity.

**What this says in plain words**
The ordering in the first line is the whole correctness story: write first, record progress second.

- Worked example of the two orderings:
  - **Progress first, write second.** Crash between them, and on restart the consumer resumes past records it never wrote. The data is lost silently.
  - **Write first, progress second.** Crash between them, and on restart the consumer re-writes records it already wrote. The data is duplicated, which the unique identity absorbs.
- Given a choice between losing and duplicating, choose duplicating, because duplication has a cure and loss does not.
- The cost line names the obligation this creates: at-least-once delivery means every sink write must carry an identity that makes a repeat detectable.

---

> - Query tier
>   - Uses independent workers and query budgets.
>   - Separate processes do not automatically isolate shared disk, memory, or storage bandwidth.
>   - Reserve storage capacity or use isolated read resources for expensive queries.
>   - Cost: bounded results and freshness metadata replace the illusion of complete, current data.

**What this says in plain words**
Four points, and the second is the one that shows operational experience.

1. **Independent workers and budgets.** The obvious isolation.
2. **Separate processes do not isolate shared storage.**
   - Worked example: the query workers run in their own containers with their own CPU and memory, which looks isolated. They read from the same disks the ingestion path writes to. A large query saturates disk read bandwidth, ingestion writes slow down, consumer lag grows, and freshness degrades. The process boundary provided no protection at all for the resource that actually mattered.
3. **So reserve capacity or use isolated read resources.** Read replicas, or storage-level reservations. Real isolation must exist at the level of the contended resource.
4. **The cost: bounded results and freshness metadata.**
   - Worked example of what the user sees: instead of "here is your data", the response says "here are 10,000 series, truncated from 50,000, current as of 30 seconds ago". Less convenient, and true. The phrase "the illusion of complete, current data" is precise: an unbounded query system does not actually provide completeness, it just does not tell you when it failed to.

---

> - Control plane versus data plane
>   - Series catalog, tenant budgets, and retention policies live in the control plane.
>   - Gateways and consumers hold cached snapshots so acceptance continues when the catalog is unavailable.
>   - Cost: a budget change propagates with snapshot delay rather than instantly.

**What this says in plain words**
Configuration lives in the control plane; the data plane holds cached copies so it keeps working when the control plane is down.
The cost is that a budget change is not instant.

**Why it matters**
Worked example of the payoff: the catalog is down for 30 minutes. Gateways keep accepting 1,000,000 points per second against their cached budgets. What stops is changing a budget or definitively resolving a series never seen before.

- Worked example of the cost: a tenant's series budget is reduced at 14:00. Gateways adopt it as their snapshots refresh, so for a bounded window some gateways still enforce the old, larger budget.
- Bounded by the 30-minute lease from the requirements table, which is what keeps "cached" from becoming "indefinitely stale".

---

> → Atomically swap the agent's active buffer for a fresh buffer.  
> → Serialize the detached batch outside its short lock.  
> → Send a stable batch identity with retry and jitter.  
> → Validate and durably append at the gateway.  
> → Acknowledge acceptance and release the local retry copy.  
> → Consumers apply idempotent writes before advancing recoverable progress.  
> → Queries read committed raw points or compatible rollups with freshness metadata.

**What this says in plain words**
One batch traced end to end. Walked concretely:

Host `web-0042` for tenant `platform` has collected 100 points over 10 seconds.

1. **Atomically swap the buffer.**
   - Under a short lock, the active buffer is detached and an empty one installed. Recording threads immediately use the new buffer.
   - Worked example of the lock duration: swapping two references takes nanoseconds. Serializing 100 points to bytes might take a millisecond. Holding the lock for the swap alone rather than the serialization is a thousandfold difference in contention.
2. **Serialize outside the lock.**
   - The detached buffer is now private to the flushing thread, so nothing else can touch it and no lock is needed.
3. **Send with a stable batch identity, retry, and jitter.**
   - The identity survives retries. Jitter prevents 100,000 hosts retrying in unison.
4. **Validate and durably append.**
   - The gateway checks schema and budgets, then appends to the log and waits for quorum.
5. **Acknowledge and release the local copy.**
   - Only now does the agent discard its copy. Worked example of the alternative: releasing before acknowledgment means a gateway crash loses data that no longer exists anywhere else.
6. **Consumers write idempotently, then advance progress.**
   - The ordering from above, appearing in the flow.
7. **Queries read committed data with freshness metadata.**
   - "committed" excludes anything still in flight, and the freshness marker says how current the answer is.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Bounded spool | The agent's local disk buffer, limited by both size and age. | Absorbs a short gateway outage, and drops beyond its limit. |
| Buffer swap | Exchanging the active collection buffer for an empty one under a short lock. | Makes the lock hold time independent of batch size. |
| Quorum write | An append confirmed by a majority of replicas. | The durability condition acknowledgment waits for. |
| Admission point | The single place where budgets and cardinality are enforced. | One place to enforce means no path can bypass it. |
| Partition | One independent ordered stream within the log. | Provides ordering within it and parallelism across them. |
| Replay | Re-reading log records and re-applying them. | The universal recovery mechanism. |
| At-least-once delivery | Every record is delivered one or more times, never zero. | The consequence of writing before committing progress. |
| Recoverable progress | A durable record of how far a consumer has processed. | Advanced only after the write it covers. |
| Idempotent write | A write whose repetition changes nothing. | What makes at-least-once safe. |
| Unique application identity | An identifier carried into the sink so a repeat is recognizable. | The obligation at-least-once creates. |
| Query budget | A per-tenant limit on how much work a query may consume. | Stops one dashboard consuming the query tier. |
| Resource contention | Two workloads competing for the same underlying resource despite process isolation. | Why separate containers do not isolate shared disks. |
| Read replica | A separate copy of the data serving reads only. | Real isolation for expensive queries. |
| Freshness metadata | A marker on a response saying how current the data is. | Replaces the illusion of completeness with a stated bound. |
| Control plane | The part managing configuration: catalog, budgets, retention. | Feeds cached snapshots one way to the data plane. |
| Snapshot delay | The bounded lag before a configuration change reaches every gateway. | The price of caching policy locally. |
| Quarantine / DLQ | A separate destination for records that can never succeed. | Keeps permanently-bad data from blocking the main flow. |
| Jitter | Randomizing retry timing so clients do not synchronize. | Prevents 100,000 hosts retrying in unison. |

## API and data model — IC3 baseline

The interface table, the identity table, the reference SQL, what it omits, the companion deviations, and two blocks on partitioning and routing.

---

> | Surface | Contract | Conflict/error behavior |
> |---|---|---|
> | `POST /v1/metric-batches` | Batch ID, producer incarnation, sequence, points | `202` after durable append; same batch ID returns the prior result; partial acceptance is reported explicitly or the whole batch fails |
> | `GET /v1/metric-batches/{producer}/{batch}` | Acceptance and processing outcome within retention | `404` after retention; authoritative answer after a lost `202` |
> | `GET /v1/query` | Tenant-scoped selector, range, resolution | `422` over fan-out or point caps; response carries freshness watermark |
> | `GET /v1/series` | Paginated names/labels | Prevent arbitrary cross-tenant enumeration; cursor scoped to tenant |
> | `POST /v1/replays` | Restricted source range and transform version | Idempotency key, rate limit, and audit; `409` on overlapping active replay |

**What this says in plain words**
Five interfaces: one write, one write-status lookup, two reads, and one administrative action.

- **Row: `POST /v1/metric-batches`.**
  - Carries a batch identifier, the producer's incarnation, a sequence number, and the points.
  - `202` means accepted for processing, returned after the durable append. It deliberately does not claim the data is queryable yet, which is the acknowledgment-versus-freshness split expressed in a status code.
  - "same batch ID returns the prior result" makes retries safe: a resend gets the original outcome rather than being appended again.
  - **Partial acceptance is reported explicitly, or the whole batch fails.** The two honest options.
    - Worked example of the dishonest third option: 100 points arrive, 97 append successfully and 3 fail. Returning plain success means the agent releases all 100 and 3 are silently gone. Either report "97 accepted, 3 rejected, here is why" or fail the batch so the agent retries all 100.
- **Row: `GET /v1/metric-batches/{producer}/{batch}`.**
  - Exists for one specific situation, named in the table: a lost `202`.
  - Worked example: the gateway appended successfully and the response was lost in the network. The agent does not know whether to resend. Resending risks duplication; not resending risks loss. This endpoint answers the question authoritatively.
  - `404` after retention is the honest limit: once the record expires, the system genuinely does not know, and saying so beats guessing.
- **Row: `GET /v1/query`.**
  - `422` means the request was well-formed but cannot be processed, which is the right code for a query that exceeds its caps. It tells the caller to narrow the query rather than to retry it.
  - "freshness watermark" is a timestamp on the response saying data is complete up to that point.
  - Worked example of its value: a dashboard shows a flat line for the last 5 minutes. Is the service idle, or is the pipeline behind? The watermark answers it: data is complete only up to 5 minutes ago, so the flat line is missing data, not zero traffic.
- **Row: `GET /v1/series`.**
  - Lists series names and labels, paginated, with the cursor scoped to the tenant.
  - Worked example of the leak it prevents: an unscoped cursor could be edited to walk into another tenant's series. Series names alone leak a great deal, such as internal service names and deployment structure.
- **Row: `POST /v1/replays`.**
  - Re-processes a source range, protected four ways: an idempotency key, a rate limit, an audit record, and `409` on an overlapping active replay.
  - Worked example of the overlap check earning its place: one replay is running over 10:00 to 12:00 and another is requested for 11:00 to 13:00. Both would write contributions for the 11:00-to-12:00 overlap concurrently. The unique contribution key makes that safe rather than corrupting, but it is wasted work and confusing to observe, so the conflict is rejected outright.

---

> | State | Identity/key | Invariant |
> |---|---|---|
> | Series | `(tenant, canonical name, sorted labels)` | Full canonical identity resolves hash collisions |
> | Logical point | `(series, producer incarnation, sample sequence)` | Retry keeps identity; source timestamp alone may collide |
> | Log record | `(partition, offset)` | Recovery coordinate for this append, not cross-replay business identity |
> | Rollup contribution | `(window, series, source batch ID)` | Unique application boundary prevents double addition |
> | Materialized window | `(tenant, series, window start, resolution, version)` | Rebuild can replace a complete version atomically |

**What this says in plain words**
Five identities, and choosing each one correctly is most of the design.

- **Row: Series.**
  - Identified by tenant, canonical name, and labels in sorted order.
  - *Sorted* matters: `{a=1,b=2}` and `{b=2,a=1}` are the same series and must produce the same identity, so the labels are canonicalized before hashing.
  - "Full canonical identity resolves hash collisions" means the hash is a lookup shortcut, and the real identity is the full text, checked on match.
  - Worked example: two different label sets hash to the same value. Without the full comparison, one series' points are silently added to the other's, and the graph mixes two unrelated measurements with nothing to indicate it.
- **Row: Logical point.**
  - Identified by series, producer incarnation, and sample sequence, explicitly *not* by timestamp.
  - Worked example of why the timestamp is insufficient: two processes on one host both report the same series at the same millisecond. Their points have identical timestamps and are different measurements. The incarnation distinguishes the processes and the sequence orders within each.
  - "Retry keeps identity" means a re-sent point carries the same triple, so the sink recognizes it.
- **Row: Log record.**
  - Identified by partition and offset, and the invariant is a warning rather than a property.
  - Worked example of the trap: using `(partition, offset)` as the business identity works until a deliberate replay re-appends the same data at new offsets. The same logical points now have different identities and are applied twice. The offset says where a record sits in the log, not what it means.
- **Row: Rollup contribution.**
  - Identified by window, series, and source batch identifier, and this is the unique application boundary.
  - Worked example: batch `b-8891` contributes 40 samples to the 14:00 window for series X. The consumer crashes and re-processes it. Inserting the contribution conflicts on the key, so the addition is skipped and the total stays correct.
- **Row: Materialized window.**
  - Identified by tenant, series, window start, resolution, and version.
  - The version is what makes a rebuild safe. Worked example: a rollup for 14:00 was computed with a buggy transform. Rather than editing it, compute version 2 completely and switch readers to it. Readers see either the complete old version or the complete new one, never a half-rebuilt window.

> - Reference DDL
>   - Keys and uniqueness match the state table above and the [companion schema](../../1sde/1.backend/2.rest102.md#infra-5).
>   - Logical points and log records live in the partitioned log and the TSDB; SQL holds the series catalog, batch outcomes, rollup contributions and windows, and replay records.

**What this says in plain words**
The SQL covers the catalog and the bookkeeping. The high-volume data lives in the log and the time-series store, stated explicitly so the reader does not look for a points table.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE metric_series (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, series_id text NOT NULL,            -- hash of the canonical identity; never trusted alone
>   name text NOT NULL, canonical_labels text NOT NULL,          -- sorted label pairs; resolves hash collisions
>   labels jsonb NOT NULL, created_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, series_id),
>   UNIQUE (tenant_id, name, canonical_labels)
> );
> CREATE INDEX metric_series_by_name ON metric_series (tenant_id, name, series_id);
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The series catalog, with two keys doing two different jobs.

- **`series_id` as primary key**, a hash of the canonical identity, with the comment "never trusted alone".
  - It is compact, so other tables reference it cheaply instead of repeating long label strings.
- **`UNIQUE (tenant_id, name, canonical_labels)`** is the real identity.
  - Worked example of the two working together: a new series arrives, its hash is computed and looked up, and the full name and labels are compared against the stored row. A match means the same series; a mismatch means a hash collision, and the unique constraint prevents the wrong row being reused.
- **`labels jsonb` alongside `canonical_labels text`.** The text form is for identity and comparison; the structured form is for querying by individual label.
- **`metric_series_by_name` on `(tenant_id, name, series_id)`.** Serves "all series with this metric name for this tenant", which is what a query selector resolves first.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE metric_batches (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, producer_id text NOT NULL, batch_id text NOT NULL,
>   producer_incarnation text NOT NULL, sequence bigint NOT NULL CHECK (sequence >= 0),
>   payload_hash text NOT NULL,                                  -- same batch_id with a different hash is 409
>   state text NOT NULL CHECK (state IN ('ACCEPTED','PARTIAL','REJECTED','PROCESSED')),
>   accepted_count integer NOT NULL CHECK (accepted_count >= 0),
>   rejected_count integer NOT NULL CHECK (rejected_count >= 0),
>   accepted_at timestamptz NOT NULL, expires_at timestamptz NOT NULL, -- 404 after retention
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, producer_id, batch_id),              -- lost 202: the retry reads the prior result
>   CHECK (expires_at > accepted_at)
> );
> CREATE INDEX metric_batches_expiry ON metric_batches (expires_at);
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
One row per batch, recording what happened to it.

- **`payload_hash`** with the comment "same batch_id with a different hash is 409".
  - Worked example of the attack or bug it catches: an agent reuses batch identifier `b-8891` for entirely different points. Without the hash, the gateway would return the earlier result and silently discard the new data. The hash detects that the identity was reused for different content and rejects it as a conflict.
- **`state` restricted to four values.** `ACCEPTED` means durably appended, `PARTIAL` means some points rejected, `REJECTED` means none accepted, `PROCESSED` means consumers have applied it.
  - Separating `ACCEPTED` from `PROCESSED` is the acknowledgment-versus-freshness split appearing in the schema.
- **`accepted_count` and `rejected_count`.** The explicit partial-acceptance report from the interface table, stored so it can be re-read.
- **`expires_at`** with "404 after retention", making the honest limit a schema field rather than a convention.
- **`PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, producer_id, batch_id)`** with "lost 202: the retry reads the prior result", naming the exact scenario the key serves.
- **`metric_batches_expiry` on `expires_at`** so the cleanup job can find expired rows without scanning the table.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE rollup_contributions (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, series_id text NOT NULL,
>   resolution_s integer NOT NULL CHECK (resolution_s > 0), window_start timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   source_batch_id text NOT NULL,                               -- unique application boundary
>   sample_count bigint NOT NULL CHECK (sample_count >= 0),
>   sum_value double precision NOT NULL, min_value double precision NOT NULL, max_value double precision NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, series_id, resolution_s, window_start, source_batch_id), -- duplicate insert is a no-op
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, series_id) REFERENCES metric_series
> );
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The most important table in the schema: what each batch contributed to each window.

- **The primary key includes `source_batch_id`**, with the comment "duplicate insert is a no-op".
  - This is the entire replay-safety mechanism, expressed as a database constraint.
  - Worked example: batch `b-8891` contributed 40 samples summing to 812 milliseconds to the 14:00 window for series X. A consumer crash causes re-processing. The insert conflicts on the primary key and is ignored, so the aggregate is untouched.
  - The property that matters: the guarantee is enforced by the database, not by application logic. An application-level "have I seen this?" check has a window between the check and the write where a concurrent duplicate slips through.
- **`sample_count`, `sum_value`, `min_value`, `max_value`.** The mergeable summary values. Notably there is no average column, because an average is derived as sum ÷ count at read time. Storing an average would make merging impossible, since averages cannot be combined without their counts.
- **`resolution_s CHECK (> 0)`.** A resolution of zero seconds is meaningless and the database refuses it.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE rollup_windows (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, series_id text NOT NULL,
>   resolution_s integer NOT NULL CHECK (resolution_s > 0), window_start timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   version bigint NOT NULL CHECK (version > 0),                 -- a rebuild replaces a complete version atomically
>   sample_count bigint NOT NULL CHECK (sample_count >= 0),
>   sum_value double precision NOT NULL, min_value double precision NOT NULL, max_value double precision NOT NULL,
>   histogram jsonb,                                             -- mergeable bucket counts, never percentiles
>   provisional boolean NOT NULL,                                -- true until the lateness window closes
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, series_id, resolution_s, window_start, version),
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, series_id) REFERENCES metric_series
> );
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The computed summaries that queries actually read, distinct from the contributions that build them.

- **`version` in the primary key**, with "a rebuild replaces a complete version atomically".
  - Worked example: the 14:00 window exists at version 1. A corrected transform computes version 2 fully, and only then are readers pointed at it. There is never a moment when a reader sees a partially rebuilt window.
- **`histogram jsonb`** with the comment "mergeable bucket counts, never percentiles".
  - The comment is a rule enforced by convention rather than by the database, and it is the schema's answer to the averaged-percentile problem.
  - Worked example: storing `{p50: 20, p99: 180}` per host makes the data unmergeable forever, because those numbers cannot be combined. Storing `{0-10ms: 400, 10-50ms: 220, 50-100ms: 31}` allows any later percentile to be computed from the merged buckets.
- **`provisional boolean`** with "true until the lateness window closes".
  - Worked example: the 14:00 window is computed at 14:01 and marked provisional. A point timestamped 14:00:30 arrives at 14:04, inside the 5-minute lateness window, and is incorporated. At 14:05 the window closes and becomes final.
  - A query reading a provisional window knows the value may still change, which is a materially different thing to show on a dashboard than a settled number.

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE metric_replays (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, replay_id text NOT NULL, idempotency_key text NOT NULL,
>   source_from timestamptz NOT NULL, source_to timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   transform_version text NOT NULL, actor_id text NOT NULL,
>   state text NOT NULL CHECK (state IN ('ACCEPTED','RUNNING','SUCCEEDED','FAILED','CANCELLED')),
>   created_at timestamptz NOT NULL, updated_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, replay_id),
>   UNIQUE (tenant_id, idempotency_key),
>   CHECK (source_to > source_from)
> );
> CREATE INDEX metric_replays_active ON metric_replays (tenant_id, source_from, source_to)
>   WHERE state IN ('ACCEPTED','RUNNING');                       -- overlap check behind the 409
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
One row per replay operation, with its range, its transform version, and who requested it.

- **`transform_version`.** Which computation the replay applies, so you can tell afterwards which version produced which data.
- **`actor_id`.** Replay is a privileged, expensive operation, so who requested it is recorded.
- **`UNIQUE (tenant_id, idempotency_key)`.** A retried replay request does not start a second replay.
- **`metric_replays_active`, a partial index.**
  - A partial index covers only rows matching its `WHERE` clause, so this one contains only replays that are accepted or running, typically a handful of rows rather than the full history.
  - Worked example: 50,000 completed replays over two years, and 2 currently running. The overlap check queries only those 2, so it stays fast forever rather than degrading as history accumulates.

---

> - Not captured by the DDL
>   - Logical points `(series, producer incarnation, sample sequence)` are TSDB samples partitioned by `(tenant, series_id, time_bucket)`; log records `(partition, offset)` are recovery coordinates that never become rows.
>   - Consumer progress is the committed log offset; `rollup_contributions` is the correctness boundary that absorbs a redelivered record.
>   - Tenant series and label budgets are control-plane configuration that gateways cache as snapshots; enforcing them is a count against `metric_series`, not a constraint.

**What this says in plain words**
Three deliberate absences.

1. **Points are TSDB samples, and log offsets never become rows.** The 1,000,000 points per second never touch this schema.
2. **Progress is the log offset; the contributions table is where correctness lives.**
   - The two-sentence summary of replay safety: the offset says where you are, and the contribution key says what has already been applied. Only the second is authoritative about correctness.
3. **Budgets are configuration, enforced by counting, not by a constraint.**
   - Worked example of why a constraint cannot do it: a database check cannot express "this tenant may have at most 1,000,000 rows in this table" without counting them on every insert, which at scale is prohibitive. The gateway holds a cached count and a cached budget and decides before creating the series.

---

> - Deviation from the companion
>   - The file's series identity `(tenant, canonical name, sorted labels)` is the `UNIQUE` constraint; `series_id` stays the primary key so the rollup tables carry a compact foreign key.
>   - `metric_batches` gains `producer_incarnation`, `sequence`, counts, and `expires_at` for the `202` and `404`-after-retention contract; `rollup_contributions`, `rollup_windows`, and `metric_replays` are added for the state table rows and `POST /replays`.
>   - The replay overlap check is a partial index plus an application query; a range `EXCLUDE` constraint is the stricter alternative.

**What this says in plain words**
Three differences from the shared schema, each with its reason.

1. **Identity is the unique constraint; the hash is the primary key**, so referencing tables stay compact.
2. **Extra columns and tables** added to support contracts the companion did not model.
3. **The overlap check is advisory, and the stricter option is named.**
   - An `EXCLUDE` constraint can make overlapping ranges impossible at the database level, which is genuinely stronger than an index plus a query.
   - Worked example of the difference: two replay requests arrive simultaneously for overlapping ranges. With the index-and-query approach, both queries can run before either inserts, and both succeed. With an `EXCLUDE` constraint, the second insert fails. The weaker option is chosen for portability, and naming the stronger one is the honest move.

---

> - Partition by stable series identity.
>   - A consumer may own multiple partitions.
>   - Ordinary consumer-group parallelism is bounded by partition count.
>   - Splitting one series across partitions abandons simple per-series ordering.

**What this says in plain words**
Three consequences of how the log is partitioned.

1. **Partition by series identity**, so all points for one series land in one partition and stay in order.
2. **One consumer can own several partitions**, so consumers and partitions are not one-to-one.
3. **Parallelism is capped by partition count.**
   - Worked example: 50 partitions means at most 50 consumers doing useful work. A 51st sits idle. So partition count is a capacity decision made up front, and increasing it later reshuffles which series live where.
4. **Splitting a series across partitions loses ordering.**
   - Worked example: series X's points go to partitions 3 and 7. Nothing orders records across partitions, so a point from 14:00:05 may be processed before one from 14:00:03. For counters, where order determines the delta interpretation, that is a correctness problem rather than an inconvenience.

---

> - Batch routing
>   - A host batch may contain many series.
>   - Gateway can fan records into their series partitions.
>   - Partial append failure requires retry-safe point identity.
>   - Do not acknowledge a whole batch when only some required records are durable unless the API reports partial acceptance.
>   - OCI Monitoring's `PostMetricData` exposes a batch-atomicity choice for this exact case
>   - Treat that as documented OCI behavior and the routing above as proposed design.

**What this says in plain words**
The complication created by the two previous decisions together.

1. **A host batch spans many series.** One host sends 100 points covering 100 different series.
2. **The gateway fans them into per-series partitions.** So one incoming request becomes appends to many partitions.
3. **Partial append failure is therefore possible.**
   - Worked example: 100 points fan out to 12 partitions. Ten partitions accept, two are unavailable. 83 points are durable and 17 are not.
4. **So do not acknowledge the whole batch.** Either report partial acceptance explicitly, or fail the whole batch so the agent retries all 100. The retry is safe because each point carries a stable identity, so the 83 already stored are recognized rather than duplicated.
5. **OCI Monitoring exposes exactly this choice.** Its `PostMetricData` operation lets the caller decide whether a batch is all-or-nothing.
6. **The last line separates fact from proposal**, which is the file's standing discipline: the OCI behavior is documented, the routing design is this file's own.

## Agent concurrency and aggregation — IC3 baseline

The first deep dive. Pseudocode, the anti-pattern to avoid, spool bounds, type-specific aggregation, cardinality, and the exact failure.

---

> ```text
> RECORD(point):
>     under short buffer lock:
>         validate bounded series count
>         apply type-specific aggregation to active buffer
>
> FLUSH():
>     under same lock:
>         detached = active buffer
>         active buffer = new empty buffer
>     serialize detached outside lock
>     persist/send with stable batch ID
>     release retry copy only after durable acceptance
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
Two operations sharing one lock, and the entire point is how little happens inside it.

**`RECORD`**, called by application threads whenever something is measured:

1. **Validate the bounded series count.** Checked here, on the host, before a new series exists anywhere. This is the first line of cardinality defence, before the gateway's.
2. **Apply type-specific aggregation to the active buffer.** A counter increment adds to a delta; a gauge replaces a value; a histogram observation increments a bucket.

**`FLUSH`**, called on a timer:

1. **Under the lock: detach the buffer and install a fresh empty one.**
   - Two reference assignments. Nanoseconds.
2. **Serialize outside the lock.** The detached buffer is now private to this thread; nothing else holds a reference, so no lock is needed.
3. **Send with a stable batch identity.**
4. **Release the retry copy only after durable acceptance.**

**Why it matters**
The lock is held for a swap, not for the work.

- Worked example of the difference: serializing 100 points might take 1 millisecond. If the lock were held throughout, every application thread recording a metric would block for up to 1 millisecond every 10 seconds. At high measurement rates that is real contention introduced into production code paths by the monitoring agent.
- Holding it for the swap alone reduces the blocked time to nanoseconds. Same correctness, roughly a thousandfold less contention.

---

> - Never copy a concurrent map and then remove copied keys as a flush protocol.
>   - Writers can update those keys between copy and remove.
>   - The removal can discard points that were never included in the sent batch.

**What this says in plain words**
A named anti-pattern: iterate the map copying entries, send the copy, then delete the copied keys.

**Why it matters**
It looks correct and silently loses data.

- Worked example, step by step, for series X in a counter buffer:

| Step | Flush thread | Application thread | Buffer value |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | reads X, copies value 40 | | 40 |
| 2 | | records 7 more | 47 |
| 3 | sends the copy containing 40 | | 47 |
| 4 | deletes key X | | absent |

- The 7 events recorded at step 2 were never sent and no longer exist. The counter is silently 7 short, permanently.
- The bug's nastiness is proportional to load: it only manifests when a write lands in the window between copy and delete, so it is invisible in testing and constant in production.
- The swap avoids it entirely: after the swap, writers touch the new buffer and the detached one is frozen, so there is no window at all.

---

> - Bound spool bytes and batch age.
>   - A row-count limit does not bound memory when labels and payloads vary.
>   - Concrete assumption: `64 MiB` spool per host and `5 min` maximum batch age.
>   - Drop low-priority unaccepted telemetry with explicit counters when disk is exhausted.
>   - Critical audit or business records require a different contract.

**What this says in plain words**
Four rules about the local buffer.

1. **Bound bytes and age, not rows.**
   - Worked example of why a row limit fails: a limit of 100,000 rows. With compact 50-byte points that is 5 megabytes. With a tenant using long label strings at 2 kilobytes per point, the same 100,000 rows is 200 megabytes. The row limit did not bound memory; it bounded a number that has no fixed relationship to memory.
2. **Two bounds together: 64 mebibytes and 5 minutes.**
   - Both are needed. Worked example: a low-volume host never reaches 64 mebibytes, so without the age bound its buffered points could sit for hours and arrive far outside the lateness window, where they are useless.
3. **Drop low-priority data with explicit counters when full.**
   - "explicit counters" is the requirement that makes it a degradation rather than corruption.
4. **Critical records need a different contract.**
   - The boundary restated where it is enforced: this mechanism is correct for telemetry and wrong for anything that must not be lost.

---

> - Type-specific aggregation
>   - Counter deltas retain producer reset/incarnation information.
>   - Gauges use a declared last-value or min/max sampling policy.
>   - Histogram buckets merge only when boundaries are compatible.
>   - Average equals total sum divided by total count.
>   - Averaging independent percentiles does not yield the combined percentile.

**What this says in plain words**
Five rules, one per metric type plus two about combining.

1. **Counter deltas carry reset information.**
   - Worked example: a process counts 4,000,000 requests, restarts, and its counter is 0. The next report shows a delta of, say, 12. Without knowing a restart happened, a consumer computing `new − old` sees −3,999,988. Carrying the incarnation lets the consumer recognize a new producer run and treat the value as a fresh start rather than a decrease.
2. **Gauges use a declared policy.**
   - Worked example: memory used is sampled 10 times in a 10-second window: 4.1, 4.3, 9.8, 4.2, and so on gigabytes. What is sent for the window? Last value reports 4.2 and hides the 9.8 spike. Max reports 9.8 and hides that it was momentary. Neither is wrong; what matters is that the policy is *declared*, so the reader knows which question the number answers.
3. **Histogram buckets merge only when boundaries match.**
   - Worked example: host A uses boundaries 0-10, 10-50, 50-100 milliseconds. Host B uses 0-25, 25-100. There is no way to combine them, because A's 10-50 bucket splits across B's boundaries and the underlying values are gone. Bucket boundaries must therefore be a fleet-wide convention, not a per-service choice.
4. **Average = total sum ÷ total count.**
   - Worked example of doing it correctly across hosts: host A has sum 800 over 400 samples, host B has sum 150 over 5. The combined average is 950 ÷ 405 ≈ 2.35. Averaging the two averages, 2 and 30, gives 16, which is wrong by a factor of seven because B's 5 samples were given equal weight to A's 400.
   - This is why the contributions table stores sum and count rather than an average.
5. **Averaging percentiles does not produce the combined percentile.**
   - The strongest version of the same point, and it is worse than the average case because there is no correction available.
   - Worked example: host A's p99 is 100 milliseconds over 1,000,000 requests; host B's is 200 milliseconds over 10. The average is 150. The true combined p99 is essentially 100, since B contributed 10 of a million requests. And unlike the mean, no weighting fixes it: a percentile is a position in a distribution, and positions from different distributions cannot be combined arithmetically at all. Only the distributions themselves can be merged, which is why histograms exist.

---

> - Cardinality
>   - Normalize route templates.
>   - Reject unbounded user IDs or request IDs as labels.
>   - Apply a per-tenant active-series budget.
>   - See [Prometheus instrumentation guidance](https://prometheus.io/docs/practices/instrumentation/).

**What this says in plain words**
Three defences, applied in order of how early they act.

1. **Normalize route templates.**
   - Worked example: `/users/8891/orders` and `/users/4417/orders` become `/users/{id}/orders`. Without normalization, a million users produce a million series for one metric.
2. **Reject unbounded identifiers as labels.**
   - User identifiers, request identifiers, session identifiers, trace identifiers. Each distinct value creates a series.
   - Worked example: `request_id` as a label at 1,000,000 requests per second creates 1,000,000 series per second, each holding exactly one point. The index grows without bound and every series is useless, because a series with one point cannot be graphed over time.
3. **Apply a per-tenant budget.** The backstop for anything the first two miss.
4. **The citation** points at the upstream instrumentation guidance, which is the canonical public source for these rules.

---

> - Exact failure
>   - The lock protects the swap, not the send.
>   - A crash after the swap but before durable spooling can lose the detached batch under the stated metrics policy.
>   - A dead process cannot reliably increment its own drop counter afterward.
>   - Infer the gap from producer sequences or an independent checkpoint, or disclose that exact loss counting is unavailable.

**What this says in plain words**
The honest statement of what the swap-buffer design does not protect against.

1. **The lock protects the swap, not the send.** It guarantees no concurrent write is lost during the handoff. It guarantees nothing about what happens afterwards.
2. **A crash between swap and durable spooling loses the detached batch.**
   - Worked example: the buffer is swapped at 14:00:10, holding 100 points. The process is killed at 14:00:10.001, before the batch reached disk or the gateway. Those 100 points existed only in that process's memory and are gone. The new buffer is also gone, but it was nearly empty.
3. **A dead process cannot count its own loss.**
   - The neat consequence, and the reason "counted drops" has a limit. The drop counter is itself in the dead process's memory. Worked example: the agent is killed and 100 points are lost. There is no surviving record that they existed, so the promise that every drop is counted holds only for drops the process survives.
4. **So infer it or disclose it.**
   - **Infer from producer sequences.** Worked example: the sink sees sequence numbers 4,120, 4,121, and then 4,123. The gap identifies exactly one missing batch, reconstructed from the surviving data rather than from the dead process.
   - **Or an independent checkpoint**, written outside the process.
   - **Or disclose that exact loss counting is unavailable.** The last option is the one worth admiring: rather than claiming a guarantee the mechanism cannot support, say plainly that this class of loss cannot be counted.

**Why it matters**
Every design has a case it does not cover. Naming it precisely, and offering two mitigations plus an honest disclosure, is the difference between a design you can operate and one that will surprise you.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Buffer swap | Exchanging the active buffer for an empty one under a short lock. | Makes lock hold time independent of batch size. |
| Lock hold time | How long a lock is held, and therefore how long other threads may block. | Nanoseconds for a swap, milliseconds for serialization. |
| Copy-then-remove | The anti-pattern of copying map entries, sending them, then deleting the keys. | Silently loses writes that land between the copy and the delete. |
| Detached buffer | A buffer no longer reachable by writers, so it needs no further locking. | What the flush thread serializes. |
| Spool | The agent's local disk buffer for unsent data. | Bounded by bytes and age, never by row count. |
| Mebibyte | 1,048,576 bytes, written `MiB`. | The spool bound is 64 of them. |
| Batch age | How long the oldest unsent data has been waiting. | The second bound, needed because low-volume hosts never hit the byte limit. |
| Counter delta | The increase in a counter over a window, rather than its absolute value. | What is transmitted, and why resets must be detectable. |
| Counter reset | A counter returning to zero when its process restarts. | Produces a negative rate unless the incarnation is known. |
| Sampling policy | The declared rule for reducing several gauge samples to one value: last, min, or max. | Different policies answer different questions; the choice must be stated. |
| Bucket boundaries | The value ranges a histogram counts into. | Must match across sources or the histograms cannot merge. |

## Delivery, time, and replay — IC3 baseline

The second deep dive. Producer retries, the sink transaction, the consistency boundary, event time, poison versus outage, and the Kafka caveat.

---

> - Producer retries
>   - An idempotent broker producer reduces duplicates within its supported scope.
>   - Gateway retries and explicit later replay still need stable application identities.

**What this says in plain words**
Two sentences, and the second is the correction.

1. **Idempotent producers help, within a scope.**
   - A message broker can offer an idempotent producer mode: it tracks a sequence per producer session and discards a duplicate caused by its own retry.
   - Worked example: the producer sends a record, the acknowledgment is lost, the client library retries. The broker recognizes the sequence number and stores it once.
2. **But that scope does not cover everything.**
   - Worked example of what it misses: the *gateway* retries after a failure, which is a different producer session, so the broker's deduplication does not apply. Or an operator runs a deliberate replay three days later, which is unquestionably a new append.
   - So the application-level identity is still required. The broker's feature reduces one class of duplicate; it does not remove the need for the design's own identity.

**Why it matters**
It is a guard against a specific wrong conclusion: "the broker is idempotent, so we do not need our own deduplication". The broker's guarantee is scoped to one producer session, and the system's lifetime is longer than that.

---

> - Sink transaction
>   - Insert a unique contribution and update its aggregate in one transaction when supported.
>   - On duplicate contribution, do not add again.
>   - Commit consumer progress only after the sink state is recoverable.
>   - Alternatively materialize deterministic complete windows and replace by version.

**What this says in plain words**
The mechanism that makes replay free, in four rules.

1. **Insert the contribution and update the aggregate in one transaction.**
   - Worked example of why they must be atomic: insert the contribution row, then crash before updating the aggregate. The contribution now says "batch b-8891 was applied", so a retry skips it, and the aggregate never received those 40 samples. The record of having applied it outlived the application itself, and the data is silently lost.
   - Both in one transaction means either both happen or neither does.
2. **On duplicate, do not add again.** The insert conflicts on the primary key and the whole operation is a no-op.
3. **Commit progress only after the sink state is recoverable.** The write-then-commit ordering, restated at the point it is enforced.
4. **Or materialize complete windows and replace by version.**
   - A genuinely different strategy: instead of incrementally adding contributions, recompute the whole window from its source and swap in the result.
   - Worked example: rather than adding batch by batch to the 14:00 window, read every record for 14:00 and compute the window fully, then write it as version 2. Recomputation is deterministic, so repeating it produces the same answer, which makes it naturally idempotent with no deduplication table at all.
   - The tradeoff: incremental is cheaper per batch, and recomputation is simpler to reason about and immune to a whole class of bugs.

---

> - Consistency boundary
>   - The log offset is the recovery coordinate
>   - The sink's unique contribution key is the correctness boundary.
>   - A consumer that commits the offset first and writes second can lose data
>   - A consumer that writes first and commits second can duplicate, which the unique key absorbs.

**What this says in plain words**
The cleanest statement of the design's central idea. Two different things, often confused.

1. **The offset is a recovery coordinate.** It says where to resume reading. It is about position.
2. **The contribution key is the correctness boundary.** It says what has already been applied. It is about effect.
   - Worked example of why the offset cannot do the second job: a deliberate replay re-appends the same logical data at new offsets. The offsets are fresh, so an offset-based check sees nothing familiar. The contribution key still recognizes it.
3. **Commit-then-write loses data.**
   - Worked example: the consumer marks offset 5,000 as done, then crashes before writing the record. On restart it resumes at 5,001. Record 5,000 is never written and nothing indicates it was skipped.
4. **Write-then-commit duplicates, and the key absorbs it.**
   - Worked example: the consumer writes record 5,000, then crashes before committing. On restart it re-reads 5,000 and writes again. The contribution key rejects the second write as a duplicate and the aggregate is untouched.

**Why it matters**
This is the sentence to have ready for "how do you get exactly-once?".

- The answer is that you do not get exactly-once delivery; you get at-least-once delivery plus an idempotent effect, which is observationally the same for the data and far cheaper to build.
- The choice of ordering is a deliberate one: given loss or duplication, choose duplication, because duplication has a cure.

---

> - Event time
>   - Keep source observation time and gateway receipt time separate.
>   - Accept bounded out-of-order points, for example within `5 min` by assumption.
>   - Expose provisional rollups until the lateness window closes.
>   - Late corrections create a new materialization version or enter a repair workflow.

**What this says in plain words**
Four rules about time, and the first is the foundation.

1. **Two distinct timestamps, kept separate.**
   - Event time is when the measurement was taken. Processing time is when the pipeline received it.
   - Worked example of why both are needed: a host spools for 3 minutes during a network problem, then sends. Event time places the points where they belong on the graph. Processing time explains why the rollup for that window had to be revised. Using only one loses either the accuracy or the explanation.
2. **Accept bounded lateness, 5 minutes.**
   - Bounded is what makes the rest possible: without a limit, no window could ever be declared final.
3. **Rollups are provisional until the window closes.**
   - Worked example: the 14:00 window is computed at 14:01 from what has arrived. More points for 14:00 may arrive until 14:05. Until then the value is marked provisional, so a dashboard can show it as still settling.
4. **Corrections after the window closes create a new version or a repair workflow.**
   - Worked example: a point timestamped 14:00 arrives at 15:30, well past the window. The options are to reject it, or to produce version 2 of that window through an explicit repair path. What is forbidden is quietly editing a closed window, because a value someone already read and acted on would change with no record.

---

> - Poison versus outage
>   - Deterministic schema errors enter quarantine with diagnostic metadata.
>   - Sink unavailability should pause/retry with backpressure.
>   - Moving every record to DLQ during an outage merely relocates the backlog.

**What this says in plain words**
Two failures that look identical from the consumer's position and require opposite responses.

1. **A poison record fails deterministically.**
   - Worked example: a record with a malformed label that the parser rejects. It will fail on every attempt, forever. Retrying is an infinite loop, and worse, it blocks every record behind it in the partition.
   - The response: move it to quarantine with enough diagnostic metadata to investigate, and continue.
2. **A sink outage fails transiently.**
   - Every record fails, and every record would succeed later. The response is to pause and retry with backpressure.
3. **The trap: using the dead-letter queue for an outage.**
   - Worked example: the database is down for 2 hours. A consumer that routes every failure to the dead-letter queue moves 7.2 billion records there. When the database returns, those 7.2 billion records must be replayed out of the dead-letter queue, which is exactly the same backlog in a less convenient place, now stripped of its original ordering.
   - The line "merely relocates the backlog" is precise: nothing was recovered, and the situation is worse because the log's ordering and offsets are no longer the recovery mechanism.

**Why it matters**
The distinguishing question is whether the failure is deterministic. A record that fails identically every time is poison. A record that fails because something else is down is not, and treating them the same turns an outage into a data-shuffling exercise.

---

> - Kafka transactions do not automatically make an external TSDB exactly once.
>   - The external sink must cooperate through idempotency or an atomic progress boundary.
>   - See [Kafka delivery semantics](https://kafka.apache.org/43/design/design/).

**What this says in plain words**
A direct correction of a widespread misconception.

1. **Kafka transactions cover Kafka.** They can make a read-process-write cycle atomic when both the read and the write are within Kafka, including the offset commit.
2. **An external database is outside that boundary.**
   - Worked example: the consumer reads from Kafka, writes to the time-series database, and commits the offset inside a Kafka transaction. The database write is not part of that transaction. If the process crashes after the database write and before the transaction commits, the transaction aborts, the offset is not committed, and the record is re-processed. The database already has the data. The transaction did not prevent the duplicate; it only ensured Kafka's own state was consistent.
3. **So the external sink must cooperate.** Either it is idempotent, which is the contribution key, or the progress marker lives atomically with the data in the sink itself.
   - Worked example of the second option: store the consumer's offset in the *same database transaction* as the data. Now the progress marker and the effect are genuinely atomic, because they are in the same system.

**Why it matters**
"We use Kafka transactions so it is exactly-once" is a common and confident wrong answer. The correction is precise and easy to state: transactions give you atomicity within one system's boundary, and the boundary here does not include the database.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Idempotent producer | A broker mode that discards duplicates caused by the producer's own retries within one session. | Helps, but does not cover gateway retries or deliberate replay. |
| Application identity | An identifier the design itself assigns, independent of any broker feature. | Required because the broker's guarantee is session-scoped. |
| Atomic transaction | Several changes that all happen or none do. | Contribution insert and aggregate update must be one. |
| Contribution key | The `(window, series, source batch)` identity of a batch's effect on an aggregate. | The correctness boundary. |
| Recovery coordinate | A position marker saying where to resume reading. | What a log offset is, and all it is. |
| Correctness boundary | The mechanism that determines whether an effect has already been applied. | Distinct from position, and the only thing that survives replay. |
| Commit-then-write | Recording progress before performing the effect. | Loses data on a crash between the two. |
| Write-then-commit | Performing the effect before recording progress. | Duplicates on a crash, which the key absorbs. |
| At-least-once plus idempotence | The practical substitute for exactly-once delivery. | What this design actually provides. |
| Deterministic recomputation | Recalculating a window from source so repetition yields the same result. | Naturally idempotent, with no deduplication table. |
| Event time | When a measurement was actually taken. | Where the point belongs on the graph. |
| Processing time | When the pipeline received the measurement. | Explains why a window was revised. |
| Lateness window | How long after its event time a point may still be accepted. | Bounded so a window can eventually be declared final. |
| Provisional window | A computed summary marked as still able to change. | True until the lateness window closes. |
| Materialization version | A complete recomputed copy of a window, swapped in atomically. | How a correction is applied without editing a closed value. |
| Poison record | A record that fails deterministically on every attempt. | Must be quarantined, never retried indefinitely. |
| Dead-letter queue | A destination for records that could not be processed. | Correct for poison, actively harmful during an outage. |
| Backpressure | Slowing consumption when the sink cannot keep up. | The correct response to a transient outage. |
| Kafka transaction | Atomicity across reads, writes and offset commits within Kafka. | Does not extend to an external database. |

## Failure, operations, and security

A nine-row failure table, then what to observe, retry discipline, tenant isolation, rollout safety, and an operational drill.

---

> | Failure | Blast radius | Detection | Degraded behavior | Recovery |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Agent disconnected | One host | Spool age and retry rate | Bounded local buffering; counted drops when policy permits | Retry stable batches with jitter |
> | Gateway fault domain lost | One fault domain's share of requests | Health checks, error rate | Remaining domains absorb load; agents retry | Load balancer drains; replace capacity |
> | Log quorum unavailable | A partition cohort | Produce failures | Stop durable acknowledgment | Recover replicas and replay local spool |
> | TSDB unavailable | Its assigned series | Consumer lag age | Pause consumption; query freshness degrades | Catch up below resource limits |
> | Series catalog / policy store unavailable (control plane) | New budgets and new-series admission | Snapshot age | Gateways accept on cached budgets; unknown new series held to the cached quota | Refresh snapshots; reconcile series created during the outage |
> | High-cardinality tenant | Shared index resources | New-series admission and memory | Reject new series over budget | Correct instrumentation and expire unused series |
> | Poison record | A bounded record set | Classified validation failures | Quarantine without infinite retry | Replay with stable original identity |
> | Duplicate replay | Target series/windows | Unique-key conflicts | No double aggregate | Audit replay progress and reconcile counts |
> | Expensive query | Query/storage resources | Query cost and queue age | Cancel or return bounded partial results by contract | Tighten fan-out and resolution limits |

**What this says in plain words**
Nine failures, with how far each spreads, how you notice, what happens meanwhile, and how it ends.

- **Row: Agent disconnected.**
  - Radius: one host. Detection is spool age plus retry rate, not an error count, because the agent is failing quietly by design.
  - Worked example of spool age as the signal: the spool is 40% full, which sounds fine. Its oldest entry is 4 minutes old, approaching the 5-minute maximum batch age, which means data is about to be dropped. Occupancy alone would not have shown that.
- **Row: Gateway fault domain lost.**
  - Radius: that domain's share of requests. Worked example: three fault domains means about a third of requests fail until agents retry into the survivors, which then carry 50% more each.
- **Row: Log quorum unavailable.**
  - Radius: "a partition cohort", meaning the partitions whose replicas are affected, not the whole log.
  - Degraded behavior is blunt: stop acknowledging. The system refuses to promise what it cannot keep, and agents fall back to their spools.
- **Row: TSDB unavailable.**
  - Detection is consumer lag *age*, in seconds, rather than lag in records.
  - Worked example of why age: a lag of 5,000,000 records means nothing on its own. A lag of 90 seconds is immediately interpretable against a 30-second freshness target. Age converts a raw count into the units the requirement is written in.
  - "Catch up below resource limits" is the recovery caution: draining at maximum speed can saturate the database and take out queries as well.
- **Row: Series catalog unavailable.**
  - The most carefully worded row. Gateways accept on cached budgets, and unknown new series are held to the cached quota.
  - Worked example of the hole it closes: during the outage a tenant starts emitting a brand-new series. The gateway cannot check the catalog. Accepting freely would make a catalog outage a cardinality bypass, so unknown series are admitted only within a bounded cached quota.
  - Recovery includes reconciling series created during the outage, which is the debt the bounded quota keeps small.
- **Row: High-cardinality tenant.**
  - Radius: shared index resources, meaning everyone.
  - Recovery has two halves, and both are slow: correct the instrumentation, which requires the tenant to deploy a change, and expire unused series, which requires compaction.
- **Row: Poison record.**
  - "Classified validation failures" means failures are categorized, so a deterministic schema error is distinguishable from a transient one.
  - Recovery replays "with stable original identity", so a fixed record re-enters carrying the identity it always had and is not double-counted.
- **Row: Duplicate replay.**
  - Detection is unique-key conflicts, which is a case of the mechanism reporting its own successful operation. A rising conflict count means duplicates are arriving and being correctly absorbed.
- **Row: Expensive query.**
  - "by contract" is the important qualifier on partial results: returning partial data is acceptable only if the contract says results may be truncated and the response says so.

---

> - Observe
>   - Oldest unprocessed record age.
>   - Estimated time to retention loss.
>   - End-to-end freshness.
>   - Spool drops.
>   - Quarantine growth.
>   - Tenant cardinality admission.

**What this says in plain words**
Six measurements, and the second is the one that shows real operational thinking.

1. **Oldest unprocessed record age.** How far behind the pipeline is, in time.
2. **Estimated time to retention loss.**
   - The best metric in the file. It combines the backlog, the drain rate, and the retention setting into a single countdown.
   - Worked example: the backlog is 4 hours old, retention is 24 hours, and the backlog is growing at 30 minutes per hour. Estimated time to retention loss is about 40 hours. That is comfortable. If the backlog were 19 hours old and still growing, the same metric reads 10 hours, and it is the number that decides whether to page someone at 3 a.m.
   - It converts three separate facts into one actionable number with an obvious threshold.
3. **End-to-end freshness.** Measured from event time to queryable, which is what the requirement promises. Measuring any single hop would miss delay in the others.
4. **Spool drops.** The counted losses, aggregated across hosts.
5. **Quarantine growth.** A rising quarantine rate means a producer started emitting something malformed, usually right after a deploy.
6. **Tenant cardinality admission.** Rejections per tenant, which is the early warning for a team that just shipped a bad label.

---

> - Retry discipline
>   - Agents retry with exponential backoff and jitter under a total elapsed deadline.
>   - Retry only transport and `5xx` responses
>   - A `4xx` validation error requires a fix, not a retry.
>   - Consumers retry sink writes with backpressure
>   - They never skip an offset to make progress.

**What this says in plain words**
Three rules, and the last is absolute.

1. **Backoff, jitter, and a total deadline.**
   - The deadline is the part often missed. Worked example: without one, an agent retries a 10-minute-old batch forever, holding spool space for data that is already outside the lateness window and therefore worthless.
2. **Retry transport errors and `5xx`, never `4xx`.**
   - `5xx` means the server failed, which may be transient. `4xx` means the request is wrong, which will be wrong every time.
   - Worked example of ignoring this: an agent sends a malformed batch, receives `400`, and retries every second forever. It never succeeds, it consumes gateway capacity permanently, and the actual fix is a code change nobody knows is needed because the failure is invisible in aggregate.
3. **Consumers apply backpressure and never skip an offset.**
   - Worked example of the temptation: a record repeatedly fails and the backlog is growing. Skipping it makes the graph recover immediately. It also silently drops data with no record, and it establishes that the pipeline's guarantee can be abandoned when convenient.
   - The correct escape is quarantine, which removes the blockage while preserving the record and making the removal visible.

---

> - Tenant isolation
>   - Authenticate producers and bind tenant identity server-side.
>   - Apply authorization to labels, queries, and replay operations.
>   - Per-tenant budgets cover ingest rate, active series, and query cost.
>   - A tenant's query receives `404` on another tenant's series, not `403`, to avoid existence leaks.
>   - Remove credentials and sensitive payloads from diagnostic logs.
>   - Encrypt transport and durable stores.

**What this says in plain words**
Six rules.

1. **Bind tenant identity server-side.**
   - Worked example of the alternative: trusting a `tenant_id` field in the payload. Any producer could claim to be any tenant, writing into their series and consuming their budget. The identity must be derived from the verified credential.
2. **Authorize labels, queries, and replays.** Three separate surfaces, all needing checks. Replay especially, since it is a privileged operation that rewrites history.
3. **Budgets cover three dimensions.** Ingest rate, active series, and query cost. A cap on one does not constrain the others; a tenant within its ingest budget can still exhaust the query tier.
4. **`404`, not `403`.**
   - `403` confirms the resource exists. Worked example of the leak: an attacker probes series names. `403` responses reveal which internal service names are real, exposing another tenant's architecture without ever reading their data.
5. **No credentials or sensitive payloads in diagnostic logs.**
   - Particularly relevant to quarantine, which stores failed records precisely because they are unusual. Worked example: a malformed record containing a token is quarantined, and the quarantine store is widely readable for debugging.
6. **Encrypt transport and durable stores.** Stated once, applying everywhere.

---

> - Rollout and rollback safety
>   - Roll the gateway fleet by fault domain
>   - Agents keep spooling, so a bad deploy costs freshness, not accepted data.
>   - Consumer schema changes follow expand, migrate, contract
>   - A new consumer must read records written by the old gateway.
>   - Rollback of a rollup transform is a new materialization version, never an in-place edit.

**What this says in plain words**
Three rules.

1. **Roll by fault domain, and agents spool through it.**
   - Worked example of the payoff: a bad gateway deploy takes a domain offline for 10 minutes. Agents spool, then send when it returns. The cost is 10 minutes of freshness and zero accepted data lost, because nothing was ever acknowledged and then lost.
   - This is the architecture paying a dividend: the spool exists for network failures and happens to make deploys safe as well.
2. **Expand, migrate, contract for schema changes.**
   - Three phases: first make readers accept both old and new formats, then start writing the new one, and only then remove support for the old.
   - Worked example of skipping it: a new consumer requires a field the old gateway never wrote. During the rollout, old-format records already sit in the log. The new consumer rejects them, and the pipeline stalls on data that was perfectly valid when written.
   - The log makes this sharper than usual: it holds up to 24 hours of records written by whatever version was deployed at the time, so a consumer must always read the past.
3. **Rollback of a transform is a new version, never an edit.**
   - Worked example: a rollup transform was wrong for 3 days. Rolling back means computing those windows again under the previous transform as a new version, not editing the stored numbers. A reader always sees one complete, identifiable version, and the history of what was shown is preserved.

---

> - Operational drill
>   - Stop a sink for a bounded interval.
>   - Measure backlog growth and net catch-up rate.
>   - Verify replay does not double aggregates.
>   - Confirm queries expose stale data instead of silently implying freshness.

**What this says in plain words**
A four-step exercise that tests every central claim at once.

1. **Stop a sink deliberately.** A controlled version of the failure the design exists to survive.
2. **Measure backlog growth and *net* catch-up rate.**
   - *Net* is the key word, and it is where the capacity section's arithmetic gets validated.
   - Worked example: the drill shows a net catch-up of 200,000 per second rather than the assumed 500,000. The 1.5× headroom assumption is wrong, the real figure is about 1.2×, and a 2-hour outage would take 10 hours to drain rather than 4. That single measurement changes the retention requirement.
3. **Verify replay does not double aggregates.** Compare totals before and after; if the contribution key works, they match exactly.
4. **Confirm queries expose staleness.**
   - Worked example of the failure this catches: during the drill, a dashboard shows a flat line for the outage period with no indication anything is wrong. An operator would read that as "no traffic" rather than "no data". The freshness watermark must be visible, not merely present in the response body.

**Why it matters**
Proposing this drill is one of the strongest moves available in the interview. It tests the catch-up assumption, the idempotence claim, and the freshness contract in a single exercise, and it is the kind of thing only someone who expects to operate the system thinks to suggest.

| Spool age | How old the oldest unsent data on a host is. | Reveals impending drops that occupancy hides. |

## Alternatives and IC4 stretch

The option table, three stretch items, the weakest assumption, the over-engineering warning, and a cross-reference.

---

> | Option | What it is | Strength | Weakness | Choose when |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Direct TSDB write | Synchronous ingestion | Small topology | Sink outages directly affect producers | Low volume and durable producer buffering |
> | Partitioned replay log | Durable append before sinks | Replay and independent consumers | Retention, partitions, and operating cost | Baseline sustained shared ingestion |
> | Work queue | Task delivery | Simple worker decoupling | Replay and ordering depend on queue contract | One task consumer without historical reprocessing |
> | Raw-only storage | Preserve all detail | Flexible future queries | Storage and read amplification | Small retention or forensic requirements |
> | Raw plus rollups | Multiple resolutions | Lower query/storage cost | Late correction and versioning complexity | Repeated time-range dashboards |

**What this says in plain words**
Five options across two decisions: what sits between producers and storage, and what is stored.

- **Row: Direct TSDB write.**
  - The naive design, and it is genuinely right sometimes. Strength: fewer moving parts, and nobody has to operate a log.
  - Worked example of its proper home: 50 hosts at 17 points per second, with each producer keeping a durable local buffer. A brief database outage is absorbed by those buffers. Adding a log here would be more operational burden than the problem justifies.
- **Row: Partitioned replay log.** The chosen baseline. Strength is replay and independent consumers; weakness is retention bytes, partition management, and a second system to run.
- **Row: Work queue.**
  - Different from a log in one decisive way: a queue usually deletes a message once it is consumed, while a log retains records for their retention period and lets any consumer re-read from any position.
  - Worked example of the difference mattering: you need to recompute last Tuesday's rollups. With a log, you replay from Tuesday's offsets. With a queue, those messages were consumed and deleted, so there is nothing to replay.
- **Row: Raw-only storage.**
  - Strength: any future question can be answered, because nothing was summarized away.
  - Weakness: read amplification, meaning a query reads far more data than it returns.
  - Worked example: a 7-day dashboard over 1,000 series at 10-second resolution reads about 60,000,000 points to render a few hundred pixels of graph. The same answer from hourly rollups reads about 168,000.
- **Row: Raw plus rollups.** The chosen baseline. The weakness named is exactly what the lateness and versioning machinery exists to handle.

---

> - IC4 stretch — no accepted loss
>   - Rank accepted-data recoverability before query freshness.
>   - Remove drop-on-full for accepted records
>   - The agent blocks or returns backpressure instead.
>   - Reduce raw retention or query precision only with an explicit product agreement.
>   - Separate billable metering from lossy operational telemetry.

**What this says in plain words**
What changes if the data must never be lost once accepted.

1. **Reorder the priorities.** Recoverability outranks freshness, which reverses nothing in the architecture but changes every tradeoff decision made under pressure.
2. **Remove drop-on-full; the agent blocks or applies backpressure.**
   - Worked example of the consequence: the spool fills and the agent can no longer accept measurements. It blocks the calling thread or returns an error. The application being measured is now affected by the monitoring system, which is precisely what telemetry design normally avoids at all costs.
   - That is the real cost of no-loss, and it must be stated: the guarantee is paid for by the measured application, not by the pipeline.
3. **Reduce retention or precision only by explicit agreement.** These become product decisions rather than operational knobs.
4. **Separate billable metering from telemetry.**
   - The recommendation that resolves the tension. Worked example: run two pipelines. The billing one carries a low volume of high-value records with no-loss guarantees and backpressure. The telemetry one carries a million points per second with a drop policy. Neither compromises for the other, and the expensive guarantees are paid for only where they are needed.

---

> - IC4 stretch — 10× load
>   - Raw daily input becomes approximately `43.2 TB/day` before replication.
>   - Local aggregation and retention choices can save more than adding brokers.
>   - Preserve metric semantics while reducing resolution.

**What this says in plain words**
At ten times the load, the answer is not simply more machines.

1. **43.2 terabytes per day before replication**, so about 130 terabytes with three copies, for one day.
2. **Aggregation and retention save more than capacity does.**
   - Worked example: halving retention from 24 hours to 12 removes 65 terabytes. Adding brokers to handle 130 terabytes a day costs continuously and solves nothing about the underlying volume. The cheapest data is the data you never store.
3. **Reduce resolution while preserving semantics.**
   - Worked example done correctly: instead of one point per series per 10 seconds, the agent sends one per 60 seconds carrying count, sum, min, max, and histogram buckets covering that minute. Points drop sixfold. A dashboard at minute resolution loses nothing, and a percentile query still works because the distribution was preserved.
   - Worked example done incorrectly: send the average of the six samples. Sixfold reduction as well, but the count is gone so it cannot be re-weighted, the maximum is gone so spikes vanish, and the distribution is gone so percentiles are impossible. The same compression ratio, with the information destroyed.
   - "Preserve metric semantics" means the reduced form must still support every query the full form did, at the coarser resolution.

---

> - IC4 stretch — cost and blast radius
>   - Isolate large tenants or workload classes into cells.
>   - Bound log retention pressure and query blast radius per cell.
>   - Migrate series ownership with a routing version and a replay boundary.
>   - Replicated log retention is the dominant storage cost
>   - Shorten it only after the catch-up drill proves the margin.

**What this says in plain words**
Four points about containment and cost.

1. **Cells for large tenants or workload classes.** An independent copy of the pipeline serving a subset.
2. **Retention pressure and query blast radius are bounded per cell.** A tenant's cardinality explosion or expensive query affects one cell.
3. **Series migration needs a routing version and a replay boundary.**
   - Moving a series between cells is delicate. Worked example: series X moves from cell A to cell B at 14:00. Without a declared boundary, points for X could be written to both cells around the transition, producing two partial copies of the same series with no indication which is complete. The routing version says which cell owns it from which point, and the replay boundary says where re-processing should start.
4. **Retention is the dominant cost, and shortening it requires evidence.**
   - The ordering is the discipline. Retention looks like an easy saving: halving it halves the largest line on the bill.
   - Worked example of why the drill must come first: retention is cut from 24 hours to 8 to save money. A 2-hour outage then produces a 6-hour recovery, which consumes most of the 8-hour window, and any complication during recovery means permanent data loss. The saving was real and the margin it consumed was invisible until the outage.

---

> - Weakest assumption
>   - The sink absorbs `1.5×` live rate during catch-up while still serving queries.
>   - If the real headroom is `1.1×`, a `2 h` outage takes `20 h` to drain and consumes nearly the whole `24 h` retention window.
>   - Mitigation: measure catch-up under query load, shed rollup work during catch-up, and alarm on estimated time to retention loss.

**What this says in plain words**
The assumption everything else rests on, with the arithmetic of it being wrong.

1. **The assumption**, stated with its hardest clause: 1.5× *while still serving queries*. The database is not idle during recovery; dashboards are being watched precisely because something broke.
2. **The arithmetic of being wrong.**
   - At 1.5×, surplus is 500,000 per second and 7.2 billion points drain in 4 hours.
   - At 1.1×, surplus is 100,000 per second and the same backlog takes 20 hours.
   - A 27% shortfall in headroom produces a fivefold increase in recovery time, because the surplus is a small difference between two large numbers and is therefore extremely sensitive.
3. **Three mitigations, each addressing a different part.**
   - **Measure catch-up under query load.** The measurement must reproduce the conditions, since a benchmark on an idle database proves nothing about recovery.
   - **Shed rollup work during catch-up.** Worked example: rollups consume a third of sink capacity. Pausing them during recovery raises surplus from 500,000 to roughly 833,000 per second and cuts drain time from 4 hours to about 2.4. Long-range dashboards go stale for the duration, which during an incident is the right trade, and the rollups are recomputed afterwards from the raw data.
   - **Alarm on estimated time to retention loss.** The countdown that says whether recovery will finish before the data expires.

---

> - Over-engineering warning
>   - Do not call the pipeline “zero loss” without naming the acknowledgment boundary and surviving failure set.
>   - Do not add Kafka transactions expecting them to make the external database exactly once.

**What this says in plain words**
Two warnings, both about claiming more than you have.

1. **"Zero loss" is meaningless without its boundary.**
   - Worked example of the missing qualifiers: this pipeline loses nothing *after* durable log acceptance, *provided* no more than one replica of a partition is lost, and *provided* recovery completes within retention. Each clause is a real limit, and "zero loss" states none of them.
   - The honest version: "no loss after acknowledgment, where acknowledgment means a quorum append, surviving the loss of one replica and any single fault domain".
2. **Kafka transactions do not reach the external database.**
   - Repeated from the delivery section because it is the specific wrong answer most likely to be offered confidently. Transactions give atomicity within Kafka's own boundary, and the database sits outside it.

---

> - Related business prompt
>   - [Healthcare ingestion](s4.01.Healthcare-ingestion-pipeline.md) has stricter record retention and reconciliation requirements.
>   - Its accepted business records must not inherit the metrics drop policy.

**What this says in plain words**
A pointer to a sibling question with the same shape and different requirements, plus a warning about mixing them.

**Why it matters**
The two designs look similar and differ in one decisive property.

- Worked example of the difference: this pipeline may drop telemetry at the agent when the spool fills, counted. A healthcare ingestion pipeline may not drop a patient record under any circumstance; it must apply backpressure and reconcile afterwards.
- The warning exists because the architectures are so similar that reusing one for the other is tempting, and the drop policy is the one thing that must not travel with it.

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Read amplification | Reading far more data than a query returns. | The cost of raw-only storage for long ranges. |
| Work queue versus log | A queue deletes consumed messages; a log retains them for replay. | Decides whether historical reprocessing is possible at all. |
| Drop-on-full | Discarding data when a buffer is full, with a counter. | Removed entirely under a no-loss requirement. |
| Backpressure | Slowing or refusing producers when downstream cannot keep up. | What replaces dropping, at the cost of affecting the measured application. |
| Billable metering | Records used for invoicing, requiring a no-loss contract. | Kept in a separate pipeline from telemetry. |

| Replay boundary | The declared point from which re-processing should start after a migration. | Makes the migration recoverable. |

**What this says in plain words**
A self-assessment grid. The middle column is a checklist; the fourth column is what actually distinguishes a stronger answer.

**Why the fourth column is the one to score**
The difference is rarely more content. It is a different kind of claim about the same content.

- **Row: Requirements.**
  - Must land: the three metric types, the loss policy, the freshness target, and tenant identity in every key.
  - Adds: separating billing-grade data from telemetry, and saying what the drop policy buys.
  - Separating signal: *negotiating* the loss contract rather than assuming it.
  - Worked example of the difference: "the pipeline is reliable" assumes loss is unacceptable and never discusses it. "May I assume this is operational telemetry where bounded counted drops at the agent are acceptable? If it is billing data, the agent must block instead, which changes the design" establishes the contract and shows you know what it costs either way.
- **Row: Estimates.**
  - Must land: a million points per second, the backlog and catch-up arithmetic, and the cardinality figure.
  - Adds: letting catch-up headroom determine retention, and naming the correlated-outage case.
  - Separating signal: "the catch-up number changes the retention decision".
  - Worked example of passing it: "at 1.5× headroom a 2-hour outage takes 6 hours to fully recover, so 24-hour retention leaves margin; at 1.1× it would take 22 hours and 24-hour retention would be marginal". One estimate directly set a configuration value.
  - The correlated-outage case is the harder addition: the log and the database may share a fault domain, and if they fail together the log's decoupling provides nothing.
- **Row: Architecture.**
  - Must land: gateway, log, consumers, query tier, and one request traced.
  - Adds: control-plane snapshots so ingestion survives a catalog outage.
  - Separating signal: every component states both its mechanism and its price.
- **Row: Deep dive.**
  - Must land: the swap-buffer flush, stable point identity, and the unique contribution key.
  - Adds: the offset-first versus write-first failure analysis, and versioned rollups.
  - Separating signal: reasoning *across* the log and sink boundary.
  - Worked example: the offset lives in the log and the contribution key lives in the database. Correctness comes from how they relate, not from either alone. A candidate who explains only the contribution key has described a mechanism; one who explains why the offset cannot serve as the correctness boundary has explained the design.
- **Row: Operations.**
  - Must land: the failure table, freshness alarms, and the outage drill.
  - Adds: cells, fault-domain rollout, and retention as a cost control.
  - Separating signal: volunteering the drill before being asked.
  - Worked example of why it reads as experience: the drill validates the catch-up assumption, the idempotence claim, and the freshness contract in one exercise. Proposing it says you expect to operate this system, not just draw it.
- **Row: Wrap.**
  - Must land: the recap and the central tradeoff.
  - Adds: naming the catch-up assumption as the weakest, first.
  - Separating signal: finding the flaw before the interviewer does.

**How to use this table in preparation**
Rehearse the whole answer, then score only the fourth column. Each row is a yes-or-no observable rather than an impression.

## Interview pushback and validation

Eight challenges, each with a baseline and a stretch answer, then eight things to test.

---

> - Q1: Does batching reduce one million points to ten thousand points?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - No
>     - This example reduces HTTP requests to ten thousand per second.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Only semantically valid aggregation can reduce logical observations.

**What is really being asked**
Whether you confuse transport packaging with data volume. It is a trap question, and it is easy to fall into because the smaller number is the one that sounds good.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Batching changes how points travel, not how many exist.

- Worked example: 10,000 requests per second, each carrying 100 points, is still 1,000,000 points per second arriving at the log and the database. Nothing was removed.
- The practical consequence is a sizing error: the gateway is sized from 10,000 requests per second and the log and sinks from 1,000,000 points per second. Quoting one number for the other component undersizes it a hundredfold.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Only aggregation that preserves the answerable queries actually reduces observations.

- Worked example that works: ten duration samples in ten seconds become one histogram carrying the bucket counts. Ten points became one, and every percentile query still works.
- Worked example that does not: ten gauge samples become their average. Ten points became one, and the maximum, the distribution, and any per-second detail are gone. The reduction was the same and the information was destroyed.
- The test to state: can every supported query still be answered from the reduced form? If yes, it is aggregation. If no, it is loss with a smaller storage bill.

---

> - Q2: A consumer crashes after writing but before committing its offset.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - It reprocesses
>     - A unique sink identity prevents double application.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - An aggregate needs atomic dedup-plus-update, not a separate best-effort dedup cache.

**What is really being asked**
The central failure of the consumer path, asked directly.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Reprocessing happens, and it is harmless because of the identity.

- Worked example: the consumer writes records 5,000 to 5,100 and crashes before committing. On restart it resumes at 5,000 and writes them again. Each insert conflicts on the contribution key and is ignored, so the aggregates are unchanged.
- The framing to state: this is at-least-once delivery with an idempotent effect, which is observationally equivalent to exactly-once for the data and far cheaper to build.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
The deduplication must be atomic with the update, not a cache consulted beforehand.

- Worked example of the broken version: a Redis set of seen batch identifiers, checked before applying. Two consumers process the same batch concurrently. Both check the cache, both find it absent, both apply the aggregate, and both then record the identifier. The aggregate is doubled, and the cache says everything was fine.
- Worked example of the working version: insert the contribution row and update the aggregate in one database transaction. The second insert conflicts on the primary key and the whole transaction rolls back, so neither the addition nor the marker is applied.
- "best-effort" is the giveaway word: a check that can be raced is not a correctness mechanism, however reliable it usually is.

---

> - Q3: Can we average host p99 latency values?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - No
>     - Merge distributions or compatible histogram buckets.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Choose resolution and error budget before comparing histogram formats.

**What is really being asked**
The most common statistical error in monitoring, asked as a yes-or-no.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
No, and the reason is not about weighting.

- Worked example: host A's p99 is 100 milliseconds over 1,000,000 requests; host B's is 200 milliseconds over 10. The average is 150. The true combined p99 is essentially 100, since B contributed 10 requests out of a million.
- The deeper reason: a percentile is a *position* in a distribution, not a quantity. Two positions in two different distributions have no arithmetic relationship. Unlike a mean, no weighting scheme fixes it, because the information needed to locate the combined position was discarded when each host computed its own percentile.
- What works: merge the distributions themselves, as histogram bucket counts, and compute the percentile from the merged result.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Merging histograms is not free either, and the cost must be chosen deliberately.

- Bucket boundaries determine accuracy. Worked example: with a bucket covering 100 to 200 milliseconds, a p99 falling inside it can only be estimated, usually by interpolation, with an error up to the bucket width.
- More buckets means more accuracy and more storage per series, multiplied by every series.
- Worked example of the tradeoff: 20 buckets per histogram across 10,000,000 series is 200,000,000 counters. 100 buckets would be a billion.
- "error budget" means deciding in advance how wrong a percentile may be, which then determines the bucket layout, which then determines the storage cost. The chain runs from the requirement to the bill.

---

> - Q4: The data is now billing-grade with no accepted loss.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Remove drop-on-full for accepted records and enforce durable acceptance/backpressure.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Design reconciliation and regional durability separately from dashboard telemetry.

**What is really being asked**
Whether you can identify which parts of the design were conditional on the loss policy.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Two changes, and the second is invasive.

1. **Remove drop-on-full.** The agent may no longer discard when the spool fills.
2. **The agent blocks or applies backpressure instead.**
   - Worked example of the consequence: the spool is full and the agent cannot accept a measurement. It blocks the calling thread. The application being measured now slows down because its monitoring is behind. That is normally the worst outcome in telemetry design, and under a no-loss requirement it is the correct one.
   - Stating this cost is the point: no-loss is not a configuration change, it moves the burden onto the measured application.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Billing data needs two things telemetry does not.

1. **Reconciliation.** An independent check that what was billed matches what was observed, run periodically against a separate record.
2. **Regional durability.** Surviving the loss of a region, not merely a replica.
- The recommendation: build them separately. Worked example: a billing pipeline carrying a few thousand high-value records per second with full durability, reconciliation, and backpressure, alongside a telemetry pipeline carrying a million low-value points per second with a drop policy. The expensive guarantees are paid for only where they are needed.

---

> - Q5: Is one partition per consumer mandatory?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - A consumer can own several partitions
>     - One partition has one active group owner.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Partition reassignment must coordinate progress and sink effects.

**What is really being asked**
A factual question about how consumer groups work, and a test of precision.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
The constraint runs one way only.

- One consumer may own many partitions. Worked example: 50 partitions and 10 consumers means 5 partitions each.
- One partition has exactly one active owner within a group, which is what preserves ordering within it.
- The practical consequence: consumer parallelism is capped by partition count. A 51st consumer against 50 partitions sits idle.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
The interesting part is what happens when ownership moves.

- Worked example of the hazard: consumer A owns partition 7, has written records up to offset 5,100, and has committed up to 5,000. A is deemed dead and partition 7 is reassigned to B. B starts at the committed offset 5,000 and re-processes 5,000 to 5,100. Meanwhile A was not actually dead and is still writing.
- Two consumers are briefly applying effects for the same partition. The contribution key absorbs the duplicates, which is exactly why it exists rather than relying on the broker's ownership guarantee.
- The general statement: rebalancing guarantees ownership eventually, not instantly, so the sink must tolerate a brief overlap.

---

> - Q6: Why is query deployment isolation insufficient?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Ingestion and queries may still compete for the same storage resources.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Reserve resources or isolate read replicas/materialized blocks.

**What is really being asked**
Whether you understand that process isolation is not resource isolation.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Separate deployments share whatever sits beneath them.

- Worked example: query workers run in their own containers with their own CPU and memory limits, which looks isolated on every dashboard. They read from the same storage volumes the ingestion path writes to. A large query saturates disk read bandwidth. Ingestion writes slow, consumer lag grows, freshness degrades, and the incident appears to be an ingestion problem.
- The isolation boundary must exist at the level of the contended resource. CPU isolation does nothing for a disk bandwidth problem.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Two real mechanisms.

1. **Reserve resources.** A guaranteed share of input-output operations or bandwidth for the ingestion path, so a query cannot take it.
2. **Isolate read replicas or materialized blocks.** Queries read a separate copy on separate hardware, so contention is physically impossible.
- Worked example of the second: an immutable storage block is written once by ingestion and then copied to query-serving nodes. Queries never touch the disks ingestion is writing to.
- The tradeoff to name: read replicas cost a duplicate copy of the data and add replication lag to query freshness.

---

> - Q7 (premise reversal): Why not drop the log and write directly to the TSDB with a bigger agent spool?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - A sink outage then becomes `100,000` independent recoveries, replay has no shared ordered source, and the agent spool is the weakest disk in the fleet.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Acceptable only for low volume with a durable producer store
>     - At one million points/s the log's cost is cheaper than fleet-wide spool operations.

**What is really being asked**
The premise reversal, and the most valuable question here. The log is the design's largest cost. Is it earning its place?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Three reasons, and all three are needed.

1. **A sink outage becomes 100,000 independent recoveries.**
   - Worked example: with a log, recovery is one thing to manage, with one backlog number and one drain rate. Without it, 100,000 hosts each hold their own backlog, each recovering at its own pace, with no central view of how much data is outstanding or when recovery will finish.
2. **Replay has no shared ordered source.**
   - Worked example: recomputing last Tuesday's rollups requires the original data. Without the log, it exists only in whatever each of 100,000 hosts happened to retain, most of which has been overwritten. Deliberate replay becomes impossible, which removes the ability to fix a transform bug retroactively.
3. **The agent spool is the weakest disk in the fleet.**
   - The sharpest of the three. The spool sits on production hosts, on whatever disk they happen to have, with no replication, sharing space with the application's own data.
   - Worked example: a host's disk fills because of application logs. The spool cannot write and its data is lost. That disk was never provisioned or monitored as a durability tier, and it now holds the only copy of accepted data.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Concede where the idea is right and quantify where it is not.

- It works for low volume with a genuinely durable producer store, which is the direct-write row in the alternatives table.
- At a million points per second, the comparison is 100,000 spool disks that must be sized, monitored, and operated on production hosts, against one log cluster with replication and a single operational story. The log is the cheaper of the two, and the argument is about total operational cost rather than about elegance.

---

> - Q8: The series catalog is down. Do we stop accepting points?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - No
>     - Gateways accept on cached budgets and reconcile new series after the catalog returns.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Bound the reconciliation debt with a cached quota for unknown series so the outage cannot become a cardinality bypass.

**What is really being asked**
Does a control-plane outage become a data-plane outage?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
No. Gateways hold cached budgets and keep accepting.

- Worked example: a 30-minute catalog outage while 1,800,000,000 points are accepted normally. What stops is changing budgets and definitively resolving series never seen before.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
There is a hole in the baseline answer, and naming it is the whole value.

- Worked example of the hole: during the outage a tenant starts emitting a series the gateway has never seen. The gateway cannot check whether it is within budget. If it accepts freely, an attacker or a bad deploy could wait for a catalog outage and create unlimited new series. The outage has become a cardinality bypass.
- The fix: a bounded cached quota for unknown series. Worked example: allow up to 1,000 unknown new series per tenant during an outage, then reject. A legitimate deploy fits comfortably; an explosion is capped.
- "reconciliation debt" is the work deferred until the catalog returns, and the quota is what keeps it small enough to settle.

---

> - Validation scenarios
>   - Agent record racing with flush.
>   - Partial batch append and retry.
>   - Sink write followed by consumer crash.
>   - Replay of an existing aggregate contribution.
>   - Producer counter reset.
>   - Late samples after a rollup closes.
>   - Recovery before log retention expires.
>   - Catalog outage while a tenant creates new series.

**What this says in plain words**
Eight tests, each mapping to a mechanism discussed earlier.

1. **Agent record racing with flush.** The direct test of the swap: record continuously while flushing repeatedly, then assert that the total recorded equals the total sent. This is the test the copy-then-remove anti-pattern fails.
2. **Partial batch append and retry.** Make some partitions fail, then retry the whole batch, and assert the already-durable points are not duplicated.
3. **Sink write followed by consumer crash.** The write-then-commit test: kill the consumer between the two and assert the aggregate is unchanged after reprocessing.
4. **Replay of an existing aggregate contribution.** Deliberately replay an already-applied range and assert every total is identical.
5. **Producer counter reset.** Restart a producer mid-stream and assert no negative rate appears in the output.
6. **Late samples after a rollup closes.** Send a point past the 5-minute window and assert it is either rejected or routed to the repair workflow, never applied silently to a closed window.
7. **Recovery before log retention expires.** The end-to-end drill: create a backlog, measure the drain, and assert recovery finishes with margin.
8. **Catalog outage while a tenant creates new series.** The direct test of the Q8 stretch answer: assert the unknown-series quota caps creation rather than allowing unlimited new series.

**Why it matters**
Each item names a mechanism that could be wrong and the observation that would reveal it.

## Recall card

Five one-liners and a four-sentence closing script, memorized rather than improvised.

---

> - “The agent batches without losing concurrent writes.”

**What this says in plain words**
The buffer swap in nine words: the agent can send a batch while still recording new measurements, and nothing recorded during the handoff is lost.

**Why it matters**
It states the property rather than the mechanism, which is the right level for a recall card.

- Worked example of the property failing: the copy-then-remove approach loses every point written between the copy and the delete. It also "batches", so naming the property is what distinguishes the two.

---

> - “Durable append separates acceptance from query freshness.”

**What this says in plain words**
The moment data is safe and the moment it is visible are two different moments, separated by the log.

**Why it matters**
It is the design's central structural claim in eight words.

- Worked example: during a 2-hour database outage, acceptance continues at 1,000,000 points per second and nothing is lost, while dashboards fall 2 hours behind. Both facts follow from this one sentence.

---

> - “Stable point identity and an atomic sink boundary make replay safe.”

**What this says in plain words**
Two mechanisms working together: an identity that survives a retry, and a sink that records having applied something in the same transaction that applies it.

**Why it matters**
Neither works alone.

- Worked example: a stable identity with a non-atomic sink still doubles the aggregate when a crash lands between recording the marker and applying the effect. An atomic sink with an unstable identity cannot recognize a replayed point as the one it already applied.
- "make replay safe" is the payoff: replay becomes an ordinary recovery action rather than a risk requiring a decision.

---

> - “Cardinality, retention, and catch-up capacity are first-class budgets.”

**What this says in plain words**
Three numbers that are usually left as defaults are treated here as deliberate, owned budgets.

**Why it matters**
Each has an owner and a consequence.

- Worked example of cardinality: 10,000,000 series is roughly 10 gigabytes of shared index memory, and one tenant's bad label can double it.
- Worked example of retention: 24 hours of replicated log is about 13 terabytes, and doubling it doubles the largest storage line.
- Worked example of catch-up: 1.5× headroom drains a 2-hour outage in 4 hours; 1.1× takes 20.
- Calling them first-class means they appear in the requirements table with numbers, not in a configuration file with defaults.

---

> - “The catalog is control plane; acceptance must not depend on it.”

**What this says in plain words**
The series catalog is configuration, and accepting data must not require it to be reachable.

**Why it matters**
It names the static-stability boundary.

- Worked example: a 30-minute catalog outage during which 1,800,000,000 points are accepted from cached budgets, and the only failures are budget edits.
- The stretch qualifier worth remembering alongside it: unknown new series are admitted only within a bounded cached quota, so the outage cannot become a cardinality bypass.

---

> - Wrap-up script
>   - “The design is a swap-buffer agent, a gateway that acknowledges after a durable log quorum, and idempotent consumers feeding a TSDB with rollups.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence one: the architecture in four components, each named by the property that matters rather than by what it is.

**Why it matters**
"swap-buffer", "after a durable log quorum", and "idempotent" are all doing work. Remove those three qualifiers and the sentence describes a design with three known holes.

---

> -   - “An acknowledgment marks durable log acceptance, and sink-side contribution identity protects replay only when stored atomically with aggregation.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence two: what an acknowledgment means, and the precise condition under which replay protection holds.

**Why it matters**
"only when stored atomically with aggregation" is a defeater attached to the claim in the same breath.

- Worked example of the condition failing: the contribution is recorded in a separate cache. Two consumers race, both find it absent, and both apply. The identity existed and protected nothing.
- Stating the condition rather than the bare claim is what makes it defensible under questioning.

---

> -   - “The main tradeoff is the log itself: it buys replay and outage isolation at the price of retention bytes and a second system to run.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence three: the biggest decision, with both sides stated.

- Bought: the ability to replay, and producers that do not feel a sink outage.
- Paid: about 13 terabytes of replicated storage per day of retention, and an entire additional system with its own capacity planning, upgrades, and on-call knowledge.

**Why it matters**
Naming "a second system to run" as a cost is unusual and credible. Most answers count only bytes and machines, and the operational burden is often the larger real cost.

---

> -   - “My weakest assumption is 1.5× catch-up headroom; I would run the sink-outage drill first to validate it.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence four: the flaw, and the specific first action.

**Why it matters**
The assumption is unusually sensitive, which is why it is the weakest.

- Worked example: at 1.5× the surplus is 500,000 per second; at 1.1× it is 100,000. A 27% shortfall in headroom becomes a fivefold increase in recovery time, because the surplus is a small difference between two large numbers.
- "run the drill first" is a concrete, cheap, immediate action, which is what distinguishes owning a weakness from merely acknowledging one.

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Swap-buffer | Exchanging the agent's active buffer for an empty one under a short lock. | Lets recording and sending proceed concurrently without loss. |
| Durable append | Writing to the replicated log and waiting for quorum. | The acknowledgment boundary. |
| Query freshness | How current the data a query can see is. | Deliberately separated from acceptance. |
| Stable point identity | An identity that survives a retry. | Half of what makes replay safe. |
