You are answering as an engineer who owns one clearly-bordered piece of infrastructure end to end. Here the piece is "revocation and status distribution" for a certificate authority: not the whole certificate authority, not issuance, not the trust store on every client.

  - You spend 8 minutes explaining how the issuing key is generated inside a hardware security module.
  - That is 18% of the interview spent on a component the question did not ask about, and the deep dive on the publish-then-advance invariant gets cut.

---

> - Why this is hard
>   - Naive: delete the certificate row and return `404` from a JSON status endpoint.

**What this says in plain words**
The obvious first design. You have a database table of certificates. To revoke one, delete its row. Offer a `GET /certificates/{id}` endpoint that returns `404 Not Found` for deleted rows. Anyone who wants to know whether a certificate is still good calls that endpoint.

**Why it matters**
Naming the naive design first earns you the right to say where it breaks. It also shows that you know why revocation is not "just a status field". The next two lines are the two breaks.

- Worked example of why it looks fine at first
  - 1,000,000 certificates in a table.
  - An operator revokes serial `4711`. The row is gone. `GET /certificates/4711` now returns `404`.
  - A service you wrote yesterday calls that endpoint before trusting a client. It works.
  - It works *only for callers you wrote yesterday*. Every existing TLS library on earth does not know your endpoint exists.

---

>   - First break: ordinary TLS verifiers do not call a custom status REST endpoint.
>   - They need supported authenticated status evidence, and deleting the resource must not erase revocation history.

**What this says in plain words**
Two distinct problems:

1. **Verifiers only speak standard protocols.** OpenSSL, Java's `PKIX` validator, Go's `crypto/x509`, a hardware load balancer, an Oracle database wallet: each already knows how to fetch a CRL or ask an OCSP responder. None of them will call your JSON endpoint. The status must be delivered in the standard formats, and it must be *signed* so the verifier can check it came from the certificate authority.
2. **Deleting is not revoking.** If the row is gone, you cannot later prove that serial `4711` was revoked on a given date for a given reason. Audits, incident reviews, and the standard rule that a revoked serial must keep appearing on the CRL until after expiry all need the history to exist.

**Worked example for (1): the verifier that cannot be taught**

- An appliance load balancer in a customer's data center terminates TLS with a certificate you issued.
- It validates client certificates. Its only configurable revocation option is "CRL URL, refresh every N minutes".
- Your JSON endpoint could be perfect and the appliance would never call it.
- The only way your revocation reaches that appliance is a signed CRL at the URL embedded inside the certificate itself.

**Worked example for (2): the history that vanished**

- 2026-03-01 09:00: a private key is stolen. The operator "revokes" by deleting the row.
- 2026-03-01 09:10: a fresh CRL is built from the table. The deleted serial is not in the table, so the CRL does not list it.
- The certificate is still accepted everywhere. The attacker keeps using the stolen key until the certificate expires naturally.
- Deleting produced the opposite of revocation: it removed the one piece of evidence the CRL needed.

---

>   - Second break: the signed artifact is cached, so a key revoked at 09:00 is still accepted until every cached CRL or OCSP response reaches `nextUpdate`
>     - If the responder is down, verifiers either accept everything (soft-fail) or reject everything (hard-fail).

**What this says in plain words**
Two more problems, and they are the center of the whole question:

1. **Signed status has a shelf life, and the shelf life works against you.** Every CRL and every OCSP response carries a `nextUpdate` timestamp, meaning "trust this answer until then". A verifier that fetched a "good" answer at 08:59 will keep trusting it until `nextUpdate`, no matter what you publish at 09:01.
2. **When the verifier cannot get any answer, it must pick a failure mode.** *Soft-fail* means "could not check, so accept". *Hard-fail* means "could not check, so reject". Soft-fail turns revocation into advice. Hard-fail turns your responder into a component whose outage stops every handshake.

**Worked example for (1): the 24-hour shadow**

- Your CRL is valid for 24 hours (`nextUpdate = thisUpdate + 24 h`).
- A verifier downloads the CRL at 08:00. It is valid until 08:00 the next day.
- At 09:00 you revoke serial `4711` and publish a new CRL by 09:05.
- The verifier is under no obligation to fetch again before 08:00 tomorrow. If it caches for the full validity, serial `4711` is accepted for another 23 hours.
- The publisher was fast (5 minutes). The bound was slow (23 hours). The bound came from the validity window, not from the publisher.

**Worked example for (2): soft-fail versus hard-fail with real numbers**

- 10,000 internal services check OCSP live on every handshake.
- The responder has a 10-minute outage.
- Soft-fail fleet: during the outage every handshake succeeds, including one from a stolen key that was revoked yesterday. Revocation protected nobody for 10 minutes.
- Hard-fail fleet: during the outage every new handshake fails. 10,000 services stop talking to each other. The responder outage is now a company-wide outage.
- Neither answer is free, which is why the source treats the fail policy as a requirement to negotiate, not a detail.

---

>   - Minimum primitives
>     - An append-only ledger with a per-issuer generation
>     - Signed CRL snapshots with a CRL number
>     - HSM-held signing keys
>     - A publish-then-advance pointer
>     - An explicit relying-party policy.

**What this says in plain words**
The five building blocks that every correct answer needs. Each is defined in the term table and unpacked in later sections; here is the one-line version of each:

1. **Append-only ledger with a per-issuer generation.** Revocations are only ever added, never removed. Each addition for one issuer gets the next integer: 1, 2, 3, … That integer is the *generation*. "Everything through generation 57" is a precise, reproducible set.
2. **Signed CRL snapshots with a CRL number.** Each published CRL is a frozen picture of the ledger through some generation, signed by the issuer, and numbered so verifiers can tell newer from older.
3. **HSM-held signing keys.** The private keys that sign CRLs and OCSP responses live inside a hardware security module (HSM). Software asks the HSM to sign; software never holds the key.
4. **A publish-then-advance pointer.** The "current CRL" is a pointer. The pointer moves to a new CRL only after that CRL has been fully written and independently verified. Never before.
5. **An explicit relying-party policy.** Someone writes down, per verifier cohort, what happens when status cannot be checked. It is not left to library defaults.

**What breaks without each one**

- Without (1): two CRLs built "as of 09:05" from a live table can differ, because rows landed between the two reads. You cannot say what a CRL covers.
- Without (2): a verifier cannot tell that a CRL it just fetched is older than the one it already has, so a stale cache can replace a fresh one.
- Without (3): a stolen server image contains the signing key, and the attacker can now sign a CRL that "un-revokes" their certificate.
- Without (4): a crash halfway through writing a CRL leaves a truncated or unsigned file at the public URL. Every verifier fails to parse it.
- Without (5): each team's TLS library decides soft-fail or hard-fail for them, and nobody knows what the fleet does during an outage until the outage.

---

> - 🗣 "Revocation is a signed, cached, eventually consistent negative; the design is bounding how stale that negative can be without making the issuer a handshake dependency."

**What this says in plain words**
The crux sentence, to be spoken aloud. Split into its four claims:

1. **Signed**: the verifier only believes a "revoked" statement that carries the issuer's signature.
2. **Cached**: verifiers hold onto the last answer they got, for as long as `nextUpdate` allows.
3. **Eventually consistent negative**: "revoked" is a negative statement ("do not trust this"), and it reaches verifiers *eventually*, not instantly, because of the cache.
4. **Bounding staleness without a handshake dependency**: the design goal is to make "eventually" as short as possible while never requiring the certificate authority to be online for a handshake to succeed.

**Why it matters**
This sentence tells the interviewer you understand the real tension before you draw a single box. The tension is: the faster you want revocation to propagate, the shorter the validity windows must be, and the shorter the windows, the more often verifiers must fetch, and the more a fetch failure becomes an outage.

- Worked example of the tension in numbers
  - Validity 24 h: a verifier can survive a 23-hour publisher outage. A stolen key can be accepted for up to 24 h.
  - Validity 1 h: a stolen key is accepted for at most 1 h. A 61-minute publisher outage makes every hard-fail verifier reject every certificate.
  - Validity 5 min: a stolen key is accepted for at most 5 min. Any CDN hiccup longer than 5 minutes is a fleet outage.

## Requirements — IC3 baseline

This section turns the interviewer's prompt into numbers. It has five parts: a note on where numbers come from, the clarifying-questions table, the functional scope, the non-functional-requirements table, the invariants, and the non-goals.

---

> - Numerical targets are interview assumptions unless an adjacent primary source states otherwise.

**What this says in plain words**
Every number in this section (1 hour, 24 hours, 1,000,000 certificates) is something you propose and the interviewer either accepts or changes. None of it is quoted from a product manual unless a citation sits next to it.

**Why it matters**
If you state "the CRL is valid for 24 hours" as a fact, the interviewer can ask "according to whom?". If you state it as "I will assume 24 hours; tell me if your fleet needs tighter", the same number becomes a design decision you own.

---

> | Question | Assumed answer | Why it changes the design |
> |---|---|---|
> | Who are the relying parties? | OCI load balancers, gateways, internal mTLS services we control; some appliances and DB wallets that only fetch CRLs | CRL is mandatory for the appliance cohort; OCSP is an addition for the controlled cohort |
> | Accepted propagation bound after key compromise? | `≤ 1 h` for controlled verifiers; `≤ 24 h` for CRL-only consumers | Fixes CRL `nextUpdate`, OCSP validity, and CDN `max-age` |
> | Revocation volume? | `1M` active certificates, `< 1%` revoked per year, spikes of `10k` in an hour | Sizes CRL bytes; forces debounced republish |
> | Is OCSP on the handshake path? | No for servers we control (they staple); optionally for clients | Responder must be statically stable and serve precomputed responses |
> | Soft-fail or hard-fail? | Owned by the relying party; we publish an SLO that makes hard-fail viable for stapled servers | Sets responder availability target and staple refresh schedule |
> | Multi-tenant? | Yes; every issuer lives in a tenant compartment | Authorization and artifacts are per issuer; never a global CRL |

**What this says in plain words**
Six questions to ask in the first six minutes, each with the answer you assume if the interviewer shrugs, and the design consequence. Row by row:

**Row: Who are the relying parties?**

- Two cohorts of verifiers.
  1. The *controlled* cohort: load balancers, API gateways, and internal services doing mutual TLS (mTLS, where both sides present certificates). You run their software. You can make them staple OCSP responses and set their fail policy.
  2. The *appliance* cohort: hardware boxes and Oracle database wallets. They can be pointed at a CRL URL and nothing else.
- Consequence: the CRL is not optional. It is the only thing the appliance cohort can consume. OCSP is a second artifact added for the controlled cohort's benefit.
- Worked example: if you designed OCSP-only, a customer's database wallet configured to check a CRL would find no CRL at the embedded URL and, depending on its settings, either accept everything or reject everything.

**Row: Accepted propagation bound after key compromise?**

- The propagation bound is the longest time between "operator clicks revoke" and "no verifier accepts the certificate anymore".
- Assumed: 1 hour for the controlled cohort, 24 hours for the CRL-only cohort.
- Consequence: those two numbers directly set three knobs.
  1. CRL `nextUpdate` (how long a downloaded CRL stays acceptable).
  2. OCSP response validity.
  3. The CDN's `max-age` (how long an edge cache may serve a copy without re-checking).
- Worked example: if the bound is 24 hours and the CRL validity is 24 hours, then publish lag plus CDN cache must fit in the remaining margin, which is why later rows hold those to 5 minutes each.

**Row: Revocation volume?**

- 1,000,000 active certificates. Less than 1% revoked per year, so about 10,000 per year. Incident spikes of 10,000 in one hour (for example, one compromised intermediate and every leaf under it).
- Consequence 1: CRL size. 10,000 entries at about 50 bytes each is about 0.5 MB.
- Consequence 2: the publisher must *debounce*. If 10,000 revocations arrive in an hour, that is about 3 per second. Publishing a new CRL per revocation would mean 3 signatures and 3 CDN purges per second for nothing; grouping them into one publish per minute is the fix.

**Row: Is OCSP on the handshake path?**

- For servers you control: no. They fetch their own OCSP response ahead of time and *staple* it into the TLS handshake, so the client never calls the responder.
- For clients: optionally. Some clients may make a live OCSP call.
- Consequence: the responder must survive control-plane outages (static stability) and must answer from precomputed, already-signed responses, because a live signature per request would put the HSM on the hot path.

**Row: Soft-fail or hard-fail?**

- The fail policy is the relying party's decision. You cannot force a customer's appliance to hard-fail.
- What you can do is publish a service-level objective (SLO) for the responder and the CRL endpoint that is good enough that hard-fail is a reasonable choice for the servers you control.
- Consequence: the SLO number (99.99% later) and the staple refresh schedule (hourly, with 8-hour validity) are chosen so that a stapled server can hard-fail without becoming fragile.

**Row: Multi-tenant?**

- Yes. Each issuer belongs to one tenant's compartment (OCI's unit of authorization scoping).
- Consequence: authorization checks are per issuer, CRLs and OCSP snapshots are per issuer, and there is never one CRL for all tenants.
- Worked example of why a global CRL is wrong: 10,000 issuers, one global CRL. Tenant A revokes 10,000 leaves in an incident. Every tenant's verifiers now download a 5 MB file that grew because of tenant A, and a bad publish caused by tenant A's spike breaks tenant B's freshness.

---

> - Functional scope
>   - Revoke a certificate by `(issuer, serial)` with a reason and optional invalidity date.
>   - Build, sign, and publish a per-issuer CRL on schedule and on demand.
>   - Answer OCSP requests per RFC 6960 with a signed status for one `CertID`.
>   - Supply fresh OCSP responses to servers we control for stapling.
>   - Expose administrative revocation status and the publication generation.
>   - Audit every revocation, publication, and signature.

**What this says in plain words**
The six things the system must do:

1. **Revoke by `(issuer, serial)`.** The caller names the issuer and the serial number, gives a reason code (for example `keyCompromise`), and may give an *invalidity date*, the moment the key is believed to have been compromised, which can be earlier than the revocation time.
2. **Build, sign, publish a CRL per issuer.** On a schedule (every hour) and on demand (an operator forces it after an incident).
3. **Answer OCSP per RFC 6960.** RFC 6960 is the OCSP standard. A `CertID` is the request's way of naming one certificate: hashes of the issuer's name and key plus the serial.
4. **Feed staplers.** Servers you control fetch their own response from the responder and present it during the handshake.
5. **Administrative status.** An operator can ask "is serial X revoked, and has that revocation been published yet?" This is a JSON view for humans and tooling, not a status protocol.
6. **Audit everything.** Every revocation, every publish, every signature call leaves a record.

**Why it matters**
Item 5 is easy to forget and is the one that makes the `202` API honest: "revoked" (in the ledger) and "published" (on a signed CRL) are two different states, and an operator needs to see both.

---

> | Requirement | Target | Enforced by |
> |---|---|---|
> | Revocation accept latency | p99 < `500 ms` to a durable `202` | Single-transaction ledger write plus outbox; no signing on the request path |
> | Publication lag | `≤ 5 min` normal; `≤ 60 s` on forced publish | `60 s` per-issuer debounce; forced publish bypasses it; CDN purge |
> | CRL validity | `nextUpdate = thisUpdate + 24 h`; republished every `1 h` | Publisher schedule; page when `nextUpdate − now < 6 h` |
> | OCSP response validity | `nextUpdate = thisUpdate + 8 h`; re-signed every `1 h` | Precompute batch per issuer; page when oldest response age `> 2 h` |
> | OCSP responder availability | `99.99%` monthly; p99 < `20 ms` | Stateless responders on an in-memory snapshot; spread across availability domains |
> | CRL download availability | `99.99%`; independent of control-plane health | Object storage plus CDN; a signed CRL validates without the service |
> | Static stability | Continue until the earliest required evidence deadline; test 8 h boundary | Cached signed status avoids live fetch only while still acceptable; do not promise a universal 24 h outage |
> | Retention | Revocation rows kept `≥ notAfter + 30 d`; audit `7 y` | No `DELETE` grant; archive job gated by the expiry rule |
> | Signing key custody | 0 plaintext private-key exports; 100% durable signing intents | HSM sign path and attempt auditing; responder validity ≤ 30 d |

**What this says in plain words**
The non-functional requirements (NFRs): a number, and the mechanism that makes the number true. Row by row:

**Row: Revocation accept latency**

- 99 out of 100 revocation calls return a durable `202 Accepted` in under half a second.
- How: the request path does exactly one database transaction (ledger row plus an outbox row) and never waits for a signature.
- Worked example: an HSM signature takes 2 ms on a good day and 2 seconds under throttling. If signing were on the request path, the p99 would be hostage to the HSM. With the outbox, the p99 is a single transaction commit, about 5–20 ms.

**Row: Publication lag**

- From a revocation landing in the ledger to a new CRL being public: at most 5 minutes normally, at most 60 seconds when an operator forces it.
- How: the publisher waits 60 seconds per issuer to coalesce bursts (the *debounce*); a forced publish skips the wait; after publishing, the CDN cache is purged so edges fetch the new file.
- Worked example: 200 revocations for one issuer arrive between 09:00:00 and 09:00:45. One publish at 09:01:00 covers all 200. Without the debounce: 200 signatures and 200 CDN purges in 45 seconds.

**Row: CRL validity**

- Each CRL says "trust me for 24 hours" but a new one is published every hour.
- How: the hourly schedule. An alarm pages a human when the newest published CRL for any issuer has less than 6 hours of validity left, because that means about 18 hours of scheduled publishes have failed.
- Worked example: the publisher dies at 03:00. The last CRL was signed at 03:00, valid until 03:00 next day. At 21:00 (6 hours left) the page fires. That leaves 6 hours to fix the publisher before verifiers start rejecting for lack of a fresh CRL.

**Row: OCSP response validity**

- Each precomputed OCSP response is valid for 8 hours but is re-signed hourly.
- How: a batch job per issuer. An alarm pages when the oldest response in the snapshot is over 2 hours old, meaning at least one hourly cycle has been missed.
- Why 8 hours and not 24: under stapling, the validity of the last `good` response is the window an attacker with a stolen key gets. 8 hours is the compromise between that window and the ability to survive a multi-hour control-plane outage.

**Row: OCSP responder availability**

- 99.99% per month, which is about 4.3 minutes of downtime allowed. 99 out of 100 answers in under 20 ms.
- How: responders keep no state, serve from memory, and are spread across availability domains (independent data-center failure zones) so one zone's loss does not take the fleet down.

**Row: CRL download availability**

- 99.99%, and, crucially, independent of whether the control plane is up.
- How: the CRL is a file on object storage behind a CDN. A verifier that downloads it checks the issuer's signature on the file itself. No service needs to be running for that check to succeed.

**Row: Static stability**

- Verifiers keep working through a control-plane outage until the earliest deadline at which some verifier needs fresh evidence it does not have. Test the 8-hour boundary explicitly, because that is where stapled servers run out.
- Do not claim "we survive 24 hours of outage" for everyone: stapled servers hold 8-hour responses, so a strict stapled server fails after 8 hours even though CRL consumers have 24.
- Worked example: outage begins at 10:00. Stapled servers have responses signed at 09:00–10:00, valid until 17:00–18:00. CRL consumers have CRLs valid until 09:00–10:00 next day. The first hard failures are stapled servers at 17:00, not CRL consumers.

**Row: Retention**

- A revocation row stays at least 30 days after the certificate's `notAfter` (its expiry). Audit records stay 7 years.
- How: no database role has `DELETE` on the revocations table. An archive job may move rows out, but only rows whose `not_after + 30 d` has passed.
- Why 30 days after expiry: the standard says an entry must appear on one scheduled CRL issued after expiry. Hourly publishes make that one hour, but 30 days covers publisher outages and clock skew with margin.

**Row: Signing key custody**

- Zero times, ever, does a private key exist outside the HSM in plaintext. Every signing attempt is recorded durably (a *signing intent*) before the signature is requested.
- How: services call the HSM's sign operation with a key handle; the HSM logs each call; the OCSP responder certificate is valid at most 30 days so a leaked responder key has a short life.

---

> - Invariants
>   - A durable revocation appears on every later CRL of that issuer until the certificate's `notAfter` has passed and one scheduled CRL after that has been issued (RFC 5280 §3.3).

**What this says in plain words**
Once a revocation is committed, every CRL the issuer publishes afterwards lists it, up to and including the first scheduled CRL after the certificate has expired. Only then may it drop off. This is the rule from RFC 5280, the certificate and CRL standard.

**What breaks without it**
A verifier with a slightly slow clock, or a CRL that dropped the serial one publish too early, accepts an expired-but-revoked certificate for a window. The "one CRL after expiry" rule closes that gap for every verifier whose clock is within one publish interval of correct.

- Worked example: serial `4711` expires at 12:00. CRLs are published hourly. The 12:00 CRL still lists it (expiry has just passed; the "one scheduled CRL after" is the 13:00 one). The 13:00 CRL lists it. The 14:00 CRL may drop it. A verifier whose clock is 40 minutes slow still sees it on the 13:00 CRL at what it believes is 12:20.

---

>   - Revocation generation and CRL number are strictly monotonic per issuer.

**What this says in plain words**
For one issuer, generations go 1, 2, 3, … with no repeats and no going backward. CRL numbers do the same. Two different issuers may both have generation 7; that is fine.

**What breaks without it**
If CRL number 41 could be published after CRL number 42, a verifier that already holds 42 would either reject 41 (good) or replace 42 with 41 (bad, revocations vanish). Monotonic numbers let the verifier always prefer the higher one.

---

>   - The public pointer moves only to a snapshot that was fully written, re-downloaded, and signature-verified.

**What this says in plain words**
The "current CRL" pointer (and the `latest` alias verifiers download) never references a file that has not been (1) completely uploaded, (2) fetched back from storage as a verifier would fetch it, and (3) checked to carry a valid signature by the right issuer key.

**What breaks without it**
A crash after 60% of the upload leaves a truncated file at the public URL. Every verifier that fetches it gets a parse error. Hard-fail verifiers now reject everything from that issuer until someone notices.

---

>   - OCSP `good` is produced only from a signed snapshot with a known `through_generation`
>   - A store outage never produces `good`.

**What this says in plain words**
Two rules for the OCSP responder:

1. The answer `good` may only come from a precomputed, signed snapshot that records which generation of the ledger it covers.
2. If the snapshot is unavailable, the responder answers `tryLater`, never `good`.

**What breaks without it**
A responder that computes `good` as "I did not find a revocation row" turns every database outage into "everything is good", including the certificate revoked five minutes ago. The absence of evidence must never be presented as evidence of absence.

---

>   - Every status artifact is signed by a key that never leaves the HSM.

**What this says in plain words**
Both CRLs and OCSP responses are signed by keys held inside the HSM. Software gets a handle to the key, never the key.

**What breaks without it**
A compromised publisher host contains the CRL signing key. The attacker signs a CRL that omits their revoked serial and uploads it. Every verifier accepts it because the signature is genuine.

---

>   - Dropping or archiving a row never turns revoked into good.

**What this says in plain words**
No maintenance operation (archive, cleanup, migration) may cause a serial that was revoked to be reported as not revoked.

**What breaks without it**
The naive design's second break returns through the back door: an archive job runs early, the next CRL omits the serial, and the certificate is trusted again. Later sections add an *additive-diff guard* that refuses to publish a CRL that dropped an unexpired serial.

---

> - Non-goals
>   - `certificateHold` and `removeFromCRL`.

**What this says in plain words**
RFC 5280 allows a temporary suspension (`certificateHold`) that can later be undone (`removeFromCRL`). This design does not support either. Revocation is permanent.

**Why it matters**
Supporting un-revocation breaks the append-only ledger model and the additive-diff guard. Saying so up front avoids a 10-minute detour.

---

>   - Browser-vendor push filters such as CRLite or CRLSets.

**What this says in plain words**
Mozilla's CRLite and Chrome's CRLSets are systems where the browser vendor collects all revocations and pushes a compact filter to every browser. That only works when one party controls the entire verifier fleet. This design serves customers' verifiers, so it is out of scope.

---

>   - Path validation, validity dates, and hostname checks
>   - OCSP `good` does not replace them.

**What this says in plain words**
Checking that the certificate chain leads to a trusted root, that the current time is within `notBefore`/`notAfter`, and that the name in the certificate matches the host being contacted: those are the verifier's job. An OCSP `good` answer says only "not revoked". A verifier that treats `good` as "fully valid" has a bug.

---

>   - Revoking a root
>   - That is a trust-store change handled in [s2.02](s2.02.Certificate-renewal-and-CA-rollover.md).

**What this says in plain words**
A root certificate is self-signed, so no CRL can meaningfully revoke it. Removing a root means changing every verifier's trust store, which is the topic of the CA-rollover question, not this one.

## Capacity and consequence — IC3 baseline

Seven estimates, each ending in a decision. The last one is not a size at all — it is the propagation bound, and it is the most important number in the file.

---

> - Revocation writes
>   - `1M × 1% / year ≈ 10k / year ≈ 30 / day`
>   - Incident spike `10k / h ≈ 3 / s` against one issuer.
>   - Decision: one serialized generation counter per issuer row is safe
>     - The ledger needs no write sharding.

**What this says in plain words**
Revoking certificates is rare. Even a bad day is three per second.

**The arithmetic**

1. One million certificates, of which about 1% are revoked in a year, is 10,000 revocations per year.
2. 10,000 ÷ 365 ≈ 30 per day. That is the steady state, and it is nothing.
3. The spike case is a mass revocation during an incident: 10,000 in an hour ≈ 3 per second, all against **one issuer**.

**Why the spike shape matters more than the rate**

- Worked example of the incident: a subordinate CA's key is suspected compromised, so every certificate it issued must be revoked at once. That load does not spread across issuers — it lands entirely on the one that was compromised.
- So the question is not "can the system take 3 writes per second?" but "can *one issuer's* write path take 3 per second?" The answer is comfortably yes, and that is what makes the next decision safe.

**The decision**

- **"One serialized generation counter per issuer row is safe."**
  - A **generation counter** is a number that increments on every revocation for that issuer. It is what makes "everything through generation `g`" a precise statement about a snapshot.
  - Serializing on one row means every revocation for that issuer takes the row's lock in turn, so generations are strictly ordered with no gaps and no ties.
  - Worked example of why the ordering is needed: a CRL published "through generation 47" is a complete, unambiguous claim — it contains every revocation numbered 47 or lower and nothing else. Without a serialized counter there would be no such thing as "through generation 47", because two revocations could share a number or a number could be skipped.
- **"The ledger needs no write sharding."** Three per second against one row is nowhere near a bottleneck, so the simplest correct structure is also the right one.
- Note what this rules out, stated later: a *global* counter across all issuers. Worked example of why that would be wrong: 10,000 issuers all incrementing one row would serialize every revocation in the system behind a single lock, and a mass revocation on one issuer would block every other issuer's revocations for the duration.

---

> - CRL bytes
>   - Each DER entry is roughly `40–60 B`: serial up to `20 B`, time `≈ 15 B`, reason extension `≈ 10 B` (order of magnitude).
>   - `10k` unexpired entries `≈ 0.5 MB`
>   - `100k ≈ 5 MB`.
>   - Decision: CRLs are per issuer and entries leave after expiry
>     - Delta or partitioned CRLs become a stretch item only past `≈ 10 MB`.

**What this says in plain words**
A revocation list entry is tiny, so a list stays small — as long as entries are removed when the certificates they name expire.

**The arithmetic, built from the ground up**

- **DER** is the binary encoding X.509 uses. One revocation entry holds three things:
  - The serial number, up to 20 bytes.
  - The revocation time, about 15 bytes.
  - The reason extension, about 10 bytes.
- That is roughly 40–60 bytes per entry, and the source flags it as an order-of-magnitude estimate rather than a measurement.
- 10,000 entries × 50 bytes ≈ 0.5 megabytes. 100,000 entries ≈ 5 megabytes.

**The decision, and the clause that does the work**

- **"Entries leave after expiry."** This is the detail that keeps CRLs from growing without bound, and it follows from what a CRL is for.
  - Worked example: a certificate revoked in March with a `notAfter` of June. From July onward, no verifier will accept it anyway — it is expired. Listing it as revoked adds bytes and changes nothing.
  - So the steady-state size is not "all revocations ever", it is "revocations of certificates that have not yet expired". With 90-day certificates, an entry lives at most 90 days.
  - Worked example of the difference over five years: without expiry-based removal, a 1% annual revocation rate accumulates 50,000 entries. With it, the list holds at most a quarter's worth — about 2,500 — and stays flat forever.
- **"Delta or partitioned CRLs become a stretch item only past ≈ 10 MB."**
  - A **delta CRL** lists only what changed since a base CRL, so verifiers fetch a small increment instead of the whole list. A **partitioned CRL** splits one issuer's list across several URLs.
  - Both are real mechanisms and both add complexity. The decision says: do not build them until the list is actually large, and at 0.5 megabytes it is not.
  - Worked example of the threshold being the right one: 10 megabytes fetched hourly by 10,000 consumers is 100 gigabytes of transfer per hour, which is when the CDN bill and the fetch latency start to matter. Below that, one file is simpler and fine.

---

> - CRL signing
>   - `10k` issuers `× 24` republishes per day `≈ 240k` signatures per day `≈ 3 / s`.
>   - Decision: CRL signing does not size the HSM
>     - OCSP precompute does.

**What this says in plain words**
Signing every issuer's CRL every hour is three signatures per second. That is not what the hardware is bought for.

**The arithmetic**
10,000 issuers × 24 republishes per day = 240,000 signatures per day ÷ 86,400 seconds ≈ 2.8 per second.

**Why this estimate exists**
Its whole purpose is to rule something out and point at the real constraint.

- Worked example of the reasoning: an HSM signing at hundreds per second is using roughly 1% of its capacity on CRLs. If you sized the hardware from this number you would buy one small device and immediately discover it cannot serve the OCSP path.
- **"OCSP precompute does"** is the pointer forward. Naming which workload actually sets the hardware requirement — and showing the arithmetic that excludes the other candidate — is the difference between having numbers and having a capacity argument.

---

> - OCSP precompute
>   - `1M` certificates re-signed every `1 h ≈ 280` signatures per second.
>   - At an assumed mean signing time of 2 ms, 280/s implies about 0.56 average signatures in flight.
>   - This does not establish per-partition throughput or p99 under contention.
>   - Benchmark the selected HSM, algorithm mix, and failure headroom before allocating capacity.
>   - Decision: precompute for every unexpired certificate
>     - The refresh interval is the HSM sizing knob, and lazy sign-on-miss is only an overflow path.

**What this says in plain words**
Pre-signing a status response for every certificate, every hour, is 280 signatures per second — a hundred times the CRL load. And then three lines of unusually careful hedging about what that number does and does not prove.

**The arithmetic**

1. 1,000,000 certificates ÷ 3,600 seconds ≈ 278, rounded to 280 signatures per second.
2. At an assumed 2 milliseconds per signature, 280 per second means 280 × 0.002 = 0.56 signatures in flight on average.

**Why the hedging is the most instructive part**

> This does not establish per-partition throughput or p99 under contention.
> Benchmark the selected HSM, algorithm mix, and failure headroom before allocating capacity.

- "0.56 average signatures in flight" sounds like enormous headroom, and the source immediately refuses to treat it as such. Three reasons are named:
  - **Per-partition throughput.** An HSM's rated speed may be for the whole device, while your keys live in one partition with its own limit. Worked example: a device rated at 1,000 signatures per second across eight partitions may give 125 per second in the partition that holds this issuer's key — less than half of what is needed.
  - **p99 under contention.** An average of 0.56 in flight says nothing about the tail. Worked example: if the HSM occasionally stalls for 200 milliseconds under concurrent load, the queue builds during those stalls and a precompute cycle that must finish within the hour can miss its window even though the average is comfortable.
  - **Failure headroom.** Sizing for exactly 280 per second means an HSM failing over to a second device must carry the whole load alone. Worked example: two devices each sized at 300 per second is fine until one dies, at which point the survivor needs 280 and has 300 — no room for a spike or a re-sign burst.
- The general discipline worth copying: **an average utilization figure is not a capacity plan.** Say what it does not establish, then say what to measure.

**The decision**

- **"Precompute for every unexpired certificate."** Sign every status response in advance, on a schedule, rather than on demand.
  - Worked example of why: signing on demand puts the HSM on the hot path of every OCSP request. At 5,000 requests per second that is 5,000 signatures per second — far beyond the hardware — and a client waits on hardware latency for every status check.
- **"The refresh interval is the HSM sizing knob."** The one parameter that trades freshness against hardware cost.
  - Worked example: halving the interval to 30 minutes doubles the signing rate to 560 per second and halves how stale a cached response can be. Doubling it to 2 hours halves the load and doubles the staleness. Every other part of the design is fixed; this is the dial.
- **"Lazy sign-on-miss is only an overflow path."** If a lookup finds no precomputed response, sign one on demand — but that is the exception, not the design.
  - Worked example of why it must stay an exception: a certificate issued five minutes ago has no precomputed response yet. Signing one on demand serves that request correctly. If a large fraction of requests took that path, the hot path would be back on the HSM.

---

> - OCSP reads
>   - Assumed `5k` requests per second at peak after stapling absorbs servers
>   - `≈ 1.5 KB` per response `≈ 7.5 MB/s`.
>   - Decision: responders read from an in-memory snapshot keyed by `CertID`
>     - No database on the read path.

**What this says in plain words**
Five thousand status requests per second, and the design's answer is that serving one must not touch a database or the hardware.

**The arithmetic**
5,000 requests per second × 1.5 kilobytes ≈ 7.5 megabytes per second.

**"After stapling absorbs servers" is the clause that makes 5,000 plausible**

- With **stapling**, a TLS server fetches a signed status for its own certificate every few hours and attaches it to each handshake. Clients then make no OCSP request at all.
- Worked example of the reduction: 10,000 servers each handling 1,000 handshakes per second is 10,000,000 potential client OCSP requests per second. With stapling it is 10,000 servers refreshing every 4 hours — about 0.7 requests per second — plus whatever direct client traffic remains. The 5,000 figure is what is left after stapling has removed the bulk.

**The decision**

- **"Responders read from an in-memory snapshot keyed by `CertID`."**
  - A **`CertID`** is how OCSP names a certificate: a hash of the issuer's name, a hash of the issuer's public key, and the serial number. A request carries one; the responder looks it up.
  - The snapshot is the precomputed, already-signed responses, held in memory.
  - Worked example of the resulting request path: parse the request, extract the `CertID`, look it up in a hash map, return the bytes. No parsing of certificates, no signing, no query, no network call.
- **"No database on the read path"** is the property to claim, and it has two payoffs.
  - **Latency.** A memory lookup is microseconds; a database query is milliseconds even when healthy.
  - **Availability.** Worked example: the ledger database is down for 20 minutes. OCSP responders keep serving from their snapshots throughout, returning slightly stale but perfectly valid signed responses. The revocation *control plane* is down; the revocation *answer path* is not.
- This is the same control-plane and data-plane separation as the sibling questions, applied to revocation status.

---

> - CRL fetches
>   - `10k` consumers refreshing hourly `≈ 3 / s`
>   - A restart storm of `10k` in one minute `≈ 170 / s × 0.5 MB ≈ 85 MB/s`.
>   - Decision: CDN fronts object storage
>     - `If-None-Match` turns unchanged fetches into `304`.

**What this says in plain words**
The steady state is trivial and the restart case is 56 times worse, which is the one to size for.

**The arithmetic**

1. Steady state: 10,000 consumers ÷ 3,600 seconds ≈ 2.8 fetches per second.
2. Restart storm: 10,000 consumers in 60 seconds ≈ 170 per second, each pulling a 0.5-megabyte CRL ≈ 85 megabytes per second.

**Why the restart case is real rather than hypothetical**
Worked example: a fleet-wide deploy, a region recovering from an outage, or a configuration rollback. Every consumer starts fresh with no cached CRL and fetches immediately.

**The decision**

- **CDN in front of object storage.** Every consumer wants the identical bytes, which is exactly what a CDN is built for.
  - Worked example of the offload: 85 megabytes per second at the origin becomes essentially zero, because the first request at each edge populates the cache and the rest are served locally.
- **`If-None-Match` turns unchanged fetches into `304`.**
  - The consumer sends the ETag of the CRL it already holds. If the published CRL has not changed, the response is `304 Not Modified` with no body.
  - Worked example of the saving in steady state: 3 fetches per second of 0.5 megabytes is 1.5 megabytes per second forever, for a file that changes hourly at most. With conditional requests it is a few hundred bytes per second.

---

> - Propagation bound
>   - Worst-case acceptance after revocation `= publish lag (5 min) + CDN max-age (5 min) + remaining validity of the artifact the verifier already holds (≤ 24 h CRL, ≤ 8 h OCSP)`.
>   - Decision: validity dominates the bound, not publisher speed
>     - Shorten `nextUpdate` rather than optimize the publisher.
>   - Decision: under stapling the OCSP validity window is the attacker's window, because a compromised server can keep stapling its last `good` response until `nextUpdate`.

**What this says in plain words**
The most important number in the file, and it is not a throughput figure. It is: after you revoke a certificate, how long can it still be accepted?

**The formula, term by term**
Worst-case acceptance = publish lag + CDN max-age + remaining validity of what the verifier already holds.

1. **Publish lag, 5 minutes.** The time from the revocation being recorded to a new artifact being published. This includes the publisher's debounce.
2. **CDN max-age, 5 minutes.** How long an edge may serve the previous artifact before re-fetching.
3. **Remaining validity of the artifact the verifier already holds — up to 24 hours for a CRL, up to 8 hours for an OCSP response.** The verifier fetched a signed artifact some time ago. That artifact is *valid* until its `nextUpdate`, so a correct verifier has no reason to re-fetch before then.

**Worked example with the numbers**
A certificate is revoked at 09:00.

- The new CRL is published by 09:05.
- An edge that had cached the old one serves it until 09:10 at the latest.
- But a verifier that fetched the *previous* CRL at 08:50, with a 24-hour `nextUpdate`, will not fetch again until 08:50 tomorrow — and it is behaving correctly.
- So the revoked certificate can be accepted until roughly 08:50 the next morning: nearly 24 hours, of which 10 minutes came from the publisher and the rest from validity.

**The first decision, and why it redirects effort**

> Validity dominates the bound, not publisher speed. Shorten `nextUpdate` rather than optimize the publisher.

- The two publisher-side terms contribute 10 minutes out of nearly 24 hours — about 0.7% of the bound.
- Worked example of optimizing the wrong thing: heroic engineering that cuts publish lag from 5 minutes to 5 seconds improves the worst case from 23h 55m to 23h 50m. Nobody can measure the difference.
- Worked example of the right lever: cutting the CRL's `nextUpdate` from 24 hours to 6 cuts the worst case to about 6 hours — a four-fold improvement from changing one field.
- The cost of the lever is stated by arithmetic elsewhere: a shorter `nextUpdate` means more frequent republishes and more frequent verifier fetches. That is the actual trade, and it is a capacity question rather than an engineering-effort one.
- The general principle: **when a bound is a sum, find which term dominates before optimizing any of them.**

**The second decision, which is a security observation rather than a capacity one**

> Under stapling the OCSP validity window is the attacker's window, because a compromised server can keep stapling its last `good` response until `nextUpdate`.

- This deserves careful reading, because it inverts the usual benefit of stapling.
- With stapling, the **server** fetches its own status and presents it to clients. A client does not check independently — it trusts the stapled response because it is signed by the responder.
- Worked example of the attack: a server's private key is compromised at 10:00. At 10:05 the certificate is revoked. But the attacker holds a signed OCSP response saying `good`, fetched at 09:30 with an 8-hour `nextUpdate`. They keep stapling it. Every client sees a valid, correctly signed, unexpired proof that the certificate is fine — until 17:30.
- The attacker does not need to forge anything or block anything. They simply keep presenting a truthful-when-issued response that has not yet expired.
- So under stapling, **the OCSP `nextUpdate` is precisely how long a compromise remains exploitable**, and it is the attacker who controls whether a fresher response is fetched.
- Worked example of the mitigation this implies: an 8-hour window is an 8-hour compromise. Shortening it to 1 hour shortens the exposure eightfold, at eight times the precompute cost — which is the same `nextUpdate` dial as above, now with a security argument attached rather than a freshness one.
- The general shape worth taking away: **when a party that may be compromised controls the freshness of its own evidence, the evidence's lifetime is the exposure window.**

## Architecture and flow — IC3 baseline

A two-zone diagram with a trust note, seven components each with what it buys and what it costs, then one revocation traced from the API call to verifiers seeing it.

---

> ```text
> [control plane]                                            [data plane / hot path]
> Operator / automation
>   -> Revocation API (compartment authz, Idempotency-Key, 202 + operationId)
>   -> Ledger DB: issuer_status | revocations | crl_snapshots | outbox | audit
>         |
>         +-> CRL publisher (per issuer, ordered, debounced)
>         |      -> HSM/KMS sign(CRL, crl_key_ref)
>         |      -> Object Storage  crl/<issuer>/<crl_number>.crl  (immutable)
>         |      -> verify download -> advance pointer -> alias "latest" -> CDN purge
>         |                                                    |
>         |                                              CDN <- verifiers fetch the CDP URL (CRL)
>         |
>         +-> OCSP precompute (per issuer, every 1 h)
>                -> HSM/KMS sign(response, responder_key_ref)
>                -> Response snapshot (object storage + in-memory on responders)
>                                                              |
>                                           OCSP responder fleet (stateless, AD-spread)
>                                               ^                          ^
>                                    TLS servers (fetch + staple)   clients (live OCSP, optional)
>
> Trust: issuer CA cert (KU cRLSign) signs CRLs;
>        delegated responder cert (EKU OCSPSigning, <= 30 d) signs OCSP responses.
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
One write path into a ledger, then two independent publication paths — CRL and OCSP — feeding two different kinds of consumer. The trust note at the bottom says which key signs which artifact, and it is not the same key.

**The shape to notice first: one truth, two representations**

- A revocation is recorded **once**, in the ledger.
- It is then published **twice**, in two formats: as an entry in a signed list (CRL), and as a signed per-certificate answer (OCSP).
- Worked example of why both exist: a CRL is one file that any verifier can cache and use offline, but it is coarse — you fetch the whole list to check one certificate, and its freshness is bounded by the list's `nextUpdate`. An OCSP response is per-certificate and can be much fresher, but it requires an online responder. Different consumers want different trades, so the design publishes both from one source of truth.
- The critical property: because both derive from the same ledger and the same generation counter, they cannot disagree about *what* was revoked — only about *how recently* they were built.

**The control plane, top to bottom**

- `Operator / automation -> Revocation API (compartment authz, Idempotency-Key, 202 + operationId)`
  - Three things in the parenthesis: authorization scoped to the issuer's compartment, an idempotency key so a retry is not a second revocation, and a `202` with an operation id because publishing takes time.
- `-> Ledger DB: issuer_status | revocations | crl_snapshots | outbox | audit`
  - Five tables. `issuer_status` holds the generation counter; `revocations` holds the facts; `crl_snapshots` records what was published; `outbox` carries events; `audit` records who did it.
- **The CRL publisher branch:**
  - `(per issuer, ordered, debounced)` — three properties in three words.
    - **Per issuer** because each issuer has its own list and its own signing key.
    - **Ordered** because a CRL claims to contain everything through a generation, so builds must not overtake each other.
    - **Debounced** because a mass revocation would otherwise trigger thousands of rebuilds of the same list.
  - `HSM/KMS sign(CRL, crl_key_ref)` — the list is signed with the issuer's CRL key, by handle.
  - `Object Storage crl/<issuer>/<crl_number>.crl (immutable)` — each CRL is written to its own immutable key, named by CRL number. Nothing is ever overwritten.
  - `verify download -> advance pointer -> alias "latest" -> CDN purge` — a four-step publication sequence, and the order is the whole safety argument. Taken apart below.
- **The OCSP precompute branch:**
  - `(per issuer, every 1 h)` — the refresh interval that the capacity section identified as the HSM sizing knob.
  - `HSM/KMS sign(response, responder_key_ref)` — note the **different key**: `responder_key_ref`, not `crl_key_ref`.
  - `Response snapshot (object storage + in-memory on responders)` — persisted for durability, held in memory for speed.

**The data plane**

- `OCSP responder fleet (stateless, AD-spread)`
  - **Stateless** because everything it needs is in the snapshot it loaded. Worked example: any responder can serve any request, so scaling is adding instances and failure is removing one.
  - **AD-spread** means instances are distributed across availability domains, so one datacenter's loss does not take the fleet.
- Two kinds of consumer, with an arrow each:
  - `TLS servers (fetch + staple)` — the bulk of traffic, absorbed by stapling.
  - `clients (live OCSP, optional)` — clients that check directly, marked optional because most will not.
- `CDN <- verifiers fetch the CDP URL (CRL)` — the other consumer path entirely. **CDP** is the CRL distribution point, the URL printed inside every certificate.

**The trust note, which is the most easily skipped line in the diagram**

> Trust: issuer CA cert (KU cRLSign) signs CRLs;
>        delegated responder cert (EKU OCSPSigning, <= 30 d) signs OCSP responses.

- Two different keys sign the two artifacts, and the reason is a genuine security argument.
- **`KU cRLSign`** — Key Usage, an X.509 extension declaring what a key may be used for. `cRLSign` means "this key may sign revocation lists". The issuer CA's own certificate carries it, so the CA signs its own CRLs.
- **`EKU OCSPSigning`** — Extended Key Usage, a narrower declaration. A **delegated responder** is a separate certificate, issued by the CA, whose only permitted use is signing OCSP responses.
- **Why delegate rather than let the CA sign OCSP responses directly:**
  - Worked example of the volume problem: OCSP precompute needs 280 signatures per second, continuously. Using the CA's own key for that would put the CA key on a high-volume automated path, which is exactly what the two-tier hierarchy elsewhere in this group exists to avoid.
  - Worked example of the blast-radius benefit: the responder certificate is short-lived — **30 days or less**. If the responder key is compromised, the attacker can sign false `good` responses for at most 30 days, and the CA itself is untouched. Compare with a compromised CA key, which is unrecoverable.
- The `<= 30 d` lifetime is doing real work: it is short enough that revoking the responder certificate is almost unnecessary, because expiry handles it. Worked example of why that matters: revoking a *responder* certificate is awkward, since the natural way to check its status is the responder it belongs to.

---

> - Revocation API owns authorization and the ledger write.
>   - Mechanism bought: a `202` work request with an operation id, so the caller never waits on signing.
>   - Cost paid: "revoked" and "published" are two states the API must expose.

**What this says in plain words**
The API records the decision and hands back a ticket. It does not wait for the world to find out.

**The mechanism**
Worked example of why waiting would be wrong: publishing involves a debounce of up to 60 seconds, an HSM signature, an object-storage write, a verification download, and a CDN purge. Holding an HTTP connection across all of that makes a revocation call take minutes and fail on any transient error in the chain.

**The cost, which is a genuine API design consequence**

- **"'Revoked' and 'published' are two states the API must expose."**
- Worked example of why conflating them is dangerous: an operator revokes a compromised certificate and the API returns success. If "success" meant only "recorded", the operator may believe the certificate is now rejected everywhere, when in fact no verifier has been told yet.
- So the API must be able to answer both questions separately: has this been recorded, and has it been published through a generation that includes it. The operation's `publicationGeneration` field in the traced flow below is exactly that second answer.
- The general shape: **when an action's effect is asynchronous, the API owes the caller two distinct states, and naming them is part of the design rather than an implementation detail.**

---

> - Ledger database owns the ordered truth per issuer.
>   - Mechanism bought: a per-issuer generation counter makes "everything through generation `g`" a well-defined snapshot.
>   - Cost paid: one row per issuer serializes revocations
>     - Fine at `3 / s`, and the reason to reject a global counter.

**What this says in plain words**
One counter per issuer turns a stream of revocations into numbered snapshots.

**Why the snapshot property matters so much**

- Worked example of what "through generation 47" buys: a published CRL carries that claim. A verifier, an auditor, or a reconciler can ask "was revocation number 45 included?" and the answer is yes, definitively, with no need to compare contents.
- Worked example of the alternative: without an ordered counter, "is this CRL up to date?" can only be answered by diffing its contents against the ledger, which is expensive and ambiguous while a build is in flight.

**The cost, and the comparison it invites**

- One row per issuer serializes that issuer's revocations. **"Fine at 3 / s, and the reason to reject a global counter."**
- Worked example of the global counter being wrong: 10,000 issuers sharing one counter row means every revocation in the system queues behind one lock. During a mass revocation on issuer `A` — 3 per second for an hour — every other issuer's revocations are delayed behind it. Per-issuer counters make one issuer's incident invisible to the rest.
- Naming the rejected alternative and the number that justifies the choice is the pattern worth copying.

---

> - CRL publisher owns snapshot building and the pointer.
>   - Mechanism bought: immutable objects per CRL number plus a pointer that advances only after verification.
>   - Cost paid: an extra download and verify per publish
>     - Orphan objects after a crash that a reconciler must tolerate.

**What this says in plain words**
Every CRL is written to a permanent, unique location, and the pointer that tells verifiers which one to use moves only after the object has been checked.

**The four-step sequence from the diagram, and why the order is the safety argument**

1. **Write the object to its immutable key.** `crl/<issuer>/<crl_number>.crl`. Nothing existing is touched.
2. **Download it back and verify the signature against the issuer certificate.**
   - Worked example of what this catches: a truncated upload. The object exists, is the right size to look plausible, and fails signature verification. Without this step it would be published and every verifier would reject the CRL — which, for a hard-fail verifier, means rejecting every certificate from that issuer.
   - Verifying by re-downloading rather than trusting the local copy also checks that storage actually holds what you think it does.
3. **Advance the pointer.** Only now does anything point at the new object.
4. **Rewrite the `latest` alias and purge the CDN.**

- The property this gives: **at every instant, the pointer names an object that has been verified to exist and to be correctly signed.** There is no window in which a verifier can fetch a half-written or corrupt CRL.

**The cost, stated honestly**

- **An extra download and verify per publish.** Worked example: at 3 publishes per second across 10,000 issuers, that is 3 extra downloads per second of roughly 0.5 megabytes — about 1.5 megabytes per second of internal traffic. Cheap, and the alternative is publishing artifacts nobody checked.
- **"Orphan objects after a crash that a reconciler must tolerate."**
  - Worked example: the publisher writes object number 48 and crashes before advancing the pointer. Object 48 exists in storage and nothing references it. On restart, the publisher builds again and produces object 49. Object 48 is an orphan — harmless, because nothing points at it, and it costs 0.5 megabytes.
  - "A reconciler must tolerate" is the instruction: the cleanup process must expect to find unreferenced objects and must not treat them as corruption. Worked example of getting this wrong: a reconciler that alarms on any unreferenced object would page on every publisher crash, which is noise rather than signal.

---

> - OCSP precompute and responders own per-certificate status.
>   - Mechanism bought: responses are signed ahead of time, so the hot path is a memory lookup with no database or HSM dependency.
>   - Cost paid: HSM volume scales with certificate count and refresh interval
>     - A nonce-requiring client cannot be served from cache.

**What this says in plain words**
Pre-sign everything so serving is a lookup. The second cost is a specific protocol wrinkle worth understanding.

**The mechanism**
Worked example of the availability payoff: both the ledger database and the HSM are unavailable for 30 minutes. OCSP responders keep answering every request from their in-memory snapshots, with valid signatures, throughout. What stops is *freshness* — the snapshot ages — not the ability to answer.

**Cost one: HSM volume scales with certificate count and refresh interval**
The 280-per-second figure, and the dial. Stated here as a component property so it is clear which component the hardware is bought for.

**Cost two: the nonce problem**

- An OCSP **nonce** is a random value a client may include in its request, requiring the responder to echo it inside the *signed* response. Its purpose is replay protection: it proves the response was generated for this specific request, just now.
- **A precomputed response cannot contain a nonce**, because the nonce did not exist when the response was signed.
- Worked example of the conflict: a client sends a nonce-bearing request. The snapshot holds a response for that `CertID`, but it has no nonce. The responder has two options — return the cached response without the nonce, which a strict client may reject, or sign a fresh response on demand, which puts that request on the HSM path.
- Worked example of why this is not a crisis in practice: most clients do not send nonces, and public responders commonly do not support them precisely because precomputation is how the load is served. But the design has to have an answer, and the answer is the sign-on-miss overflow path.
- The general shape: **precomputation and per-request freshness proofs are fundamentally incompatible**, and any design that precomputes must decide what it does for clients that demand the latter.

---

> - Object storage and CDN own distribution.
>   - Mechanism bought: static stability
>     - A signed CRL is valid without any service being up.
>   - Cost paid: `max-age` and purge latency add to the propagation bound.

**What this says in plain words**
Because the artifact is signed, it can be served by infrastructure nobody trusts, and it keeps working when everything else is down.

**The mechanism**

- **"A signed CRL is valid without any service being up."** The strongest form of static stability in this group.
- Worked example: the entire revocation control plane — API, ledger, publisher, HSM — is down for four hours. Verifiers fetch the CRL from the CDN and validate its signature against the issuer certificate they already hold. Revocation checking continues, using an artifact that is up to four hours older than it would otherwise be. Nothing in the serving path noticed.
- A compromised CDN edge cannot help itself either: it can serve stale bytes, and it cannot forge a CRL it has no key for.

**The cost**
`max-age` and purge latency are two of the three terms in the propagation bound. The capacity section already showed they are the small terms, which is the useful context: this cost is real and it is not where the bound lives.

---

> - HSM/KMS owns key custody.
>   - Mechanism bought: CRL and responder keys never exist in service memory
>     - Each signature is audited.
>   - Cost paid: throttling or outage stops freshness, not validity
>     - The design must alarm on remaining validity.

**What this says in plain words**
Both signing keys stay in hardware. When the hardware is unavailable, artifacts stop being *refreshed* but do not stop being *valid* — and that distinction determines what you alarm on.

**The mechanism**
Worked example of the property: an attacker with full control of the publisher host can request CRL signatures while they hold that access, and every request is logged against the key handle. They cannot take the key. That bounds the abuse in time and makes it visible.

**The cost, and the alarm it dictates**

- **"Throttling or outage stops freshness, not validity."**
- Worked example: the HSM is unavailable from 10:00. The CRL published at 09:30 with a 24-hour `nextUpdate` remains perfectly valid until 09:30 tomorrow. Nothing fails. Revocations recorded after 10:00 are simply not reaching anyone.
- **"The design must alarm on remaining validity."** The consequence, and it is the same lesson as the dead scheduler in the sibling questions: alarm on the outcome, not on the component.
  - Worked example of the wrong alarm: "HSM error rate". It fires, someone acknowledges it, and the real question — how long until published artifacts expire — is not on any dashboard.
  - Worked example of the right alarm: "minimum `nextUpdate − now` across all published CRLs". It counts down during the outage and tells you exactly when this becomes an emergency.

---

> - Relying parties own the fail policy.
>   - Mechanism bought: a published SLO makes hard-fail viable
>     - We cannot impose it.
>   - Cost paid: uncontrolled clients keep soft-fail, so their bound is the certificate TTL.

**What this says in plain words**
The decision that determines whether revocation works at all belongs to the verifier, not to this service. All the service can do is make the better choice defensible.

**The mechanism, and the admission attached to it**

- **Hard-fail** means rejecting a certificate when revocation status cannot be obtained; **soft-fail** means accepting it.
- **"A published SLO makes hard-fail viable."** Worked example: a verifier team will not adopt hard-fail without knowing how often status will be unavailable, because hard-fail turns a revocation-infrastructure outage into a total outage. An availability commitment — say 99.99% on the CDP and the responder — is what lets them make that choice with a known risk.
- **"We cannot impose it."** The honest limit. The service publishes artifacts; it cannot make anyone check them, or fail closed when the check does not complete.

**The cost**

- **"Uncontrolled clients keep soft-fail, so their bound is the certificate TTL."**
- Worked example: a partner integration or a customer device soft-fails. For those verifiers, revocation provides no guarantee whatsoever — an attacker who can block their access to the CDP restores a revoked certificate to full working order. The only control that still applies is expiry.
- Which is why short certificate lifetimes appear in every question in this group as the honest backstop: expiry cannot be blocked, cached around, or soft-failed.

---

> → Operator calls `POST /v1/issuers/{issuer}/revocations` with an `Idempotency-Key`.
> → API resolves the tenant from auth context, authorizes the issuer in its compartment, and validates the serial encoding.
> → Transaction: claim the intent, allocate `generation = next_generation`, insert the revocation row, store the `ACCEPTED` operation, write outbox and audit rows.
> → Return `202 {revocationId, operationId}` with `Location: /v1/operations/{operationId}`.
> → Publisher consumes the event, debounces `60 s` per issuer, and builds the CRL through generation `g`.
> → HSM signs the DER CRL with the issuer's CRL key; the publisher records the SHA-256.
> → Object is written to its immutable key, re-downloaded, and signature-verified against the issuer certificate.
> → Transaction: insert `crl_snapshots(crl_number, through_generation)`, set `published_generation = g`, advance the pointer.
> → Alias `latest` is rewritten and the CDN path purged; OCSP precompute re-signs the affected `CertID` as `revoked` first.
> → Operation moves to `SUCCEEDED` with `publicationGeneration = g`; verifiers observe `revoked` on their next fetch.

**What this is**
One revocation traced from the operator's call to verifiers seeing it. Ten steps. This is the narration for "walk me through a revocation".

**Step 1 — the call**

> Operator calls `POST /v1/issuers/{issuer}/revocations` with an `Idempotency-Key`.

- Worked example of why the key is required rather than optional: the response is lost to a network blip and the operator's script retries. Without the key, a second revocation row is created with a second generation number, and the CRL now lists the same serial twice. Harmless to verifiers, confusing to auditors, and it means the operation id the caller polls does not correspond to the row that exists.

**Step 2 — authorization and validation**

> API resolves the tenant from auth context, authorizes the issuer in its compartment, and validates the serial encoding.

- **"Resolves the tenant from auth context"** rather than from the request body. Worked example of why that matters: a tenant id supplied by the caller is a claim; a tenant resolved from the authenticated credential is a fact. Taking it from the body would let a caller revoke in another tenant by editing a field.
- **"Validates the serial encoding"** — serials have rules: positive, at most 20 octets. Worked example of what this catches: a serial pasted with a leading `0x`, or as a decimal string where hexadecimal was meant. Revoking the wrong serial number is revoking the wrong certificate, and there is no way to tell afterwards which was intended.

**Step 3 — the transaction, and everything inside it**

> Transaction: claim the intent, allocate `generation = next_generation`, insert the revocation row, store the `ACCEPTED` operation, write outbox and audit rows.

- Five actions, atomically.
  1. **Claim the intent** — the idempotency record, so a retry finds this attempt rather than starting a new one.
  2. **Allocate the generation** — under the issuer row's lock, which is what makes generations strictly ordered.
  3. **Insert the revocation row** — the fact itself.
  4. **Store the `ACCEPTED` operation** — the ticket the caller will poll.
  5. **Outbox and audit rows** — the event to publish and the record of who did it.
- Worked example of why the outbox is inside the transaction: if the event were published separately, a crash between the commit and the publish would leave a revocation that is recorded and never gets built into a CRL — recorded, invisible, and silent. The outbox makes the intent-to-publish commit atomically with the fact.

**Step 4 — the handoff**

> Return `202 {revocationId, operationId}` with `Location: /v1/operations/{operationId}`.

- Two identifiers because they name two different things: the revocation (the fact) and the operation (the work of publishing it). The `Location` header points at where to ask about the second.

**Step 5 — the publisher's debounce**

> Publisher consumes the event, debounces `60 s` per issuer, and builds the CRL through generation `g`.

- **Debounce** means waiting briefly and coalescing everything that arrives in the window into one build.
- Worked example of why 60 seconds is worth it: a mass revocation of 10,000 certificates in an hour is about 3 per second. Without debouncing that is 10,000 CRL builds and 10,000 HSM signatures. With a 60-second debounce it is about 60 builds, each containing roughly 170 new entries.
- Worked example of the cost: a single urgent revocation waits up to 60 seconds before appearing. Against a propagation bound measured in hours, that is a rounding error — which is exactly the argument the capacity section made about optimizing the publisher.

**Step 6 — signing**

> HSM signs the DER CRL with the issuer's CRL key; the publisher records the SHA-256.

- The **issuer's CRL key**, matching the trust note. The SHA-256 is recorded so the verification step has something to compare against, and so the snapshot row can identify exactly which bytes were published.

**Step 7 — write, re-download, verify**

> Object is written to its immutable key, re-downloaded, and signature-verified against the issuer certificate.

- The three steps that must happen before anything points at the object. Note that verification is against the **issuer certificate**, which is the same check a verifier will perform — so the publisher is testing the artifact the way its consumers will.

**Step 8 — the commit that makes it real**

> Transaction: insert `crl_snapshots(crl_number, through_generation)`, set `published_generation = g`, advance the pointer.

- Three changes atomically: the record of what was published, the issuer's published-generation marker, and the pointer itself.
- **`through_generation`** is the claim that makes snapshots meaningful: CRL number 48 contains everything through generation 47.
- Worked example of why these must be atomic: advancing the pointer without recording the snapshot would leave the system unable to say what the current CRL contains. Recording the snapshot without advancing the pointer would claim a publication that verifiers cannot see.

**Step 9 — distribution, and one important ordering**

> Alias `latest` is rewritten and the CDN path purged; OCSP precompute re-signs the affected `CertID` as `revoked` first.

- **"first"** is the word to catch. The OCSP response for the affected certificate is re-signed as `revoked` *before* the CRL distribution completes.
- Worked example of why that ordering is chosen: OCSP is the fresher channel — a per-certificate response can be regenerated for one `CertID` in milliseconds, while the CRL path involves a debounce, a CDN purge, and verifier caches with long `nextUpdate` values. Updating the fast channel first minimizes the window in which a verifier gets `good` for a certificate that has been revoked.
- Worked example of the reverse being worse: if the CRL updated first, a client using OCSP — the mechanism chosen specifically for freshness — would be the *last* to learn, which inverts the point of having it.

**Step 10 — the answer to "is it done?"**

> Operation moves to `SUCCEEDED` with `publicationGeneration = g`; verifiers observe `revoked` on their next fetch.

- **`publicationGeneration`** is the field that resolves the API's two-states problem from the first component bullet. The operator can see not just that the revocation was recorded, but that a published artifact now includes it.
- **"On their next fetch"** is the honest final clause. The service's work is done; the verifiers' caches are not, and the propagation bound from the capacity section governs how long that takes.

## API and data model — IC3 baseline

Seven endpoints, six state tables, reference DDL, what the DDL deliberately omits, and six service conventions.

---

> | Surface | Contract | Conflict/error behavior |
> |---|---|---|
> | `POST /v1/issuers/{issuer}/revocations` | `{serial, reason, invalidityDate?}`; `I`; `202 {revocationId, operationId}` | Same serial replays the original `202`; different reason on a revoked serial returns `409`; unknown issuer `404` |
> | `GET /v1/issuers/{issuer}/revocations/{serial}` | `{revokedAt, reason, generation, publicationGeneration, crlNumber?}` | Administrative view, not a status protocol; `404` follows tenant visibility |
> | `GET /v1/issuers/{issuer}/crl` | `If-None-Match`; `application/pkix-crl`, `ETag`, `Cache-Control: max-age=300` | `304` when unchanged; never serves a snapshot the pointer has not reached |
> | `POST /v1/issuers/{issuer}/crl-publications` | `{reason}`; `I`; forces a publish through the current generation; `202 operation` | `409` if the issuer is disabled; coalesces with an in-flight publish of the same generation |
> | `POST /v1/ocsp` | DER `application/ocsp-request` in, `application/ocsp-response` out | Status lives inside the signed response; `malformedRequest` / `unauthorized` are OCSP statuses, not JSON |
> | `GET /v1/ocsp/{base64url(request)}` | RFC 5019-style cacheable `GET`; same response as `POST` | Requests over `1 KB` rejected; unknown issuer hash returns `unauthorized` |
> | `GET /v1/operations/{id}` | `{state, publicationGeneration?}` | Shared work-request contract `ACCEPTED → RUNNING → SUCCEEDED / FAILED` |

**What this says in plain words**
Seven surfaces of three different kinds: a management API in JSON, a CRL fetch, and two OCSP endpoints that speak a binary protocol rather than JSON at all.

**Row by row**

- **`POST .../revocations` — revoke a certificate.**
  - `{serial, reason, invalidityDate?}`. The optional third field is explained at the end of the section and is worth waiting for.
  - **"Same serial replays the original `202`"** — revoking twice is not an error. Worked example of why that is right: during an incident three people all revoke the same compromised certificate. All three should get a success, not two errors that make them wonder whether it worked.
  - **"Different reason on a revoked serial returns `409`"** — but changing the *reason* is a conflict.
    - Worked example of why the distinction matters: a certificate revoked as `superseded` is later discovered to have been key-compromised. Those are materially different facts — `keyCompromise` tells verifiers something `superseded` does not, and it may trigger different handling. Silently accepting the change would rewrite history; silently ignoring it would lose the more serious fact. `409` forces the operator to deal with it explicitly.
  - **Unknown issuer `404`** rather than `403`, consistent with the rest of the group's existence-leak rule.
- **`GET .../revocations/{serial}` — the administrative view.**
  - Returns five fields, and the interesting pair is `generation` and `publicationGeneration`: what number this revocation was assigned, and what number has actually been published.
  - Worked example of reading them: `generation: 48, publicationGeneration: 47` means this revocation is recorded and not yet in any published artifact. That is exactly the "revoked versus published" distinction the architecture section said the API owes its callers.
  - **"Administrative view, not a status protocol."** The warning matters. Worked example of the misuse: a developer discovers this endpoint and uses it to check certificate status from an application, because JSON is easier than DER. It is not signed, it requires tenant authentication, and it is not what verifiers should ever consult. Status checking is OCSP or the CRL.
- **`GET .../crl` — fetch the list.**
  - `application/pkix-crl` is the standard media type. `Cache-Control: max-age=300` is the 5-minute CDN term in the propagation bound.
  - **"Never serves a snapshot the pointer has not reached."** The API-level statement of the verify-then-advance rule: a CRL that was written but not yet verified and pointed at is not reachable here.
- **`POST .../crl-publications` — force a publish now.**
  - The escape hatch for urgency: rather than waiting for the debounce and the hourly schedule, publish through the current generation immediately.
  - **"Coalesces with an in-flight publish of the same generation."** Worked example of why: during an incident five operators each hit the force-publish button. Without coalescing that is five builds and five HSM signatures producing five identical CRLs with five CRL numbers. With it, they all attach to the one build already running.
  - `409` if the issuer is disabled, because a disabled issuer should not be producing new signed artifacts.
- **`POST /v1/ocsp` — the standard OCSP endpoint.**
  - DER in, DER out. This is not a JSON API and does not pretend to be.
  - **"Status lives inside the signed response; `malformedRequest` / `unauthorized` are OCSP statuses, not JSON."**
    - The most important row in the table for anyone who has not implemented OCSP.
    - Worked example of the mistake this forbids: returning HTTP `400` with a JSON error body for a malformed request. An OCSP client does not parse JSON and does not interpret HTTP status codes as protocol answers — it expects a DER response whose *contents* say `malformedRequest`. Returning JSON produces a client-side parse failure, which the client may then treat as an unreachable responder and soft-fail.
    - The general shape: **when implementing a standard binary protocol over HTTP, the protocol's error model wins over HTTP's.**
- **`GET /v1/ocsp/{base64url(request)}` — the cacheable variant.**
  - RFC 5019 defines a lightweight OCSP profile in which the request is base64-encoded into the URL path, so the response can be cached by ordinary HTTP infrastructure.
  - Worked example of why this matters at scale: a `POST` is not cacheable by a CDN, so every request reaches the responder fleet. A `GET` with the request in the path is a plain cacheable URL — identical requests for the same certificate hit the CDN edge and never touch the responders.
  - **"Requests over 1 KB rejected"** — a defence against oversized URLs being used to consume resources, and a practical limit since a legitimate single-certificate request is a few hundred bytes.
  - **"Unknown issuer hash returns `unauthorized`"** — again an OCSP status rather than an HTTP error, and the right one: the responder is saying it is not authoritative for that issuer.
- **`GET /v1/operations/{id}`** — the shared work-request contract, with `publicationGeneration` as the field that answers "has my revocation actually reached an artifact?"

> | State | Key | Fields/invariant |
> |---|---|---|
> | Issuer status | `(tenant_id, issuer_id)` | `next_generation`, `published_generation`, `crl_key_ref`, `responder_key_ref`, publish interval, validity |
> | Revocation | `(tenant_id, issuer_id, serial)`; `UNIQUE (tenant_id, issuer_id, generation)` | `generation`, `revoked_at`, `reason`, `invalidity_date`, `not_after` copied at revoke time for retention |
> | CRL snapshot | `(tenant_id, issuer_id, crl_number)` | `through_generation`, `this_update`, `next_update`, `object_ref`, `sha256`; `CHECK (next_update > this_update)` |
> | OCSP response | `(issuer_id, serial)` resolved from `CertID` | `status`, `through_generation`, `this_update`, `next_update`, DER reference |
> | Responder key | `(tenant_id, issuer_id, responder_version)` | HSM `key_ref`, responder certificate reference, `not_after`; two versions overlap during rotation |
> | Issuer hash map | `(hash_alg, issuer_name_hash, issuer_key_hash)` | Resolves an OCSP `CertID` to `(tenant_id, issuer_id)` without any caller-supplied tenant |

**What this says in plain words**
Six kinds of state. Five are keyed by tenant; the sixth deliberately is not, and that exception is the most interesting row in the table.

- **Issuer status.** Holds both counters — `next_generation` (what to allocate) and `published_generation` (what verifiers can see) — plus both key handles and the publish settings.
- **Revocation.** Two keys: the primary is `(tenant, issuer, serial)`, so one row per certificate, and a *unique* constraint on `(tenant, issuer, generation)` so generations are never reused.
  - **`not_after` copied at revoke time for retention** is the design note. Taken apart in the DDL below.
- **CRL snapshot.** What was published, when, where the bytes are, and their hash.
- **OCSP response.** Keyed by `(issuer_id, serial)` — **resolved from `CertID`**, not supplied directly.
- **Responder key.** Versioned, with **"two versions overlap during rotation"** as the invariant.
  - Worked example of why overlap is needed: a responder certificate is replaced on day 30. Responses signed by the old key are still in verifier caches and still valid until their `nextUpdate`. If the old key's certificate vanished immediately, clients validating a cached response would fail to find its signer. Keeping both live through the overlap means every response in circulation has a verifiable signer.
- **Issuer hash map — the row with no tenant in its key.**
  - **"Resolves an OCSP `CertID` to `(tenant_id, issuer_id)` without any caller-supplied tenant."**
  - This solves a genuine problem. An OCSP request arrives as raw DER from an anonymous client on the internet. It contains a `CertID` — hashes of the issuer's name and public key, plus a serial — and **nothing else**. There is no tenant field, no authentication, no header naming who this is for.
  - Worked example of the routing problem: the responder must decide which of 10,000 issuers this request is about, using only two hashes. That is what this table is: a lookup from those two hashes to an issuer.
  - Worked example of why a tenant prefix would break it: if the key were `(tenant_id, hash_alg, name_hash, key_hash)`, the responder would need to know the tenant before it could look anything up — and the whole point is that the request does not say. The absence of the tenant in the key is what makes the lookup possible at all.
  - And the security note: because the request is anonymous, the response must reveal nothing beyond the certificate's status. Status for a serial is public information by design — it is what OCSP exists to publish — so this is safe, but only because the map returns an issuer rather than exposing anything queryable about a tenant.

---

> - Reference DDL
>   - Keys and uniqueness match the state table above and the [companion schema](../../1sde/1.backend/2.rest102.md#pki-3).
>   - OCSP responses are deliberately not relational
>     - Responders serve a precomputed in-memory snapshot keyed by `(issuer_id, serial)`.
>     - DER bytes sit on object storage and the CDN.

**What this says in plain words**
Five tables are in SQL. The OCSP responses are not, on purpose.

**Why OCSP responses are not relational**
Worked example of the scale: one million certificates, each with a signed response of about 1.5 kilobytes, re-signed hourly. That is 1.5 gigabytes of blob data rewritten every hour. Serving it from a relational store would put a database query on a path the capacity section already decided must be a memory lookup.

- The snapshot is loaded into responder memory and the DER bytes live on object storage. The database never appears on the OCSP read path, which is precisely the availability property claimed earlier.

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE issuer_status (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, issuer_id text NOT NULL,
>   next_generation bigint NOT NULL DEFAULT 1,       -- next revocation generation to allocate
>   published_generation bigint NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,  -- public pointer; advanced only after a signed snapshot exists
>   crl_key_ref text NOT NULL,                       -- HSM handle for CRL signing, never key material
>   responder_key_ref text NOT NULL,                 -- current responder key handle
>   publish_interval_s integer NOT NULL DEFAULT 3600,
>   crl_validity_s integer NOT NULL DEFAULT 86400,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, issuer_id),
>   CHECK (published_generation < next_generation),
>   CHECK (publish_interval_s > 0 AND crl_validity_s > publish_interval_s)
> );
> ```

**The four things worth stopping on**

1. **`next_generation` and `published_generation` are separate columns.**
   - The two-state model in the schema. `next_generation` is what the next revocation will take; `published_generation` is what the world can see.
   - Worked example: `next_generation = 49, published_generation = 47` means generation 48 has been allocated to a revocation that is recorded and not yet published.
2. **`published_generation ... -- advanced only after a signed snapshot exists`.**
   - The comment states the verify-then-advance rule in the one place a future maintainer will read it while changing this column.
3. **`CHECK (published_generation < next_generation)`.**
   - A constraint enforcing that you cannot claim to have published a generation that has not been allocated.
   - Worked example of the bug this makes impossible: a publisher with an off-by-one sets `published_generation = next_generation`, claiming to have published a generation number that no revocation has yet been given. The database rejects it rather than leaving the counters in a state nothing else can interpret.
4. **`CHECK (publish_interval_s > 0 AND crl_validity_s > publish_interval_s)`.**
   - The second half is the interesting one: the CRL's validity must be **longer** than the interval between publishes.
   - Worked example of the disaster it prevents: publishing hourly with a 30-minute validity. Every CRL expires 30 minutes before its replacement is built, so for half of every hour there is no valid CRL at all. Hard-fail verifiers reject every certificate from that issuer, on a recurring schedule, forever.
   - The defaults are 3,600 seconds between publishes and 86,400 seconds of validity — a 24-fold margin, so 23 consecutive publish failures are survivable before anything expires.
   - This is the kind of misconfiguration that is easy to introduce during tuning and catastrophic in production, which is why it is a constraint rather than documentation.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE revocations (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, issuer_id text NOT NULL, serial numeric(49,0) NOT NULL,
>   generation bigint NOT NULL, revoked_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   reason text NOT NULL, invalidity_date timestamptz,
>   not_after timestamptz NOT NULL,                  -- copied at revoke time; retention needs no join into issuance
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, issuer_id, serial),
>   UNIQUE (tenant_id, issuer_id, generation),
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, issuer_id) REFERENCES issuer_status,
>   CHECK (serial > 0),
>   CHECK (invalidity_date IS NULL OR invalidity_date <= revoked_at)
> );
> CREATE INDEX revocations_retention ON revocations (not_after);
> ```

**The five things worth stopping on**

1. **`serial numeric(49,0)` with `CHECK (serial > 0)`.**
   - 49 decimal digits covers a 20-byte X.509 serial. The check enforces positivity, which the type does not.
   - The remaining rule — at most 20 octets when DER-encoded — is enforced in code, as the conventions note says.
2. **`PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, issuer_id, serial)`.**
   - One row per certificate. Worked example of what this enforces for free: revoking the same serial twice cannot create two rows, which is the database-level backing for the idempotent `202` replay.
3. **`UNIQUE (tenant_id, issuer_id, generation)`.**
   - Generations are never reused within an issuer. Worked example of why that must be a constraint: if two revocations shared generation 48, then "the CRL through generation 48" would be ambiguous — it might contain one of them or both, and no published claim could be trusted.
4. **`not_after timestamptz NOT NULL, -- copied at revoke time; retention needs no join into issuance`.**
   - Deliberate denormalization, and the comment gives the reason.
   - Worked example of the problem it solves: a CRL must drop entries once the certificate has expired, so the builder needs each serial's `not_after`. Fetching that from the issuance service would mean a cross-service join for every entry, on every build — 10,000 lookups to build a 10,000-entry list, every hour, for every issuer.
   - Copying it at revocation time means the CRL builder and the retention sweep read one table and nothing else.
   - Worked example of the deeper reason, which the conventions make explicit: there is deliberately **no foreign key** into issuance, so a certificate that was signed but never recorded — the orphan case from the sibling designs — can still be revoked by serial. Copying `not_after` is what makes that possible without a reference that would have to exist.
5. **`CHECK (invalidity_date IS NULL OR invalidity_date <= revoked_at)`.**
   - The **invalidity date** is when the certificate is believed to have *become* untrustworthy, which can precede when someone got around to revoking it.
   - Worked example: a key is compromised on 3 March and discovered on 10 March. `invalidityDate` is 3 March; `revokedAt` is 10 March. A verifier evaluating a signature made on 5 March can now conclude it was made with a compromised key, which the revocation date alone would not have told it.
   - The check enforces the only sensible relationship: you cannot believe a certificate became invalid *after* you revoked it.
6. **`revocations_retention ON (not_after)`** — the index the expiry sweep walks to find entries that can be dropped.

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE crl_snapshots (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, issuer_id text NOT NULL, crl_number bigint NOT NULL,
>   through_generation bigint NOT NULL, this_update timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   next_update timestamptz NOT NULL, object_ref text NOT NULL, sha256 text NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, issuer_id, crl_number),
>   UNIQUE (tenant_id, issuer_id, through_generation),
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, issuer_id) REFERENCES issuer_status,
>   CHECK (next_update > this_update)
> );
> ```

**The three things worth stopping on**

1. **`UNIQUE (tenant_id, issuer_id, through_generation)`.**
   - At most one published CRL per generation. Worked example of what this prevents: two publishers racing both build a CRL through generation 47 and both insert a snapshot. Two CRL numbers now claim the same contents, and a verifier comparing CRL numbers to decide which is newer gets a meaningless answer. The constraint makes the second insert fail, so one publisher wins and the other retries against the advanced state.
2. **`object_ref` and `sha256` together.**
   - Where the bytes are, and what they hash to. Worked example of the pair's use: a reconciler verifying that published artifacts are intact downloads `object_ref` and compares against `sha256` — no signature verification needed to detect storage corruption.
3. **`CHECK (next_update > this_update)`.** A CRL that expires before it was issued is nonsense, and the database refuses it.

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE responder_keys (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, issuer_id text NOT NULL, responder_version bigint NOT NULL,
>   key_ref text NOT NULL,                           -- HSM handle, never key material
>   responder_cert_ref text NOT NULL,                -- delegated certificate with id-kp-OCSPSigning
>   not_before timestamptz NOT NULL, not_after timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, issuer_id, responder_version),
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, issuer_id) REFERENCES issuer_status,
>   CHECK (not_after > not_before)
> );
> CREATE INDEX responder_keys_expiry ON responder_keys (not_after);
> ```

**What to notice**

- **Versioned by `responder_version`**, which is what allows two versions to coexist during rotation.
- **`responder_cert_ref text -- delegated certificate with id-kp-OCSPSigning`.** `id-kp-OCSPSigning` is the object identifier for the OCSP-signing Extended Key Usage — the formal name of the trust note from the architecture diagram.
- **`responder_keys_expiry ON (not_after)`** — the index behind the rotation schedule. Worked example: with a 30-day responder certificate, a sweep over this index finds keys approaching expiry and triggers rotation, the same shape as the certificate-expiry sweep in the sibling designs. A responder certificate that expires unnoticed means every OCSP response becomes unverifiable at once.

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE issuer_hash_map (
>   hash_alg text NOT NULL, issuer_name_hash bytea NOT NULL, issuer_key_hash bytea NOT NULL,
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, issuer_id text NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (hash_alg, issuer_name_hash, issuer_key_hash),  -- no tenant prefix: resolved from CertID alone
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, issuer_id) REFERENCES issuer_status,
>   CHECK (hash_alg IN ('SHA1', 'SHA256'))
> );
> ```

**The two things worth stopping on**

1. **The comment on the primary key: `-- no tenant prefix: resolved from CertID alone`.**
   - Every other table in this design starts its key with `tenant_id`. This one cannot, and the comment says why: the lookup input is an anonymous `CertID` that carries no tenant.
   - It is worth flagging explicitly *because* it breaks the pattern. Worked example of the review comment this pre-empts: "why does this table not follow the tenant-scoped key convention?" The answer is in the schema.
2. **`CHECK (hash_alg IN ('SHA1', 'SHA256'))`.**
   - SHA-1 appears, and it is correct that it does. OCSP's `CertID` uses SHA-1 by default under RFC 6960, and enormous numbers of deployed clients send SHA-1 hashes.
   - Worked example of why this is not a security problem: the hashes here identify an issuer for *routing*. A collision would misroute a request to the wrong issuer, which yields an `unknown` or `unauthorized` status rather than a forged `good` — the response's own signature is what carries the security, and that is not SHA-1.
   - Supporting both means a modern client sending SHA-256 and a legacy client sending SHA-1 both resolve to the same issuer, which means the map holds two rows per issuer.

---

> - What the DDL does not capture
>   - The OCSP snapshot and the issued-serial set replicated from the issuance ledger live in responder memory.
>   - CRL DER bytes live on object storage; `object_ref` and `sha256` only point at them.
>   - `crl_key_ref` and `key_ref` are HSM handles; no key material is stored.
>   - Deviation from the companion
>     - `revocations.not_after`, the publish settings on `issuer_status`, `responder_keys`, and `issuer_hash_map` are additions.
>     - They let retention sweeps, rotation overlap, and `CertID` routing run without a join into the issuance service.

**What this says in plain words**
Three things live outside the database, and one list of deliberate differences from the shared schema.

1. **"The OCSP snapshot **and the issued-serial set** replicated from the issuance ledger live in responder memory."**
   - The second half is easy to skim past and is important. The responder needs to know not only which serials are *revoked*, but which were ever *issued*.
   - Worked example of why: a request arrives for a serial that is not in the revocation list. Is the answer `good` or `unknown`? If the responder only knows about revocations, it must answer `good` for every serial ever invented, including ones no CA issued. With the issued-serial set replicated, it can answer `good` for issued-and-not-revoked and `unknown` for everything else.
   - Worked example of why that distinction matters: answering `good` for an arbitrary serial means the responder vouches for certificates it has never seen — which is exactly the property an attacker would want if they could produce a certificate outside the issuance path.
2. **CRL DER bytes on object storage.** The database holds a pointer and a hash, not megabytes of blob.
3. **HSM handles only.** The custody rule, stated once more.
4. **The deviation list**, with the reason attached: `revocations.not_after`, the publish settings, `responder_keys`, and `issuer_hash_map` are additions that **"let retention sweeps, rotation overlap, and `CertID` routing run without a join into the issuance service."**
   - The common theme is independence. Every addition removes a runtime dependency on another service.
   - Worked example of why that is worth schema duplication: the OCSP responder must answer during an issuance-service outage. Any field it needs that lives only in issuance would become an availability dependency on the hot path.

---

> - Proposed service conventions
>   - Intent rows use the shared `api_intents` table from the API conventions, retained past the retry horizon.
>   - `serial` is `numeric(49,0)`, positive, and at most 20 octets DER-encoded
>   - The SQL type alone does not enforce the X.509 rule.
>   - Revocation takes no foreign key into the issuance service
>   - An orphan certificate that was signed but never recorded must still be revocable by serial.
>   - CDP and AIA URLs are embedded at issuance in [s2.01](s2.01.Multi-tenant-certificate-authority.md), so they are a frozen contract for every issued certificate.
>   - Reason codes follow RFC 5280 `CRLReason`
>   - `keyCompromise` with an `invalidityDate` earlier than `revokedAt` is the reason that field exists.

**What this says in plain words**
Five conventions, and the third and fourth are the ones with real consequences.

1. **Shared `api_intents` table, retained past the retry horizon.** The idempotency records must outlive the longest period a client might retry, or a late retry creates a duplicate.
2. **Serial rules enforced in code, not by the type.** `numeric(49,0)` bounds the magnitude; the positivity check is a constraint; the 20-octet DER limit is application logic.
3. **"Revocation takes no foreign key into the issuance service. An orphan certificate that was signed but never recorded must still be revocable by serial."**
   - This is the strongest design statement in the section, and it connects directly to `s2.01`.
   - Worked example of the scenario: an issuance worker calls the HSM, receives a signature, and crashes before recording the version. A valid certificate exists in the world with a serial the issuance ledger has no row for.
   - If revocation required a foreign key into issuance, that certificate would be **unrevocable** — the one certificate in the entire system you most urgently need to revoke, and the schema would refuse.
   - So revocation is by serial, with no reference. Worked example of the cost: you can revoke a serial that was never issued, and nothing stops a typo. That is the accepted trade, and it is clearly the right one — a spurious CRL entry is harmless, while an unrevocable orphan is a permanent security hole.
   - The general principle: **the emergency path must not depend on the normal path having worked.**
4. **"CDP and AIA URLs are embedded at issuance, so they are a frozen contract for every issued certificate."**
   - **CDP** is where a verifier fetches the CRL; **AIA** is where it fetches the OCSP responder (and the issuer certificate). Both are printed *inside* each certificate at the moment it is signed.
   - Worked example of why "frozen contract" is the right phrase: a certificate issued today with a 90-day lifetime carries that CDP URL for 90 days, and no change to the revocation service can alter it. Changing the URL scheme means every certificate issued before the change still points at the old location, so the old location must keep working until the last of them expires.
   - Worked example of the failure: moving the CRL to a new domain and decommissioning the old one. Every unexpired certificate still directs verifiers to the old URL. Hard-fail verifiers reject them all; soft-fail verifiers stop checking revocation entirely, silently.
5. **Reason codes follow RFC 5280 `CRLReason`, and `invalidityDate` exists for `keyCompromise`.**
   - The standard reason codes — `keyCompromise`, `superseded`, `cessationOfOperation`, `affiliationChanged`, and others — are what verifiers understand.
   - Worked example of the `invalidityDate` case, restated because this is where the field's purpose is finally named: a key is compromised on 3 March and discovered on 10 March. Revoking on 10 March with an invalidity date of 3 March lets a verifier evaluating a signature from 5 March conclude it was made with a compromised key.
   - Worked example of why this matters beyond TLS: for signatures with long-term meaning — signed documents, code signing, audit records — the question "was this signature valid when it was made?" is the whole point, and `revokedAt` alone answers it wrongly.

## Revocation ledger and CRL publication — IC3 baseline

The ordered ledger, what a snapshot contains, signing, the fenced publish procedure, a stable distribution point, crash recovery, and freshness.

---

> - Ordered ledger
>   - Allocate an issuer generation and insert the revocation in one transaction.
>   - A committed generation identifies a consistent prefix, unlike a wall-clock cutoff.
>   - A CRL intent reserves its number and exact ledger prefix before signing.

**What this says in plain words**
Three rules, and the middle one explains why this design uses a counter rather than a timestamp.

1. **Allocate the generation and insert the revocation in one transaction.** The number and the fact commit together, so a generation is never allocated to a revocation that does not exist.
2. **"A committed generation identifies a consistent prefix, unlike a wall-clock cutoff."**
   - This is the sentence to understand, and it is a general lesson about using time as a boundary.
   - **A consistent prefix** means: "everything numbered 47 or below" is a set whose membership is fixed and complete the moment generation 47 commits. Nothing can later appear with number 46.
   - **A wall-clock cutoff cannot promise that.** Worked example: a publisher builds a CRL containing "everything revoked before 09:00:00". A transaction that began at 08:59:58 commits at 09:00:01 with a `revoked_at` of 08:59:58. It belongs in the CRL by the stated rule, and the CRL was already built without it. The published list is silently incomplete, and nothing detects it, because the claim "everything before 09:00" cannot be checked after the fact.
   - Worked example of the same case with generations: the late transaction takes generation 48, allocated under the issuer row's lock at commit time. A CRL through generation 47 does not claim to include it. The claim is exactly true.
   - The general principle worth carrying: **a monotonic counter allocated at commit gives a boundary you can name; a timestamp read before commit does not.** Any design that snapshots "everything up to time T" from a concurrent writer has this bug.
3. **"A CRL intent reserves its number and exact ledger prefix before signing."**
   - The same intent-before-signature discipline as the sibling designs. The publisher writes down exactly which CRL number it will produce and exactly which prefix it will cover, *before* the irreversible HSM call.
   - Worked example of why: a retry after a crash must produce the same artifact. If the covered prefix were recomputed at retry time, the retry would include revocations that arrived in between, producing a different CRL for the same reserved number.

---

> - Snapshot contents
>   - Persist issuer version, CRL number, covered generation, thisUpdate, nextUpdate, and canonical TBS bytes.
>   - Keep unexpired revoked serials.
>   - Keep an expired serial until it has appeared on at least one successfully published scheduled CRL after expiry.
>   - A fixed one-interval grace cannot prove that condition through a publisher outage.

**What this says in plain words**
What goes into a snapshot record, and a surprisingly careful rule about when an entry may finally be dropped.

**What is persisted**
Issuer version, CRL number, covered generation, the two timestamps, and **canonical TBS bytes** — the exact bytes that were signed, stored rather than regenerated. Same reasoning as everywhere in this group: storing the bytes is what makes the intent truly immutable, because regenerating them could produce something different.

**The removal rule, which is the interesting part**

- The obvious rule is "drop an entry once the certificate expires". The actual rule is stricter: **keep it until it has appeared on at least one successfully published scheduled CRL *after* expiry.**
- Worked example of why the obvious rule is wrong:
  1. A certificate expires at 12:00 on Tuesday.
  2. The publisher drops its entry immediately and publishes a CRL at 12:05 without it.
  3. A verifier is still holding Monday's CRL, which *does* list the serial. Fine so far.
  4. But now consider a verifier holding a CRL published at 11:55 Tuesday — it lists the serial. And one fetching at 12:05 — it does not. Both are valid. The certificate is expired, so both verifiers reject it anyway.
  - So far so harmless. The problem is the publisher outage case, which the fourth bullet names.
- **"A fixed one-interval grace cannot prove that condition through a publisher outage."**
  - Worked example: the rule is softened to "keep for one publish interval after expiry" — one hour. The certificate expires at 12:00, so the entry is due to be dropped at 13:00. The publisher is down from 11:30 to 16:00. At 16:00 it resumes, sees the entry is past its one-hour grace, and drops it. **No published CRL ever contained that serial after its expiry.**
  - Why that matters: a verifier with a badly skewed clock, or one applying a clock-tolerance allowance, may still consider the certificate within its validity window. Its most recent CRL — the one from 11:30 — may or may not list the serial depending on exactly when it fetched. The system has lost the ability to say "every verifier had at least one opportunity to see this revocation while the certificate still looked valid".
  - The correct rule makes removal conditional on an *observed event* — a successful publication — rather than on elapsed time. A publisher outage delays the removal instead of skipping it.
- The general shape, and it recurs throughout this group: **a cleanup gated on a timer silently skips work when the producer was down; a cleanup gated on evidence waits instead.**

---

> - Signing
>   - Baseline uses the issuing CA key authorized for cRLSign.
>   - Select the exact issuer key version and verify the completed DER artifact.
>   - A separate CRL signer needs the indirect-CRL profile and compatible verifier behavior.

**What this says in plain words**
The CA signs its own CRLs with its own key. Using a different key is possible and is a bigger change than it sounds.

1. **The issuing CA key, authorized for `cRLSign`.** The Key Usage extension from the architecture diagram's trust note.
2. **"Select the exact issuer key version and verify the completed DER artifact."**
   - **Exact key version** matters because CAs rotate. Worked example: the CA rotates from key version 4 to 5 while a CRL build is in flight. An unpinned call would sign with version 5, producing a CRL that verifiers holding only version 4's certificate cannot validate. Pinning the version at intent time means the build signs with what it planned to.
   - **Verify the completed artifact** before it goes anywhere — the same check the publisher performs after upload.
3. **"A separate CRL signer needs the indirect-CRL profile and compatible verifier behavior."**
   - An **indirect CRL** is one signed by a key other than the issuing CA's. It is legitimate under RFC 5280, and it requires the CRL to carry an issuing-distribution-point extension marking it as indirect, and each entry to carry a certificate-issuer extension naming which CA the serial belongs to.
   - Worked example of why this is not a free swap: a verifier that does not implement indirect CRLs will reject the artifact outright, or — worse — fail to associate entries with the right issuer. The mechanism exists and support is uneven, which is the same shape as the cross-signing caveat in the sibling question.
   - So the baseline keeps it simple: the CA signs its own list, which every verifier handles.

---

> ```text
> PUBLISH(issuer):
>     transaction:
>         claim current publisher fence
>         reserve or resume unique CRL intent(number, covered_generation,
>                     thisUpdate, nextUpdate, exact_TBS, body_hash)
>     sign exact_TBS using pinned issuer CRL key
>     assemble and verify CRL DER
>     put immutable object keyed by issuer, intent_id, and artifact_hash
>     fetch stored object; verify actual bytes, issuer, number, and ledger coverage
>     transaction:
>         require current publisher fence and live lease
>         require intent.number > published_head.number
>         require intent.covered_generation >= published_head.covered_generation
>         verify removal rules against previous published snapshot
>         commit snapshot, published_head, audit, and notification outbox
> ```

**What this is**
The publication procedure: two transactions with one HSM call and one storage round trip between them. The same shape as issuance in `s2.01`, for the same reason — a signature cannot be rolled back.

**Transaction 1 — reserve**

- `claim current publisher fence` — a fencing token, so a stalled publisher that resumes cannot act as though it is still the owner.
- `reserve or resume unique CRL intent(number, covered_generation, thisUpdate, nextUpdate, exact_TBS, body_hash)`
  - **"reserve or resume"** — if an intent already exists from a previous attempt, this attempt continues it rather than creating a new one. That is what makes a crashed publish retryable without producing a second CRL number.
  - Six fields pinned: the number, the covered prefix, both timestamps, the exact bytes to sign, and their hash. Everything that determines the artifact is fixed before anything irreversible happens.

**The middle — the irreversible part**

- `sign exact_TBS using pinned issuer CRL key` — signs the stored bytes, with the pinned key version.
- `assemble and verify CRL DER` — build the complete artifact and check its signature immediately.
- `put immutable object keyed by issuer, intent_id, and artifact_hash`
  - The key includes the **artifact hash**, which is a nice detail. Worked example of its effect: two attempts that produce different signature bytes (see crash recovery below) write to two *different* keys. Neither can overwrite the other, so both survive and the head commit decides which one is real.
- `fetch stored object; verify actual bytes, issuer, number, and ledger coverage`
  - Four checks on the re-downloaded object, not one. Worked example of what each catches: wrong bytes catches a truncated upload; wrong issuer catches a misrouted write; wrong number catches a key-construction bug; wrong ledger coverage catches a build that included more or fewer revocations than the intent reserved.

**Transaction 2 — commit, with four preconditions**

- `require current publisher fence and live lease` — am I still the owner? Worked example of failing this: this publisher stalled for two minutes, its lease expired, another publisher took over and published. This one's fence is stale and its commit is refused — leaving an unreferenced object, which the crash-recovery bullet says is fine.
- `require intent.number > published_head.number` — **the CRL number must increase.**
  - Worked example of why this is essential: CRL numbers are how verifiers decide which CRL is newer. A verifier holding CRL 48 that fetches CRL 47 should ignore it. If the publisher could commit a lower number as the head, the public artifact would go backwards and verifiers would be split between two views.
- `require intent.covered_generation >= published_head.covered_generation` — **coverage may never regress.**
  - Note the `>=` rather than `>`: a republish covering the same generation is allowed, which is what a scheduled refresh with no new revocations does. What is forbidden is publishing a CRL that covers *less* than the current one.
  - Worked example of the failure this blocks: a delayed build that started before three recent revocations commits after a newer build. Its coverage is generation 44 against a published head of 47. Committing it would un-revoke three certificates from the public artifact's point of view.
- `verify removal rules against previous published snapshot` — check that nothing was dropped that the removal rule says must stay.
  - Worked example: the previous CRL listed serial `X`, whose certificate expired an hour ago. This build dropped it. The check confirms serial `X` has already appeared on a published CRL after its expiry — and if not, the build is wrong.
- `commit snapshot, published_head, audit, and notification outbox` — four things atomically, so there is no state in which the head advanced without a snapshot record or an audit entry.

---

> - Stable CDP
>   - Serve the immutable object selected by the authoritative published head.
>   - Bound endpoint and CDN cache age by the declared status-validity policy.
>   - Do not let workers unconditionally overwrite latest.crl after committing a conditional database pointer.
>   - That separate overwrite would let a delayed worker regress the public artifact.

**What this says in plain words**
The distribution point must resolve through the database pointer, not through a file that anyone can overwrite. The third bullet names a specific bug that is very easy to write.

**The bug, spelled out**

- The design has a careful conditional commit: the head advances only if the number increases and coverage does not regress.
- Then the worker rewrites `latest.crl` so the CDP has a stable URL.
- **That second write is unconditional**, and it undoes the protection.
- Worked example:
  1. Publisher A builds CRL 47 and stalls just before rewriting `latest.crl`.
  2. Publisher B builds CRL 48, commits the head conditionally (48 > 47, coverage advanced), and rewrites `latest.crl` to point at CRL 48.
  3. Publisher A wakes up. Its *database* commit already failed or already happened — but its `latest.crl` write has no such guard. It overwrites the alias to point at CRL 47.
  - The database says the head is 48. The public artifact is 47. Verifiers fetch a CRL that is missing whatever generation 48 added, and the system's own records say everything is fine.
- **"Serve the immutable object selected by the authoritative published head"** is the fix: the endpoint resolves the current head from the database and serves that object, rather than serving whatever an alias currently points at.
- The general lesson: **a conditional write followed by an unconditional write is only as safe as the unconditional one.** Any protection applied to one and not the other is not protection.

**The second bullet**
"Bound endpoint and CDN cache age by the declared status-validity policy" — the `max-age` must be consistent with what verifiers are told about freshness. Worked example of the inconsistency: promising status no more than 5 minutes stale while the CDN caches for an hour makes the promise false for 55 minutes of every hour.

---

> - Crash recovery
>   - Before head commit, an uploaded artifact is unreferenced and may be retried or garbage-collected.
>   - After head commit, an outbox relay repairs missed notifications.
>   - Retrying randomized signing may yield different signature bytes for the same reserved TBS.
>   - Verify and publish the exact immutable artifact selected by the winning head.

**What this says in plain words**
The head commit is the dividing line. Before it, nothing has happened publicly. After it, everything downstream is repairable.

1. **Before head commit: the artifact is unreferenced.**
   - Worked example: the publisher uploads CRL 48 and crashes. Object 48 sits in storage, nothing points at it, verifiers never see it. On restart the publisher resumes the same intent and tries again. The stray object is garbage-collected later.
   - This is the payoff of verify-then-advance: a crash in the middle leaves litter, not damage.
2. **After head commit: an outbox relay repairs missed notifications.**
   - Worked example: the head commits and the publisher crashes before purging the CDN or notifying anyone. The outbox row committed with the head, so a relay picks it up and completes the downstream work. The publication is real; only the announcement was delayed.
3. **"Retrying randomized signing may yield different signature bytes for the same reserved TBS."**
   - The same subtlety as in `s2.01`: ECDSA and RSA-PSS are randomized, so signing identical bytes twice produces two different, both-valid signatures.
   - Worked example of the consequence here: attempt 1 produced artifact `a1b2…` and attempt 2 produced `c3d4…`. Both are valid CRL 48 with identical contents. They are different bytes with different hashes — which is exactly why the storage key includes the artifact hash, so neither overwrites the other.
4. **"Verify and publish the exact immutable artifact selected by the winning head."**
   - The resolution. Two valid artifacts may exist; the head commit names one, and that is the one served. The other is litter.
   - Worked example of why this must be explicit: a naive implementation might serve "the object for CRL 48", which is now ambiguous. Selecting by the head's recorded `object_ref` and `sha256` removes the ambiguity entirely.

---

> - Freshness
>   - Proposed regular publication is every 1 h with a 60 s event debounce.
>   - Incident publication bypasses debounce but uses the same fenced path.
>   - Previously cached signed evidence remains acceptable until its verifier-enforced freshness deadline.
>   - CDN purge accelerates refresh but is not the correctness proof.

**What this says in plain words**
Hourly publication, a one-minute debounce, an urgent path that skips the debounce and nothing else, and two reminders about what freshness actually depends on.

1. **Every hour, with a 60-second debounce.** The debounce collapses a mass revocation into a manageable number of builds.
2. **"Incident publication bypasses debounce but uses the same fenced path."**
   - The urgent path is faster, not looser. It skips the wait; it does not skip the fence, the intent reservation, the verification, or the conditional head commit.
   - Worked example of why that matters: an emergency is exactly when a second operator also hits publish, when a publisher is mid-stall, and when everyone is in a hurry. A path that relaxed the safety checks under urgency would fail precisely when the consequences are worst.
3. **"Previously cached signed evidence remains acceptable until its verifier-enforced freshness deadline."**
   - The propagation bound, restated. Publishing faster does not shorten what a verifier already holds.
4. **"CDN purge accelerates refresh but is not the correctness proof."**
   - Worked example of the mistaken belief: an operator purges the CDN after an emergency revocation and reports that the revocation has propagated. It has not. Every verifier holding a CRL with a `nextUpdate` twelve hours out will not fetch again for twelve hours, purged edge or not.
   - The purge helps whoever fetches next. It does nothing for those who already have a valid copy — which, by design, is most of them.

| Purge is not proof | CDN purging helping the next fetch, not existing holders. | Most verifiers hold a still-valid copy. |

## OCSP responder, stapling, and relying-party policy — IC3 baseline

Request routing, status semantics, precomputation, the nonce policy, responder key custody, stapling, the fail-policy choice, and the propagation bound said out loud.

---

> - Request routing
>   - `CertID = {hashAlgorithm, issuerNameHash, issuerKeyHash, serialNumber}` (RFC 6960).
>   - The issuer hash map resolves the pair to `(tenant_id, issuer_id)`
>   - A request never names a tenant.
>   - SHA-1 and SHA-256 hashes are both precomputed per issuer because clients differ.

**What this says in plain words**
An OCSP request contains four fields and nothing else. The responder must work out everything from them.

**The four fields**

1. **`hashAlgorithm`** — which hash was used for the next two fields.
2. **`issuerNameHash`** — a hash of the issuer's distinguished name.
3. **`issuerKeyHash`** — a hash of the issuer's public key.
4. **`serialNumber`** — the certificate being asked about.

**"A request never names a tenant"**

- The constraint that shapes the whole routing design, and it is easy to miss until you try to implement it.
- Worked example: 10,000 issuers across many tenants. A request arrives from an anonymous client on the internet. There is no authentication, no header, no tenant field — just two hashes and a serial. The responder must decide which issuer this concerns.
- That is exactly what `issuer_hash_map` is for, and it is why that table is the one table in the schema whose key does not begin with `tenant_id`.
- Worked example of the alternative failing: a design that expected a tenant in the path — `/v1/{tenant}/ocsp` — would not be a standard OCSP responder, because the URL is embedded in every certificate's AIA extension at issuance and standard clients construct requests without any such notion.

**"SHA-1 and SHA-256 hashes are both precomputed per issuer because clients differ"**

- Worked example of why both are needed: RFC 6960 specifies SHA-1 for `CertID` by default, and a very large installed base of clients sends SHA-1. Newer clients may send SHA-256. A responder supporting only one simply cannot route half its traffic.
- So the map holds two rows per issuer, and the responder looks up whichever the client used.
- Worked example of why SHA-1 here is not a weakness: these hashes route a request to an issuer. A collision misroutes a lookup, producing `unknown` or `unauthorized` — not a forged `good`. The security of the answer rests on the response's own signature, which is not SHA-1.

---

> - Status semantics
>   - Proposed stronger policy: good requires membership in the issued set and absence from the covered revocation set.
>   - RFC OCSP good status alone does not generally prove issuance or path validity.
>   - `revoked`: `revocationTime` and `reason` from the ledger.
>   - `unknown`: the issuer is known but the serial is not in the issued set.
>   - Never answer `good` for a serial the issuer never recorded
>   - The responder holds an issued-serial set replicated from the issuance ledger.
>   - Store or snapshot unavailable: answer `tryLater`, never `good`.
>   - `good` does not assert validity dates, path validity, or hostname
>   - That stays with the verifier.

**What this says in plain words**
Exactly what each status means, and one deliberate strengthening of the standard.

**The strengthening, which is the most important idea here**

> Proposed stronger policy: good requires membership in the issued set and absence from the covered revocation set.
> RFC OCSP good status alone does not generally prove issuance or path validity.

- Under the standard, `good` means "not on my revocation list". That is weaker than most people assume.
- Worked example of the weakness: a responder that only knows about revocations receives a query for serial `0x9999`, a number no CA ever issued. It is not on the revocation list, so a naive implementation answers `good`. The responder has just vouched for a certificate that does not exist.
- Worked example of why that is dangerous: combine it with any path by which an attacker obtains a signed certificate outside the normal issuance flow — the orphan case from `s2.01`, where a worker signs and crashes before recording. A revocation-only responder would answer `good` for it. A responder checking the issued set answers `unknown`, which a cautious verifier treats as a failure.
- So this design requires **two** conditions for `good`: the serial is in the issued set, and it is not in the covered revocation set. That is stricter than the RFC and it is the right default for a private CA where you control both sides.

**The four statuses**

- **`good`** — issued and not revoked, under the strengthened rule.
- **`revoked`** — with `revocationTime` and `reason` taken from the ledger. Worked example of the reason mattering: a verifier may treat `keyCompromise` differently from `superseded`, for instance by refusing to accept any signature the certificate ever made rather than only future ones.
- **`unknown`** — "the issuer is known but the serial is not in the issued set". A precise meaning: I am authoritative for this issuer, and I have never heard of this certificate.
- **`tryLater`** — **"Store or snapshot unavailable: answer `tryLater`, never `good`."**
  - The fail-safe rule. Worked example of violating it: the snapshot failed to load, so the responder has no data. Answering `good` because nothing is in its (empty) revocation set would tell every client that every certificate is fine — turning an internal failure into a fleet-wide revocation bypass.
  - `tryLater` says "I cannot answer", which lets the verifier apply its own fail policy. That is the honest response, and it hands the decision to the party entitled to make it.

**What `good` does not mean**

> `good` does not assert validity dates, path validity, or hostname. That stays with the verifier.

- Worked example: a certificate expired last week. OCSP will still answer `good` — it was issued and it was not revoked. That is a correct answer to the question OCSP asks.
- The verifier is still responsible for checking the validity window, building a path to a trusted anchor, and matching the hostname. A `good` response is one input to a decision, not the decision.
- Worked example of the mistake: an application that checks OCSP and skips path validation because "the CA said it is good". The responder has said nothing about whether that certificate chains to anything the application trusts.

---

> - Precomputed responses
>   - A batch job signs one response per unexpired certificate per issuer every `1 h` with `nextUpdate = thisUpdate + 8 h`.
>   - Responders load the snapshot into memory keyed by `(issuer_id, serial)` and serve DER bytes without signing.
>   - The `GET` form follows RFC 5019 so a CDN can cache responses with `max-age` bounded by `nextUpdate`.
>   - Revoked serials are re-signed first after each publish so the negative propagates before the routine refresh.

**What this says in plain words**
Sign everything hourly, give each response eight hours of validity, serve from memory, cache at the CDN — and always update the revoked ones first.

**The 1-hour refresh with an 8-hour `nextUpdate`**

- The gap between them is deliberate and is worth reading carefully.
- Worked example of why validity is eight times the refresh interval: responses are re-signed hourly, so under normal operation a client always gets something under an hour old. The 8-hour window is *slack* — if the precompute job fails, responses stay valid for another seven hours while someone fixes it.
- Worked example of the cost, which the capacity section already named: 8 hours is also the attacker's window under stapling. A compromised server can keep presenting its last `good` response for up to 8 hours after revocation.
- So this single pair of numbers encodes the central trade of the whole design: **longer validity buys outage tolerance and sells compromise containment.** Shortening `nextUpdate` to 2 hours would quarter the attacker's window and leave only one hour of slack for a failed precompute run.

**Serving from memory**
Keyed by `(issuer_id, serial)` — the pair the `CertID` resolves to. The responder returns stored DER bytes and performs no signature operation, which is what keeps the HSM off the hot path.

**The RFC 5019 `GET` form**

- **"`max-age` bounded by `nextUpdate`"** is the important constraint. A cached response must not outlive its own validity.
- Worked example of getting it wrong: caching for 24 hours a response whose `nextUpdate` is 8 hours out. After hour 8 the CDN serves an expired response, and a strict verifier rejects it — so the certificate fails not because anything is wrong with it but because the cache outlived the evidence.

**"Revoked serials are re-signed first after each publish"**

- The ordering rule from the architecture trace, stated here with its reason: **"so the negative propagates before the routine refresh."**
- Worked example: 1,000,000 certificates are re-signed hourly, taking most of the hour. A revocation lands at 09:05. If the newly revoked serial were re-signed in its natural position in the batch, it might not be updated until 09:50 — 45 minutes during which the responder holds and serves a `good` response for a certificate that is revoked.
- Re-signing revoked serials first means the negative is available within seconds. The cost is nothing: revocations are a tiny fraction of the set.
- The general shape: **when a batch updates everything on a schedule, the urgent subset is done out of order.**

---

> - Nonce policy
>   - Baseline ignores request nonces and returns the precomputed response
>   - This is the lightweight-profile behavior and the reason responses are cacheable.
>   - Nonce-required clients need a separately supported live-signing profile or an explicitly negotiated alternative.
>   - Do not silently redirect them to a non-nonce protocol and call it equivalent.
>   - A live-signed nonce path exists only as a rate-limited stretch item.

**What this says in plain words**
Nonces and precomputation are incompatible, the baseline chooses precomputation, and the third bullet insists on being honest about it.

**Why they are incompatible**

- A **nonce** is a random value the client puts in its request and requires the responder to echo inside the *signed* response, proving the response was generated just now for this request.
- A precomputed response was signed before the request existed, so it cannot contain the nonce. There is no way around this; it is not an implementation gap.

**The baseline: ignore nonces, serve the cached response**

- **"This is the lightweight-profile behavior and the reason responses are cacheable."** RFC 5019 explicitly defines this profile, so ignoring nonces is standard behavior rather than a shortcut.
- Worked example of what it buys: because the response does not depend on the request's nonce, identical requests have identical answers, so a CDN can cache them. That is what makes 5,000 requests per second affordable.

**The honesty requirement, which is the part worth dwelling on**

> Do not silently redirect them to a non-nonce protocol and call it equivalent.

- Worked example of the dishonest behavior: a client sends a nonce because its threat model includes response replay. The responder returns a cached response without the nonce and the client, if lenient, accepts it. The client believes it has replay protection and does not.
- That is worse than refusing, because the client's security decision was made on a false premise. A client that knows it cannot get nonces can choose differently — shorter validity windows, a different check, or accepting the risk knowingly.
- The general principle: **when you cannot provide a security property a caller asked for, say so; silently substituting a weaker guarantee is worse than providing none.**
- The live-signing path exists as a **rate-limited stretch item**, which is the honest offering: it is possible, it costs HSM capacity per request, and it is therefore bounded.

---

> - Responder key custody
>   - A delegated responder certificate carries EKU `id-kp-OCSPSigning`, is signed by the issuing CA, and is valid `≤ 30 d`.
>   - The private key lives in the HSM under `responder_key_ref`
>   - The batch job calls sign and never reads the key.
>   - The nocheck extension asks clients to omit responder-certificate revocation checks.
>   - Bound that exposure with short responder validity and controlled rollover.
>   - Rotation keeps two responder versions overlapping so a snapshot signed by the previous key stays verifiable until its `nextUpdate`.

**What this says in plain words**
The responder has its own short-lived certificate and its own HSM-held key, and one of its extensions asks clients to skip a check — which is why the lifetime is short.

**The delegated certificate**

- `id-kp-OCSPSigning` Extended Key Usage, signed by the issuing CA, valid 30 days or less.
- Worked example of the blast-radius benefit: if the responder key is compromised, the attacker can sign false `good` responses for at most 30 days, and only for that issuer. The CA key is untouched, and the CA can issue a fresh responder certificate immediately.

**The `nocheck` extension, which is the interesting part**

- **`id-pkix-ocsp-nocheck`** is an extension placed on a responder certificate asking clients not to check *its* revocation status.
- Worked example of the problem it solves: a client validating an OCSP response wants to know the responder certificate is not revoked. How does it check? By asking an OCSP responder — which is the thing it is trying to validate. That is circular, and a naive implementation can recurse or deadlock.
- The extension breaks the loop by saying "do not check me".
- **The cost, stated plainly: "Bound that exposure with short responder validity and controlled rollover."**
  - Worked example of the exposure: if the responder key is compromised, you cannot revoke the responder certificate effectively, because clients have been told not to check. The compromise lasts until the certificate expires.
  - Which is *why* the lifetime is 30 days rather than a year. The lifetime is not a convention — it is the containment mechanism for a risk the `nocheck` extension creates.
  - The general shape: **when you disable a safety check, the thing that replaces it has to be named, and here it is a short expiry.**

**Rotation overlap**

- **"Two responder versions overlapping so a snapshot signed by the previous key stays verifiable until its `nextUpdate`."**
- Worked example: the responder rotates at 12:00. Responses signed at 11:30 by the old key are in verifier caches with `nextUpdate` at 19:30. If the old responder certificate were withdrawn at 12:00, every one of those cached responses would become unverifiable — clients could not find the signer's certificate. Keeping both live for at least 8 hours means every response in circulation has a valid signer.
- The overlap length is therefore not arbitrary: it must be at least the response validity window.

---

> - Stapling
>   - Servers we control fetch their own response every `1 h` and present it in the TLS `status_request` extension.
>   - The server keeps the last good response on disk, so a control-plane or responder outage does not break handshakes until that response reaches `nextUpdate`.
>   - Servers alarm when remaining staple validity drops under `2 h`.
>   - Must-staple (RFC 7633) turns a missing staple into a hard failure and is enabled per certificate profile, never globally.

**What this says in plain words**
Servers fetch their own status and hand it to clients during the handshake. Four details, each doing distinct work.

1. **"Servers we control fetch their own response every 1 h and present it in the TLS `status_request` extension."**
   - `status_request` is the TLS extension that carries a stapled OCSP response inside the handshake.
   - Worked example of the traffic reduction: 10,000 servers each handling 1,000 handshakes per second means up to 10,000,000 client OCSP requests per second without stapling. With it, those 10,000 servers make about 2.8 requests per second in total. Six and a half orders of magnitude.
   - Worked example of the privacy benefit: without stapling, a client's OCSP request tells the responder which server that client is connecting to, and when. Stapling removes that signal entirely.
2. **"The server keeps the last good response on disk."**
   - Static stability, one layer further out. Worked example: the responder fleet is unavailable for three hours. Servers keep stapling the response they already have, which stays valid for up to 8 hours. No handshake fails.
   - **On disk**, so a server restart mid-outage does not lose it — the same cache-on-boot lesson as the sibling designs.
3. **"Servers alarm when remaining staple validity drops under 2 h."**
   - The outcome alarm, at the right layer. Worked example of why 2 hours: with a 1-hour refresh and an 8-hour validity, remaining validity should normally sit between 7 and 8 hours. Dropping below 2 means six consecutive refreshes have failed, and there are two hours left to fix it before handshakes are affected.
   - Note what is alarmed on: not "OCSP fetch failures", which would be noisy and might be transient, but the remaining budget — the thing that actually determines whether there is a problem.
4. **"Must-staple (RFC 7633) turns a missing staple into a hard failure and is enabled per certificate profile, never globally."**
   - **Must-staple** is an extension placed in a *certificate* declaring that a valid stapled response is mandatory. A compliant client that receives no staple rejects the connection.
   - Worked example of what it buys: it closes the attack where a compromised server simply omits the staple and relies on the client soft-failing. With must-staple, omitting the staple is itself a failure.
   - Worked example of why "never globally": the extension is baked into the certificate at issuance and lasts its whole lifetime. Enable it fleet-wide, and any server whose stapling breaks — a misconfiguration, a responder outage past the validity window, an old TLS terminator that does not staple at all — goes hard-down with no way to soften it short of reissuing every certificate.
   - Per profile means you enable it where stapling is known to work reliably and the threat model justifies it, and leave it off elsewhere. This is the same "exception gets its own profile" discipline as the legacy RSA case in `s2.01`.

---

> - Soft-fail versus hard-fail
>   - Soft-fail: the verifier accepts when status is unreachable
>     - Revocation becomes advisory and the bound is the certificate TTL.
>   - Hard-fail rejects when no fresh acceptable status evidence exists.
>   - A cached fresh CRL or staple can avoid a live responder dependency until its deadline.
>   - Choose fail policy per relying-party profile.
>   - Strict internal clients require fresh status evidence and fail closed when none remains.
>   - Explicit soft-fail profiles accept a longer exposure window for unavailable status.

**What this says in plain words**
The verifier's choice, its consequences in both directions, and the observation that makes hard-fail practical.

**Soft-fail**

- Accept when status is unreachable. **"Revocation becomes advisory and the bound is the certificate TTL."**
- Worked example of how completely it defeats revocation: an attacker holding a stolen key and able to block the victim's access to the CDP and the responder. Every soft-fail verifier accepts the revoked certificate indefinitely. The attacker does not need to break anything cryptographic — just make a network unreachable.

**Hard-fail**

- Reject when no fresh acceptable status evidence exists. **Note the careful phrasing: "no fresh acceptable status evidence"**, not "cannot reach a responder".

**The third bullet, which is what makes hard-fail viable**

> A cached fresh CRL or staple can avoid a live responder dependency until its deadline.

- Hard-fail does not mean checking the responder on every handshake. It means *having* fresh evidence.
- Worked example: a verifier holds a CRL fetched two hours ago with a `nextUpdate` twenty-two hours out. The responder and the CDN are both unreachable. The verifier accepts certificates normally, because it has fresh acceptable evidence. It only fails closed once that evidence expires.
- This is the observation that turns hard-fail from "the revocation service is now a hard dependency of every connection" into "the revocation service must be reachable at least once per validity window". Those are enormously different availability requirements.
- Worked example of the resulting requirement: with 24-hour CRLs, the CDP must be reachable once a day per verifier. That is an availability target you can meet.

**Per-profile choice**

- **"Strict internal clients require fresh status evidence and fail closed when none remains."** For clients you control, hard-fail is achievable and worth it.
- **"Explicit soft-fail profiles accept a longer exposure window."** For clients you do not control, soft-fail is what you will get, and the word **explicit** matters: it should be a recorded decision with a known exposure, not an accident.

---

> - Propagation bound stated aloud
>   - Stapled server, uncompromised: `≤ 1 h` refresh plus `5 min` publish lag.
>   - Stapled server whose key is stolen: `≤ 8 h`, the validity of the last `good` response the attacker holds.
>   - A strict CRL verifier can accept previously fresh evidence only through nextUpdate plus its declared clock allowance.
>   - A fetch interval cannot extend nextUpdate unless the profile explicitly allows stale evidence.
>   - A live-OCSP client may retain a previously good response through that response's accepted validity window.
>   - A five-minute publisher or CDN refresh does not erase an attacker's still-valid signed response.

**What this says in plain words**
The numbers, per scenario, said out loud rather than hedged. This is the bullet to deliver in an interview when asked "how fast does revocation take effect?"

**The two stapling cases, and the contrast between them**

1. **Uncompromised stapled server: about 1 hour 5 minutes.**
   - The server refreshes hourly and the publisher lags 5 minutes. A cooperating server picks up the revocation within about an hour.
2. **Stapled server whose key is stolen: up to 8 hours.**
   - **"The validity of the last `good` response the attacker holds."**
   - Worked example: the key is stolen at 10:00 and the certificate revoked at 10:05. The attacker holds a `good` response fetched at 09:30 with `nextUpdate` at 17:30. They simply keep stapling it. Every client sees a correctly signed, unexpired proof that the certificate is fine, until 17:30.
   - The attacker forges nothing and blocks nothing. They present truthful-when-issued evidence that has not yet expired.
- **The contrast is the point**: the same mechanism gives roughly one hour when the server cooperates and eight hours when it does not. And the case you care about is precisely the one where it does not cooperate.
- Which means the effective bound under stapling is the **response validity**, controlled by the party who may be compromised. That is the sentence to say.

**The three CRL-side statements**

3. **"A strict CRL verifier can accept previously fresh evidence only through `nextUpdate` plus its declared clock allowance."**
   - The **clock allowance** is a small tolerance for skew between the verifier's clock and the issuer's. It extends the acceptance window slightly and should be declared rather than assumed.
4. **"A fetch interval cannot extend `nextUpdate` unless the profile explicitly allows stale evidence."**
   - The correction of a common confusion. A verifier configured to fetch every 6 hours does not thereby get 6-hour freshness if the CRL's `nextUpdate` is 24 hours out — nor does a verifier fetching every 48 hours get to use a CRL past its `nextUpdate`.
   - Worked example: the artifact's own `nextUpdate` is the authority on how long it may be used. The fetch interval controls how often you *refresh*, which affects how stale your copy typically is, not how long it remains acceptable.
5. **"A live-OCSP client may retain a previously good response through that response's accepted validity window."**
   - Even clients checking directly are not immune. A client that fetched a `good` response an hour ago has no reason to fetch again before `nextUpdate`.

**The closing line, which is the summary of the whole section**

> A five-minute publisher or CDN refresh does not erase an attacker's still-valid signed response.

- Worked example of the false comfort this destroys: an operator reports "we revoked it and purged the CDN within five minutes". True, and it changes nothing for any verifier or attacker holding a signed artifact that has not yet expired.
- The lever is `nextUpdate`, on both artifacts. That is the whole conclusion of the capacity section and this one, arrived at twice from different directions.

## Failure, operations, and security

A nine-row failure table, five observations, retry discipline, tenant isolation, rollout safety, and an explicit documented-versus-proposed boundary.

---

> | Failure | Blast radius | Detection | Degraded behavior | Recovery |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Ledger database unavailable (control-plane outage) | New revocations and publications | Health checks, `503` rate | CRLs and precomputed responses stay valid to `nextUpdate`; API returns `503` with `Retry-After`; responders never query the ledger | Fail over the replica; publisher resumes from `published_generation` |
> | Publisher crashes mid-publish | One issuer's freshness | Pointer lag; `nextUpdate − now` alarm | Previous CRL keeps serving | Rerun `PUBLISH` at the same `crl_number`; `put_if_absent` makes it idempotent |
> | HSM or KMS throttled or down | Every fresh signature | Signing error rate, queue age | Validity countdown; nothing becomes invalid early | Prioritize CRLs over OCSP batch; page at `50%` validity consumed |
> | CDN serves stale CRL after a failed purge | Verifiers behind that edge | Canary fetch compares `ETag` with the pointer | `max-age=300` caps staleness at `5 min` | Re-purge; verifiers can use the numbered object URL |
> | OCSP responder fleet overloaded | Live-OCSP clients only | p99 latency, `5xx` | Stapled servers unaffected; shed unknown-issuer requests first | Scale stateless responders; CDN caches `GET` responses |
> | Responder certificate expires | Every OCSP response from that issuer | Remaining-validity gauge | Verifiers reject responses; hard-fail servers stop handshakes | Auto-rotate at `50%` life with two overlapping responder versions |
> | Mass revocation spike (`10k / h`) | One issuer's CRL size and precompute backlog | Generation rate, CRL bytes | Debounce coalesces publishes; revoked serials re-signed before routine refresh | Per-tenant revocation rate quota; large-CRL guard |
> | Bad object or wrong signing key | One issuer's verifiers | Post-write download and verify fails | Pointer never advances; old CRL keeps serving | Rebuild with the correct `crl_key_ref`; audit the key reference change |
> | Revocation row dropped by mistake | Would turn revoked into good | Additive-diff guard and audit comparison | Publish blocked for that issuer | Restore the row from the audit log; republish |

**What this says in plain words**
Nine failures. Read the fourth column: in almost every row, the degraded behavior is "the previous artifact keeps serving" — because a signed artifact does not stop being valid when the thing that made it breaks.

**Row by row**

- **Ledger database unavailable.**
  - The degraded-behavior cell has three clauses and the third is the payoff: **"responders never query the ledger."** The OCSP path has no dependency on the database at all, so a total control-plane outage does not affect status answers, only status *changes*.
  - `503` with `Retry-After` is the honest response to the API caller: not a failure they should interpret, a wait they should respect.
  - Recovery "resumes from `published_generation`", which is exactly what that column is for — the publisher knows where it got to without reconstructing anything.
- **Publisher crashes mid-publish.**
  - Radius is one issuer's *freshness*, not its correctness. The previous CRL keeps serving because the pointer never moved.
  - **"Rerun `PUBLISH` at the same `crl_number`; `put_if_absent` makes it idempotent."** `put_if_absent` writes the object only if that key does not already exist, so a rerun that produces the same artifact hash is a no-op rather than an overwrite.
- **HSM or KMS throttled or down.**
  - **"Validity countdown; nothing becomes invalid early."** The distinction the architecture section insisted on: freshness stops, validity does not.
  - **"Prioritize CRLs over OCSP batch"** is a specific, defensible triage. Worked example of why that order: a CRL covers an entire issuer in one signature, while the OCSP batch needs one signature per certificate. With scarce HSM capacity, one signature protecting 100,000 certificates from going stale beats 100,000 signatures protecting the same set individually.
  - **"Page at 50% validity consumed"** — with a 24-hour CRL, that pages 12 hours in, leaving 12 hours to fix it. Alarming on the budget rather than on the error rate.
- **CDN serves stale CRL after a failed purge.**
  - **Detection: "Canary fetch compares `ETag` with the pointer."** A synthetic fetch through the CDN, compared against what the database says is current. Worked example of why this is the right detector: CDN purges fail silently. Nothing in the control plane knows an edge is still serving an old object unless something goes and looks.
  - **"Verifiers can use the numbered object URL"** as a workaround — the immutable per-number URL is never stale, because each number is a distinct object.
- **OCSP responder fleet overloaded.**
  - **"Blast radius: Live-OCSP clients only"**, and **"Stapled servers unaffected"** — the design's whole traffic-reduction argument paying out during an incident.
  - **"Shed unknown-issuer requests first"** is a well-chosen load-shedding rule. Worked example: requests carrying an issuer hash the map does not resolve are almost certainly noise — scanners, misconfigured clients, or attacks. Dropping those first preserves capacity for legitimate traffic, and the legitimate answer to them was going to be `unauthorized` anyway.
- **Responder certificate expires.**
  - The largest radius in the table: **every** OCSP response from that issuer becomes unverifiable at once, because clients cannot validate the signer.
  - **"Hard-fail servers stop handshakes"** — this is the failure that turns a certificate-management oversight into an outage.
  - Recovery is prevention: **auto-rotate at 50% of life**, so a 30-day certificate rotates at day 15, with two versions overlapping.
- **Mass revocation spike.**
  - Radius is CRL size and precompute backlog — a capacity effect, not a correctness one.
  - Degraded behavior names both mitigations already in the design: the debounce coalesces publishes, and revoked serials are re-signed first.
  - **"Large-CRL guard"** is a new mechanism here: something that notices a CRL growing past expected bounds. Worked example of what it catches: a bug revoking an entire issuer's certificates would balloon the list from 0.5 to 50 megabytes, and the guard stops the publish rather than distributing it.
- **Bad object or wrong signing key.**
  - **"Detection: post-write download and verify fails"** — the verify-then-advance step catching exactly what it exists for.
  - **"Pointer never advances; old CRL keeps serving."** A wrong signing key produces an artifact that no verifier would accept, and it never reaches a verifier.
  - **"Audit the key reference change"** — because a wrong `crl_key_ref` did not appear by itself; someone or something changed it, and that is the actual incident.
- **Revocation row dropped by mistake.**
  - **"Would turn revoked into good"** — the conditional is doing work. It *would*, and the guard means it does not.
  - **The additive-diff guard** is the mechanism: a new CRL must be a superset of the previous one, minus only entries whose removal the expiry rule permits. Worked example: a migration bug deletes 400 revocation rows. The next build produces a CRL missing 400 serials that the previous one contained. The guard compares, finds removals the expiry rule does not justify, and blocks the publish.
  - **"Publish blocked for that issuer"** is the right degraded behavior: serving a stale CRL is far better than serving one that silently un-revokes 400 certificates.
  - Recovery restores from the audit log, which is why the audit log is append-only and why `revocations` never gains a `DELETE` path.

---

> - Observe
>   - Minimum `nextUpdate − now` per issuer for CRL and OCSP snapshots.
>   - Publication lag from revocation generation to `published_generation`.
>   - Oldest precomputed response age and responder certificate remaining validity.
>   - CRL bytes per issuer and additive-diff guard trips.
>   - OCSP status mix (`good`, `revoked`, `unknown`, `tryLater`), CDN hit ratio, HSM signature failures.

**What this says in plain words**
Five groups. The first is the one that matters most, and it is a *minimum* across issuers rather than an average.

1. **Minimum `nextUpdate − now` per issuer, for both artifact types.**
   - The outcome alarm for the whole system: how much validity remains before verifiers start rejecting.
   - **Minimum**, because one issuer about to go stale is the incident. Worked example of why an average misleads: 9,999 issuers with 23 hours remaining and one with 20 minutes averages to 23 hours. The one is the outage.
2. **Publication lag from revocation generation to `published_generation`.**
   - The direct measure of the "revoked but not published" gap. Worked example: `next_generation` at 52 and `published_generation` at 47 means five revocations are recorded and invisible to the world.
3. **Oldest precomputed response age, and responder certificate remaining validity.**
   - Two different clocks on the OCSP path. The first catches a stalled precompute batch; the second catches the highest-radius failure in the table before it happens.
4. **CRL bytes per issuer, and additive-diff guard trips.**
   - Bytes for the size threshold that would trigger delta CRLs. **Guard trips** because a trip is never routine — it means a build tried to remove entries it should not have, which is always a bug worth investigating.
5. **OCSP status mix, CDN hit ratio, HSM signature failures.**
   - **The status mix is the subtle one.** Worked example of what it reveals: `unknown` rising sharply means clients are asking about serials that are not in the issued set — possibly a replication lag in the issued-serial set, possibly scanning, possibly a genuine issuance-path problem. And any `tryLater` at all means a responder somewhere has no snapshot, which is a fail-safe working and a fault to fix.
   - **CDN hit ratio** measures whether the RFC 5019 `GET` caching is actually working. Worked example: a hit ratio falling from 95% to 20% means something changed in how clients construct requests, and the responder fleet is about to take five times the load.

---

> - Retry discipline
>   - Revocation clients retry the same `Idempotency-Key` with exponential backoff and jitter.
>   - The publisher retries the same `crl_number`
>   - A new number is allocated only after a verified failure of the old object.
>   - HSM throttling is retried with jitter and a bounded elapsed deadline
>   - Freshness alarms cover the remainder.
>   - OCSP clients honor `Retry-After` on `tryLater`
>   - Nothing in the system retries its way to `good`.

**What this says in plain words**
Four rules and a closing sentence that is the best line in the section.

1. **Revocation clients retry the same idempotency key.** The retry is the same request, not a new one.
2. **"The publisher retries the same `crl_number`. A new number is allocated only after a verified failure of the old object."**
   - Worked example of why burning numbers is bad: if every retry took a fresh number, a flapping HSM could consume a hundred CRL numbers in an hour. Verifiers use the number to order CRLs, and large gaps make it harder to reason about what was ever published — and each abandoned number leaves an intent row and possibly an object.
   - **"Verified failure"** is the precondition for moving on: you must establish that the old object is genuinely unusable before allocating a new number, rather than assuming it from a timeout.
3. **HSM throttling: jitter, bounded elapsed deadline, and "freshness alarms cover the remainder".**
   - The retry gives up after a deadline, and what catches the problem after that is not a retry loop but the remaining-validity alarm.
   - Worked example of why that division is right: retrying a throttled HSM for six hours produces load and no progress. Giving up after a bounded period and letting the freshness alarm escalate to a human is the correct escalation, because the fix is capacity or configuration, not persistence.
4. **OCSP clients honor `Retry-After` on `tryLater`.** The responder says when to come back; the client should listen rather than hammering.
5. **"Nothing in the system retries its way to `good`."**
   - The sentence that summarizes the design's entire safety posture.
   - Worked example of what it forbids: a responder that, unable to load its snapshot, falls back to answering `good` after N failed attempts. A publisher that, unable to verify an artifact, publishes it anyway after enough retries. A verifier that, after repeated `tryLater` responses, decides to accept.
   - In every case the failure is the same shape: **persistence being treated as evidence.** Retrying tells you nothing new about the truth; it only tells you that you tried again.
   - The positive form: a `good` answer must come from data, and never from exhaustion.

---

> - Tenant isolation
>   - An issuer belongs to a compartment
>   - A policy of the documented OCI shape `allow group X to manage certificate-authority-family in compartment Y` gates revocation and forced publish.
>   - CRL objects and OCSP snapshots are per issuer
>   - Nothing aggregates across tenants.
>   - OCSP routing uses issuer hashes, so a request cannot select another tenant's issuer by name.
>   - A tenant's token receives `404` on another tenant's issuer, not `403`, to avoid existence leaks.
>   - Revocation and forced-publish rates are quota-limited per tenant so one incident cannot starve HSM capacity for others.

**What this says in plain words**
Five mechanisms. The third and fifth are specific to revocation rather than generic multi-tenancy.

1. **Compartments and an OCI-shaped policy statement** gating the two dangerous verbs: revoke and force-publish.
2. **"CRL objects and OCSP snapshots are per issuer. Nothing aggregates across tenants."**
   - Worked example of what a shared artifact would leak: a single global CRL containing every tenant's revocations would tell every verifier — including customer devices — how many certificates each issuer has revoked and when. Revocation timing during an incident is sensitive information.
   - Worked example of the blast-radius half: a bad publish to a shared artifact affects everyone. Per issuer, it affects one.
3. **"OCSP routing uses issuer hashes, so a request cannot select another tenant's issuer by name."**
   - A security property that falls out of the protocol rather than being added.
   - Worked example: an attacker cannot enumerate issuers by guessing names, because the request does not carry a name — it carries a hash of a name. To query an issuer you must already know its exact distinguished name and public key, which means you already hold a certificate from it.
4. **`404` not `403`** across tenants, consistent with the group.
5. **"Revocation and forced-publish rates are quota-limited per tenant so one incident cannot starve HSM capacity for others."**
   - The noisy-neighbour control, aimed at the one resource that cannot scale horizontally.
   - Worked example: tenant `A` suffers a compromise and revokes 100,000 certificates while repeatedly hitting force-publish. Without quotas, the HSM saturates and every other tenant's CRLs stop being refreshed — so one tenant's incident becomes everyone's freshness outage.
   - Note the subtlety: this is a quota on an *emergency* operation, which is uncomfortable but correct. The alternative is that any tenant's emergency can degrade the whole system's safety.

---

> - Rollout and rollback safety
>   - A DER-builder change is shadow-built and verified with an independent parser before the pointer moves.
>   - Responders must load the previous and the new snapshot format during a deploy.
>   - CDP and AIA URLs never change
>   - A URL change would orphan every issued certificate.
>   - Canary responders behind the load balancer
>   - Rollback is the pointer staying at the last verified snapshot.
>   - Schema changes follow expand, migrate, contract
>   - `revocations` never gains a `DELETE` path.

**What this says in plain words**
Six rules. The first and the last are the distinctive ones.

1. **"A DER-builder change is shadow-built and verified with an independent parser before the pointer moves."**
   - **Shadow-built** means the new builder produces an artifact alongside the old one, without publishing it.
   - **An independent parser** means verification uses a *different* implementation than the one that built it.
   - Worked example of why independence matters: a builder with a subtle DER encoding bug — a wrong tag, a mis-encoded length — will happily parse its own output, because the parser shares the bug's assumptions. An independent parser, such as OpenSSL, does not.
   - Worked example of the consequence without this: a malformed CRL is published and signed. Verifiers cannot parse it, so for hard-fail clients every certificate from that issuer fails. The artifact is correctly signed and completely unusable.
   - The general shape: **a self-check by the component that produced the artifact is not a check.**
2. **"Responders must load the previous and the new snapshot format during a deploy."** Standard cross-version compatibility, with a short window here but a real one.
3. **"CDP and AIA URLs never change. A URL change would orphan every issued certificate."**
   - The frozen contract from the conventions, restated as a deployment rule because a deployment is how someone would break it.
   - Worked example: moving the CRL to a new domain and decommissioning the old. Every unexpired certificate still directs verifiers to the old URL. Hard-fail verifiers reject them all; soft-fail verifiers silently stop checking revocation entirely — which is the worse outcome, because nothing fails and the protection is gone.
4. **Canary responders behind the load balancer.** A bad responder build reaches a fraction of traffic.
5. **"Rollback is the pointer staying at the last verified snapshot."**
   - Elegant, and worth noticing: there is no rollback *procedure*. Because the pointer only advances after verification, a failed publish simply leaves the pointer where it was. The previous good artifact continues serving with no action taken.
   - Worked example of the contrast: a design that overwrote `latest.crl` in place would need a real rollback — find the previous bytes, restore them, purge the CDN — under incident pressure. Here, doing nothing is the rollback.
6. **"`revocations` never gains a `DELETE` path."**
   - The strongest statement in the list. Revocation rows are never deleted by the application, ever.
   - Worked example of why: deleting a revocation row silently converts `revoked` back to `good` on the next publish. That is the single most dangerous mutation possible in this system — it restores a compromised certificate to full working order across the fleet.
   - Expiry-based removal from the *CRL* is different: the entry leaves the published artifact while the row remains in the ledger. The list shrinks; the truth does not.

An interviewer who knows the documentation covers CRL publication to a bucket and does not describe a managed OCSP endpoint now has a factual disagreement to resolve, and the candidate is defending an error rather than explaining a design.

## Alternatives and IC4 stretch

A six-row option table, four stretch scenarios, the weakest assumption, and three over-engineering warnings.

---

> | Option | What it is | Strength | Weakness | Choose when |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Full CRL | Signed list of unexpired revoked serials at a CDP URL | Offline-verifiable, CDN-cacheable, no handshake dependency | Staleness equals validity; bytes grow with revocations | Baseline for every issuer; mandatory for appliances |
> | Delta CRL | Signed list of changes since a base CRL number | Small frequent updates | Two artifacts to keep consistent; patchy verifier support | Base CRL past `≈ 10 MB` and verifiers that support it |
> | Live-signed OCSP | Responder signs each answer on request | Fresh; nonce support | HSM on the hot path; responder is a tier-0 dependency | Few controlled clients that require nonces |
> | Precomputed OCSP plus stapling | Batch-signed responses served from memory and stapled by servers | No client-to-CA call; hard-fail is viable | Validity window is the compromise window; signing scales with cert count | Servers we control; baseline OCSP shape |
> | Short-lived certificates | TTL of hours to days; expiry is the revocation | No status infrastructure on the verifier path | Issuance becomes a hot path; clock skew matters; every consumer must auto-renew | Automated internal mTLS; the honest control for soft-fail clients |
> | Push filters (CRLite-style) | Vendor pushes a compact set of all revocations | Fresh with no per-site fetch | Only one party controlling the verifier fleet can do it | Mention only; not applicable to customer verifiers |

**What this says in plain words**
Six ways to tell a verifier a certificate is no longer trustworthy. Two are chosen as the baseline, one is a stretch, one is the honest fallback, and two are named to be rejected.

**Reading the table as a spectrum**
The options run from "publish a file and hope people fetch it" to "make the problem disappear by expiring quickly". Each buys freshness at a different price.

- **Full CRL — the baseline.**
  - Strengths are all about independence: **offline-verifiable** (the signature is checkable with no network), **CDN-cacheable** (identical bytes for everyone), and **no handshake dependency** (fetched on a schedule, not per connection).
  - **"Staleness equals validity"** is the weakness in three words, and it is the propagation bound from the capacity section: a verifier holding a CRL with 20 hours of `nextUpdate` left will use it for 20 hours, correctly.
  - **"Mandatory for appliances"** in the last column. Worked example of why: a hardware appliance or a wallet-based client often supports CRL fetching and nothing else. If you do not publish a CRL, those verifiers have no revocation mechanism at all.
- **Delta CRL — the size escape hatch.**
  - **"Two artifacts to keep consistent"** is the real cost. Worked example: a verifier must hold base CRL 40 and delta CRL 47, and correctly compose them. If the base expires, or the verifier fetches a delta whose base it does not have, the composition fails in ways that are harder to debug than a single large file.
  - **"Patchy verifier support"** — the same caveat as indirect CRLs and cross-signing. The mechanism is standard and the implementations are uneven.
- **Live-signed OCSP — fresh and expensive.**
  - **"HSM on the hot path"** is disqualifying at this scale: 5,000 requests per second would mean 5,000 signatures per second, far beyond the hardware.
  - **"The responder is a tier-0 dependency"** is the deeper problem. Worked example: with live signing, a responder outage means no status answers at all — no cache can help, because every answer is unique. With precomputation, an outage still leaves valid cached responses in circulation.
  - Chosen only for **"few controlled clients that require nonces"**, which is exactly the rate-limited stretch path from the OCSP section.
- **Precomputed OCSP plus stapling — the chosen OCSP shape.**
  - **"No client-to-CA call"** — the privacy and traffic benefit.
  - **"Hard-fail is viable"** — because a stapled response arrives in the handshake, a client can insist on having one without needing to reach anything itself.
  - **"Validity window is the compromise window"** — the honest restatement of the attacker's-window problem, in the weakness column where it belongs.
- **Short-lived certificates — the honest control.**
  - **"No status infrastructure on the verifier path"** — nothing to fetch, nothing to check, nothing to soft-fail.
  - Three weaknesses, each real: issuance becomes a hot path with its own availability requirement, **clock skew matters** (a 1-hour certificate is intolerant of a 10-minute clock error in a way a 90-day one is not), and every consumer must auto-renew.
  - **"The honest control for soft-fail clients"** is the phrase that connects this row to the weakest assumption below: for verifiers that will not check revocation, expiry is the only mechanism that still functions.
- **Push filters (CRLite-style) — named and excluded.**
  - A **CRLite**-style scheme compresses every revocation in the ecosystem into a compact probabilistic structure that a vendor pushes to its clients, so a verifier can check locally with no fetch.
  - **"Only one party controlling the verifier fleet can do it"** is the disqualifier. Worked example: a browser vendor can ship this because it updates its own client on its own schedule. A private CA cannot push anything to a partner's JVM or a customer's appliance.
  - **"Mention only"** in the last column — the right posture. Knowing the technique exists and knowing precisely why it does not apply is better than not knowing either.

---

> - IC4 stretch — stronger propagation bound
>   - Requirement: `≤ 5 min` for the controlled fleet after a compromise.
>   - A five-minute compromise target requires every accepted good-status lease to expire within that budget, including skew and processing delay.
>   - A 15-minute OCSP response cannot by itself meet a five-minute bound.
>   - Add a signed revocation delta feed on the configuration channel of [s3.03](s3.03.Versioned-configuration-distribution.md) so sidecars learn of a revocation without polling.
>   - State the limit: uncontrolled clients stay bounded by the certificate TTL, so the profile also moves to short-lived leaves.

**What this says in plain words**
Someone demands five-minute revocation. The stretch answer works out what that actually requires and shows that the obvious approach cannot deliver it.

**The arithmetic that kills the obvious answer**

> A five-minute compromise target requires every accepted good-status lease to expire within that budget, including skew and processing delay.
> A 15-minute OCSP response cannot by itself meet a five-minute bound.

- The instinct is "shorten `nextUpdate` to 15 minutes and we are close enough". The arithmetic says no.
- Worked example: a response is signed at 10:00 with `nextUpdate` at 10:15. The key is stolen and the certificate revoked at 10:01. A compromised server keeps stapling that response until 10:15 — fourteen minutes, nearly three times the budget.
- And that is before clock skew and processing delay, which only extend it.
- To meet five minutes by validity alone, `nextUpdate` would have to be well under five minutes — meaning re-signing every certificate every few minutes. At one million certificates that is thousands of signatures per second, permanently.
- **The framing to take away: a propagation target is a constraint on the *lease*, not on the publisher.** Any "good" answer a verifier holds is a lease on acceptance, and the bound is how long the longest outstanding lease lives.

**The actual answer: change the channel, not the interval**

> Add a signed revocation delta feed on the configuration channel of [s3.03](s3.03.Versioned-configuration-distribution.md) so sidecars learn of a revocation without polling.

- Instead of shortening leases so verifiers re-ask sooner, **push the revocation to them** over the configuration channel they already have.
- Worked example: a sidecar is already subscribed to a configuration distribution system for other reasons. A revocation lands there within seconds as a signed delta. The sidecar applies it locally and rejects the certificate immediately, regardless of what its cached OCSP response says.
- Worked example of why this beats shortening the interval: it costs one small signed message per revocation — roughly 30 per day at the steady-state rate — instead of thousands of signatures per second forever. The cost scales with *revocations*, not with *certificates*.
- The general shape: **when a pull-based freshness bound is too slow, the fix is usually a push channel for the rare urgent case, not a faster poll for the common one.**

**The limit, stated rather than hidden**

> State the limit: uncontrolled clients stay bounded by the certificate TTL, so the profile also moves to short-lived leaves.

- The push channel only reaches things you control. A partner integration or a customer appliance receives nothing.
- So for those, the bound stays the certificate lifetime — and the only way to tighten it is short-lived certificates.
- Saying this out loud is what makes the five-minute claim honest: **five minutes for the controlled fleet, certificate TTL for everyone else.** A claim of "five-minute revocation" without that qualifier would be false.

---

> - IC4 stretch — 10× load
>   - `10M` certificates at a `1 h` refresh is `≈ 2,800` signatures per second
>   - Shard precompute per issuer across HSM partitions.
>   - Sign lazily for certificates with no observed consumer in `24 h`
>   - Precompute only the active set.
>   - Move issuers past `10 MB` to delta CRLs or partitioned CRLs scoped by the Issuing Distribution Point extension.
>   - Responders stay stateless
>   - The snapshot grows, the read path does not change.

**What this says in plain words**
Ten million certificates makes precompute the binding constraint. Three responses, and the second is the interesting one.

**The arithmetic**
10,000,000 ÷ 3,600 ≈ 2,800 signatures per second — ten times the baseline, and well past a single HSM.

**Response one: shard precompute per issuer across HSM partitions**
The straightforward scaling move. Worked example: eight partitions each handling 350 signatures per second, with issuers assigned to partitions. It also gives isolation — one issuer's mass revocation saturates one partition.

**Response two: the active-set filter**

> Sign lazily for certificates with no observed consumer in `24 h`. Precompute only the active set.

- The observation behind it: a status response nobody ever requests is a signature spent on nothing.
- Worked example: of ten million certificates, perhaps one million are on servers handling live traffic and being checked. The other nine million belong to workloads that are idle, decommissioned-but-unexpired, or simply never queried. Precomputing all ten million means 90% of the HSM's work is discarded.
- With the filter: precompute the active million (280 per second, back to the baseline load), and sign on demand for the rest — using the sign-on-miss overflow path that already exists.
- Worked example of why the fallback is acceptable here: a request for an inactive certificate is by definition rare. Paying HSM latency on a rare request is fine; paying it on every request is not.
- The general shape: **precompute the working set, not the universe, and let the existing overflow path handle the tail.**

**Response three: delta or partitioned CRLs past 10 MB**

- The threshold from the capacity section, now reached. **Partitioned CRLs scoped by the Issuing Distribution Point extension** — the IDP extension is what tells a verifier which subset of an issuer's certificates a given CRL covers, so several lists can partition one issuer's serials without ambiguity.
- Worked example of why the extension is required rather than just splitting the file: without IDP, a verifier fetching one partition cannot tell whether a serial's absence means "not revoked" or "covered by a different partition". The extension makes the scope explicit.

**The closing observation**

> Responders stay stateless. The snapshot grows, the read path does not change.

- Worth noticing because it is the payoff of the architecture. Ten times the certificates means a bigger in-memory map and identical serving code.
- Worked example of the contrast: a design that queried a database per request would need read replicas, connection pooling, and query optimization at 10×. This one needs more memory.

---

> - IC4 stretch — cost and blast radius
>   - HSM signature volume is the dominant cost
>   - The OCSP refresh interval and the active-set filter are the cost knobs.
>   - Per-issuer objects and snapshots make a bad publish affect one issuer
>   - The additive-diff guard catches a fleet-level bug per issuer.
>   - Responders are cells per availability domain
>   - A cell outage degrades live OCSP in one domain and never touches stapled servers.

**What this says in plain words**
Where the money goes, and two independent containment mechanisms.

1. **"HSM signature volume is the dominant cost."**
   - Worked example of the comparison: one million responses at 1.5 kilobytes is 1.5 gigabytes of storage — trivial. The CRLs are megabytes. The CDN traffic is modest. The signatures are 280 per second, continuously, on hardware that is either billed per operation or provisioned for peak throughput.
   - **The two knobs are the refresh interval and the active-set filter**, and they are multiplicative. Worked example: doubling the interval to 2 hours halves the load; applying a filter that covers 10% of certificates cuts it by ten. Together, a twentyfold reduction.
2. **Per-issuer objects and snapshots.**
   - **"A bad publish affects one issuer."** Worked example of the alternative: a global CRL with a builder bug breaks every verifier in every tenant simultaneously. Per issuer, the same bug is caught by the first issuer it affects.
   - **"The additive-diff guard catches a fleet-level bug per issuer."** A nice property: because the guard runs per issuer, a systematic bug trips it on the first issuer rather than after damage has spread.
3. **Responders as cells per availability domain.**
   - **"A cell outage degrades live OCSP in one domain and never touches stapled servers."** Two levels of containment in one sentence: the cell bounds the blast radius geographically, and stapling means most traffic was never depending on the responders anyway.

---

> - IC4 stretch — migration to must-staple
>   - Inventory verifiers and their fetch telemetry before enabling any hard-fail.
>   - Enable must-staple per certificate profile and cohort, with a rollback path that reissues without the extension.
>   - Treat the responder SLO as a published contract from that point.

**What this says in plain words**
Turning on must-staple makes a missing staple fatal. Three preconditions before doing it.

1. **"Inventory verifiers and their fetch telemetry before enabling any hard-fail."**
   - The same instruction as the trust-store inventory in `s2.02`, for the same reason: the failures will be verifiers nobody knew about, and finding them under incident pressure is the worst time.
   - **Fetch telemetry** specifically: not just who the verifiers are, but whether they actually fetch status. Worked example of what it reveals: a cohort that has never made a single OCSP request will fail immediately under must-staple, and CDN logs say so before you turn it on.
2. **"Per certificate profile and cohort, with a rollback path that reissues without the extension."**
   - The rollback path is the sentence to notice, and it is expensive.
   - Worked example of why: must-staple is baked into the certificate at issuance and lasts its whole lifetime. There is no configuration flag to turn it off. Rolling back means **reissuing every affected certificate** without the extension and deploying them — which is a full rotation campaign.
   - Which means must-staple must be enabled on a cohort small enough that reissuing it is survivable. Starting with 10% of a fleet is a bad idea if reissuing 10% takes three days.
3. **"Treat the responder SLO as a published contract from that point."**
   - Once must-staple is on, a responder outage past the response validity is a hard outage for every certificate carrying it.
   - Worked example of the escalation: before must-staple, a responder outage degrades a check. After, it breaks handshakes. The responder moves from a supporting service to a tier-0 dependency, and the availability commitment has to be stated and met rather than assumed.

---

> - Weakest assumption
>   - Verifiers refresh before `nextUpdate` and appliances fetch the CRL at all
>   - A wallet-based client that never fetches has the certificate TTL as its bound, and revocation is theater for it.
>   - Mitigation: per-consumer fetch telemetry from CDN logs, a longer-TTL legacy profile that carries a CRL it actually fetches, and short-lived certificates for everything automated.

**What this says in plain words**
The whole revocation system assumes someone is listening, and for a meaningful population nobody is.

**"Revocation is theater for it"**

- The bluntest sentence in the file, and it is the correct one.
- Worked example: an Oracle wallet-based database client configured without revocation checking. Every certificate it accepts is accepted on the strength of the signature and the validity dates alone. You can revoke a certificate it uses, publish perfectly, purge every cache — and that client will keep accepting it until it expires.
- The revocation happened. The publication happened. The effect, for that verifier, is precisely zero.
- Worked example of why admitting this is stronger than glossing over it: an interviewer asking "how do you know revocation works?" is testing whether you know it often does not. Claiming a five-minute propagation bound while a third of your verifiers never fetch anything would be a claim about the mechanism rather than about reality.

**The three mitigations, each aimed at a different part of the problem**

1. **"Per-consumer fetch telemetry from CDN logs."**
   - You cannot instrument a verifier you do not control, but you *can* see whether it ever fetched.
   - Worked example: CDN access logs grouped by client show which cohorts fetch the CDP and how often. A cohort with zero fetches in 30 days is one that has no revocation coverage, and that is a finding you can act on — even though the verifiers themselves are opaque.
   - The general shape: **when you cannot instrument the consumer, instrument the thing it fetches from.**
2. **"A longer-TTL legacy profile that carries a CRL it actually fetches."**
   - Meet the constrained client where it is: give it a certificate profile whose CDP points at something its implementation supports, and accept a longer lifetime in exchange.
   - Worked example: an appliance supports CRL over HTTP but not OCSP, and cannot renew often. It gets a profile with a plain HTTP CDP and a longer TTL — worse on both axes than the modern fleet, and *measurably* covered rather than assumed.
   - Same discipline as the legacy RSA profile in `s2.01`: the exception gets its own profile so it does not weaken everything else.
3. **"Short-lived certificates for everything automated."**
   - For the population you *do* control, stop depending on revocation at all. Expiry cannot be blocked, cached around, or soft-failed.

---

> - Over-engineering warning
>   - Do not build a live-signed nonce path for internal mTLS
>   - Precomputed responses plus stapling cover the controlled fleet.
>   - Do not build delta CRLs for a `0.5 MB` list.
>   - Do not build a global CRL
>   - It couples every tenant's freshness and blast radius.

**What this says in plain words**
Three things not to build, each anchored to a specific fact from earlier in the file.

1. **"Do not build a live-signed nonce path for internal mTLS."**
   - Anchored to who the clients are. Worked example: nonces protect against response replay, which matters for a client that cannot trust its network and cannot cache safely. Internal mTLS clients in a controlled fleet get freshness from stapling and short response validity instead.
   - Building it means putting the HSM on a request path and making the responder tier-0, to solve a problem the controlled fleet does not have.
2. **"Do not build delta CRLs for a 0.5 MB list."**
   - Anchored to the number from the capacity section. Worked example of the disproportion: delta CRLs add a second artifact, a composition rule, a consistency requirement between base and delta, and a dependency on patchy verifier support — to reduce a fetch from 0.5 megabytes to perhaps 5 kilobytes, on a file fetched hourly and served by a CDN.
3. **"Do not build a global CRL. It couples every tenant's freshness and blast radius."**
   - Two distinct harms in one clause.
   - **Freshness coupling.** Worked example: one global CRL means every tenant's revocations wait for the same publish cycle, and a build blocked by one tenant's additive-diff guard trip stops publication for everyone.
   - **Blast-radius coupling.** Worked example: a malformed global CRL breaks every verifier in every tenant at once, where a per-issuer artifact breaks one.
   - And the leak noted in the isolation section: a shared list reveals each tenant's revocation volume and timing to anyone who can fetch it.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Offline-verifiable | Checkable from the signature alone, with no network call. | The CRL's core strength. |
| Staleness equals validity | A verifier correctly using an artifact until its `nextUpdate`. | The CRL's core weakness. |
| Delta CRL | A signed list of changes since a base CRL number. | Two artifacts to compose; patchy support. |
| Tier-0 dependency | A service whose outage breaks the primary path. | What live-signed OCSP makes the responder. |

## Level signal — IC3 baseline vs IC4 stretch

One row per interview phase: what must land, what raises it, and the observable that separates the two.

---

> | Phase | IC3 must land | IC4 adds | Separating signal |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Requirements | Relying-party cohorts, propagation bound, soft-fail versus hard-fail named | Splits the bound by cohort and states which cohort cannot be bounded | Treats the fail policy as a requirement, not an implementation detail |
> | Estimates | Revocation rate, CRL bytes, OCSP signing load | Shows that validity, not publisher speed, dominates the bound | A number changes `nextUpdate`, not just fleet size |
> | Architecture | Control-plane ledger, publisher, object storage plus CDN, responders off the database | States the cost of each split and where static stability comes from | Every component has a mechanism and a price |
> | Deep dive | Generation ledger, publish-then-advance, `good` never from an outage | Additive-diff guard, responder key custody, the attacker's window under stapling | Reasons about what a cached artifact still says after the truth changed |
> | Operations | Failure table, freshness alarms, HSM custody, tenant quotas | Rollout of must-staple, per-issuer blast radius, cost knobs | Volunteers degradation and rollback before being asked |
> | Wrap | Recap and the CRL-versus-OCSP tradeoff | Names short-lived certificates as the honest control and the weakest assumption first | Identifies the flaw before the interviewer does |

**What this says in plain words**
A self-assessment grid. The second column is a checklist of content; the fourth is what actually distinguishes a stronger answer from a merely complete one.

**Why the fourth column is the one to score**
In every row the difference is not more material. It is a different kind of claim about the same material.

- **Row: Requirements.**
  - Must land: the relying-party cohorts, the propagation bound, and soft-fail versus hard-fail named explicitly.
  - Adds: splitting the bound by cohort, and stating which cohort **cannot** be bounded at all.
  - Separating signal: **"Treats the fail policy as a requirement, not an implementation detail."**
  - Worked example of the difference: a weaker answer designs a publication pipeline and mentions near the end that verifiers may soft-fail. The stronger answer opens by saying the fail policy determines whether any of this has an effect — because a soft-fail verifier that cannot reach the CDP accepts a revoked certificate indefinitely, and no amount of publishing changes that. The policy is not something the design chooses, and that is precisely why it belongs in requirements.
  - The stretch half is the harder one: naming the cohort that cannot be bounded. Worked example: wallet-based clients that never fetch have the certificate TTL as their bound and nothing else. Saying so up front is what makes every later number honest.
- **Row: Estimates.**
  - Must land: the revocation rate, CRL bytes, and OCSP signing load.
  - Adds: showing that validity — not publisher speed — dominates the propagation bound.
  - Separating signal: **"A number changes `nextUpdate`, not just fleet size."**
  - Worked example of passing it: the bound decomposes into 5 minutes of publish lag, 5 minutes of CDN age, and up to 24 hours of held-artifact validity. The first two are 0.7% of the total, so optimizing the publisher is wasted effort and shortening `nextUpdate` is the entire lever. That conclusion comes from arithmetic and it redirects where engineering goes.
  - Worked example of failing it: quoting the same three terms and then describing an optimized publisher.
- **Row: Architecture.**
  - Must land: the control-plane ledger, the publisher, object storage plus CDN, and responders that do not touch the database.
  - Adds: the cost of each split and where static stability comes from.
  - Separating signal: **every component states both its mechanism and its price.**
  - Worked example of the price most often omitted: the CDN buys static stability — a signed CRL is valid with no service up — and costs `max-age` and purge latency, both of which land in the propagation bound. Saying the second half unprompted is the signal.
  - "Where static stability comes from" is the specific stretch: not "the system is resilient" but "the artifact is signed, so it is valid without anything running".
- **Row: Deep dive.**
  - Must land: the generation ledger, publish-then-advance, and `good` never coming from an outage.
  - Adds: the additive-diff guard, responder key custody, and the attacker's window under stapling.
  - Separating signal: **"Reasons about what a cached artifact still says after the truth changed."**
  - This is the best-phrased separating signal in the file, because it names the thing revocation is uniquely about.
  - Worked example of what that reasoning sounds like: the certificate was revoked at 10:05, and a compromised server holds a `good` response signed at 09:30 with `nextUpdate` at 17:30. Nothing is forged and nothing is blocked. The attacker simply keeps presenting evidence that was true when issued and has not yet expired, and every client accepts it until 17:30.
  - Worked example of the weaker version: describing publication mechanics accurately and treating "we published the revocation" as the end of the story. The whole difficulty of revocation is what happens to answers already in circulation.
- **Row: Operations.**
  - Must land: the failure table, freshness alarms, HSM custody, and tenant quotas.
  - Adds: the must-staple rollout, per-issuer blast radius, and the cost knobs.
  - Separating signal: **"Volunteers degradation and rollback before being asked."**
  - Worked example specific to this design: saying unprompted that rollback is the pointer staying at the last verified snapshot — that there is no rollback *procedure*, because a failed publish simply leaves the pointer where it was. That is a design property most answers do not notice they have.
- **Row: Wrap.**
  - Must land: the recap and the CRL-versus-OCSP tradeoff.
  - Adds: naming short-lived certificates as the honest control, and the weakest assumption first.
  - Separating signal: finding the flaw before the interviewer does.
  - Worked example of why this particular admission is credible: the weakest assumption is that verifiers refresh at all, and for a real population they do not. Naming that — rather than a comfortable-sounding weakness — is what makes the rest of the answer trustworthy, and it leads naturally to short-lived certificates as the one control that cannot be blocked, cached around, or soft-failed.

**How to use this table in preparation**
Rehearse the whole answer, then score only the fourth column. Each entry is a yes-or-no observable rather than an impression, which is what makes it usable on yourself.

## Interview pushback and validation

Eight questions with a baseline and a stretch answer each, then ten validation scenarios.

---

> - Q1: Revocation is urgent
>   - Why return `202` instead of publishing synchronously?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - The durable ledger write is synchronous and ordered
>     - Signing and distribution are asynchronous and idempotent, and the operation exposes the publication generation.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Forced publish bypasses the debounce, but the bound is still the validity of artifacts already cached, so a synchronous publish would not shorten it.

**What this question is testing**
Whether you can defend asynchrony under the one circumstance where it feels wrong.

**The baseline: split the operation in two**

- **The part that must be immediate is immediate.** The ledger write — the durable, ordered record that this certificate is revoked — happens synchronously before the `202` returns.
- **The part that takes time is asynchronous.** Signing, object storage, verification, pointer advance, CDN purge.
- **And the operation exposes the publication generation**, so the caller can find out when the second part completed.
- Worked example of why this split is the right one: the fact that must never be lost is the revocation decision. Once it is committed with a generation number, every subsequent build will include it, whatever crashes in between. Holding the HTTP connection open through the signing and distribution chain would add failure modes to an operation whose essential part already succeeded.

**The stretch, which turns the question around**

> The bound is still the validity of artifacts already cached, so a synchronous publish would not shorten it.

- The strongest form of the answer: a synchronous publish would not make revocation faster **in any way a verifier can observe**.
- Worked example: revoke at 09:00. A synchronous publish finishes at 09:00:30 instead of 09:05. A verifier holding a CRL with `nextUpdate` at 07:00 tomorrow will not fetch until then either way. The four and a half minutes saved are invisible to every verifier in the fleet.
- Which is the propagation bound from the capacity section, deployed as a rebuttal: the urgency is real, and the synchronous call is aimed at the wrong term of the sum.
- The general shape: **when someone asks for a faster path, check whether the latency they are attacking is the one that determines the outcome.**

---

> - Q2: The database is down and the responder cannot find the serial.
>   - Return `good`?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Never
>     - `good` comes only from a signed snapshot with a known generation, and an outage answers `tryLater`.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Responders serve from memory and never touch the ledger, so an outage changes freshness, not answers.

**What this question is testing**
Whether the fail-safe is a rule or a preference. The correct first word is "Never".

**The baseline**

- **`good` comes only from a signed snapshot with a known generation.** It is a positive claim requiring positive evidence, not the absence of a negative.
- Worked example of the failure this forbids: the snapshot failed to load, so the responder's revocation set is empty. A naive implementation reasons "this serial is not in my revocation set, therefore `good`" — and tells every client that every certificate is fine. An internal failure has become a fleet-wide revocation bypass, with correctly signed responses saying so.
- `tryLater` is the honest answer: I cannot answer. It hands the decision to the verifier, which is the party entitled to apply a fail policy.

**The stretch, which improves on the premise**

> Responders serve from memory and never touch the ledger, so an outage changes freshness, not answers.

- The question assumes the responder queries the database. In this design it does not.
- Worked example: the ledger is down for 30 minutes. Responders continue answering every request from their loaded snapshots, with valid signatures, throughout. What stops is the snapshot getting *newer*; what continues is answering.
- So `tryLater` applies only in the narrower case where a responder has no snapshot at all — a fresh instance that has not loaded, or a corrupted load. That is a much rarer situation, and correcting the premise is what makes this a stretch answer rather than a longer one.

---

> - Q3: A customer deletes the certificate resource.
>   - Is it revoked?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - No
>     - The public certificate stays valid until `notAfter` unless a revocation is on a signed CRL or OCSP response.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Deletion can trigger a revocation with reason `cessationOfOperation`, and the revocation row outlives the deleted resource.

**What this question is testing**
Whether you understand that a certificate is a signed statement loose in the world, not a row in your database.

**The baseline: "No"**

- Deleting the resource removes your record of it. The certificate itself is a signed document held by whoever holds it, and every verifier that sees it will accept it on the strength of the signature and the dates.
- Worked example: a customer deletes their certificate resource at 10:00. The private key is still on a server that is still serving TLS. Every client still connects successfully. Nothing about deletion reached any verifier.
- **"Unless a revocation is on a signed CRL or OCSP response"** — the only thing that changes a verifier's mind is a signed statement saying so. Absence from your database is not a statement; it is an absence.
- The general principle: **a certificate cannot be un-issued. It can only be revoked or allowed to expire.**

**The stretch: two additions**

1. **"Deletion can trigger a revocation with reason `cessationOfOperation`."**
   - Make the product behavior match the user's expectation, deliberately. `cessationOfOperation` is the standard RFC 5280 reason meaning the certificate is no longer needed rather than compromised.
   - Worked example of why the reason matters: a verifier or auditor seeing `cessationOfOperation` knows this was a lifecycle event, not an incident. `keyCompromise` would trigger a different response.
2. **"The revocation row outlives the deleted resource."**
   - The subtle and important half. The certificate resource is gone; the revocation record must remain until the certificate expires.
   - Worked example of why: the CRL builder needs the serial and its `not_after` to keep publishing the entry. If the revocation row were deleted with the resource, the serial would fall off the next CRL and the certificate would silently return to `good`.
   - This is exactly why `revocations` carries its own copy of `not_after` and takes no foreign key into issuance: the revocation ledger must be able to outlive everything it refers to.

---

> - Q4: Why not set CRL `nextUpdate` to five minutes and be done?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - A five-minute validity makes every publisher or CDN hiccup a fleet outage for hard-fail verifiers.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Shorten validity only where the staple already holds a local copy
>     - Keep `24 h` for appliances that fetch hourly at best.

**What this question is testing**
Whether you know which direction the `nextUpdate` lever cuts. The capacity section said shortening it is *the* lever; this question asks what shortening it costs.

**The baseline: short validity is fragile**

- Worked example: `nextUpdate` at five minutes means a verifier's evidence expires every five minutes. A publisher that misses one cycle, a CDN edge that fails to refresh, or a thirty-second network partition leaves hard-fail verifiers with expired evidence — and they reject every certificate from that issuer.
- The `crl_validity_s > publish_interval_s` constraint from the DDL exists precisely because of this. With a 24-hour validity and hourly publishing, 23 consecutive failures are survivable. With a 5-minute validity and 1-minute publishing, four failures cause an outage.
- **The general shape: validity is slack.** Shortening it tightens the compromise window and removes the buffer that absorbs operational failure. Both effects are real and they pull in opposite directions.

**The stretch: choose per cohort rather than globally**

> Shorten validity only where the staple already holds a local copy. Keep `24 h` for appliances that fetch hourly at best.

1. **Where stapling holds a local copy**, short validity is cheap. Worked example: a server refreshes hourly and keeps the response on disk. A short `nextUpdate` means it refreshes more often, and a missed refresh is visible to *the server*, which can alarm — rather than being discovered by clients failing.
2. **For appliances that fetch hourly at best**, 24 hours is the only workable number. Worked example: an appliance fetching once an hour with a 5-minute validity has valid evidence for 5 minutes out of every 60. It would be hard-failing 92% of the time.
- The general shape, recurring throughout this group: **a parameter that must be different for different cohorts belongs in a profile, not in a global setting.**

---

> - Q5 (premise reversal): Why have OCSP at all?
>   - Short-lived certificates make revocation unnecessary.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - For automated consumers that is the honest answer, and short TTL is the primary control for them.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - CRL stays for appliances that cannot renew hourly and stapling stays for public endpoints
>     - The deciding variable is whether every consumer can renew automatically.

**What a premise reversal is**
The interviewer attacks whether a component should exist. The right response is to concede what is true before explaining what remains.

**The baseline concedes immediately**

- **"For automated consumers that is the honest answer."** For a fleet that renews automatically on a short cycle, revocation infrastructure genuinely is close to unnecessary — expiry does the job, and it cannot be blocked or soft-failed.
- Conceding this is stronger than defending OCSP, because it is true and the interviewer knows it.

**The stretch names what remains, and then names the deciding variable**

- **CRL for appliances that cannot renew hourly.** Worked example: a hardware device requiring a manual certificate upload cannot have a 24-hour certificate. It needs a long lifetime, which means it needs revocation, which means it needs a CRL.
- **Stapling for public endpoints.** Worked example: a public-facing certificate may be issued with a longer lifetime for compatibility, and its clients are browsers that expect stapling.
- **"The deciding variable is whether every consumer can renew automatically."**
  - The sentence that makes the answer a decision rule rather than a list. One question determines the whole shape: can every consumer renew on a short cycle?
  - If yes — short certificates, minimal revocation infrastructure.
  - If no — the ones that cannot are exactly the ones needing revocation, and they set the requirement.
  - Worked example of why the exception dominates: a fleet of 10,000 services that all auto-renew plus 50 appliances that cannot means you still build and operate CRL publication, for 0.5% of the fleet. The minority with the constraint determines what gets built.

---

> - Q6: The attacker holds the stolen key and keeps stapling the last `good` response.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Correct, and that is why OCSP validity is the compromise window
>     - `8 h` is the stated bound.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Shorten the window for high-value profiles and pair it with short-lived leaves so the certificate itself expires inside the window.

**What this question is testing**
Whether you already knew. The correct first word is "Correct" — this is not an objection to rebut, it is a property of the design that should already have been stated.

**The baseline**

- **"That is why OCSP validity is the compromise window."** The attack is not a flaw to patch; it is the meaning of the `nextUpdate` number.
- **"8 h is the stated bound."** A specific figure, already in the design, already justified.
- Worked example one more time: the key is stolen at 10:00 and revoked at 10:05. The attacker holds a `good` response fetched at 09:30, valid to 17:30, and keeps stapling it. Nothing is forged and nothing is blocked. Every client sees correctly signed, unexpired evidence that the certificate is fine.

**The stretch: two changes that compose**

1. **Shorten the window for high-value profiles.** Worked example: a 1-hour `nextUpdate` for the profile covering payment endpoints reduces the exposure eightfold, at eight times the precompute cost for that subset only.
2. **"Pair it with short-lived leaves so the certificate itself expires inside the window."**
   - The elegant half. Worked example: a 4-hour certificate with a 1-hour response validity means that even if the attacker stapled a stale response indefinitely, the *certificate* expires within 4 hours and no verifier accepts it regardless of what status says.
   - The two controls compose: status freshness bounds how long the lie survives, and certificate lifetime bounds it independently. Whichever is shorter wins, and neither depends on the attacker cooperating.
   - The general shape: **when a control can be defeated by the party it constrains, add a second control that party does not influence.**

---

> - Q7: Can any key in the HSM sign the CRL?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Use the issuer's cRLSign-authorized key in the baseline.
>   - An alternative signer needs correctly encoded indirect-CRL scope and verifier support, not merely a certificate with cRLSign.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - A dedicated CRL-signing key limits use of the issuing key and rotates with overlap like the responder key.

**What this question is testing**
Whether you know that "the key has the right Key Usage" is not sufficient for a CRL signed by something other than the issuer.

**The baseline, and the correction that follows it**

- Baseline: the issuer's own `cRLSign`-authorized key.
- **"An alternative signer needs correctly encoded indirect-CRL scope and verifier support, not merely a certificate with cRLSign."**
  - The misconception: give another key a certificate with `cRLSign` Key Usage and it can sign this issuer's CRLs. It cannot — not in a way verifiers will accept.
  - An **indirect CRL** requires more than a permitted key. It needs the Issuing Distribution Point extension marking the CRL as indirect, and each entry needs a certificate-issuer extension naming which CA that serial belongs to. Without those, a verifier has a CRL signed by a key that is not the issuer and no way to associate it with the certificates it covers.
  - Worked example of the failure: an operator configures a separate signing key with `cRLSign` and publishes. Verifiers fetch a CRL whose signer does not match the issuer of the certificates being checked, cannot establish the relationship, and either reject the CRL or ignore its contents. Hard-fail verifiers reject every certificate from that issuer.
  - **And "verifier support"** is the second half: indirect CRLs are legitimate and implementation support is uneven, the same caveat as cross-signing and delta CRLs.

**The stretch: a dedicated CRL-signing key, with the full cost**

- **"Limits use of the issuing key"** is the benefit. Worked example: the CA's key is used only for issuance, and a separate key handles CRLs, so the highest-value key operates on fewer paths.
- **"Rotates with overlap like the responder key"** is the honest cost: a dedicated signer becomes another key with its own certificate, its own expiry, its own rotation schedule, and its own overlap requirement. It is the responder-key problem again, in a second place.
- Which is why the baseline does not do it: the benefit is real and modest, and the added lifecycle is not free.

---

> - Q8: An intermediate is compromised and ten thousand leaves must go.
>   - Where is the intermediate revoked?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - On its parent's CRL, so the root's CRL path must exist and be fetchable
>     - Leaves go on the intermediate's own CRL in a few coalesced publishes.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Replace the compromised issuer through an uncompromised trust path and verify adoption.
>   - Cross-signing by the compromised authority does not restore trust.

**What this question is testing**
Whether you know that revocation happens on the *parent's* list, and that this implies the root must have a working CRL path — which many designs never exercise.

**The baseline: two lists, two levels**

1. **The intermediate is revoked on its parent's CRL.** A CA's revocation is published by whoever signed it, which for an intermediate is the root.
   - **"So the root's CRL path must exist and be fetchable."** This is the sting.
   - Worked example of the problem: a root CA signs one or two intermediates in its entire life and revokes nothing. Its CRL is empty, published rarely if at all, and its CDP may point at a URL nobody has tested since setup. Then an intermediate is compromised and that untested path is suddenly the critical path of an emergency.
   - Which is a concrete argument for publishing and monitoring root CRLs even when they are permanently empty — the daily rebuild in `s2.01` exists partly for this.
2. **The leaves go on the intermediate's own CRL, in a few coalesced publishes.**
   - Worked example: 10,000 leaf revocations at 3 per second with a 60-second debounce is about 55 minutes of ledger writes collapsing into roughly 55 CRL builds. The list grows by 10,000 entries — about 0.5 megabytes — which is well within the size budget.
   - Note both levels are needed. Revoking the intermediate handles verifiers that check the chain's intermediate; revoking the leaves handles verifiers that only check the leaf. Doing one and not the other leaves a population unprotected.

**The stretch, and the standalone warning**

- **"Replace the compromised issuer through an uncompromised trust path and verify adoption."** Reissuing under a different intermediate, and then confirming from telemetry that consumers actually moved — the adoption discipline from `s2.02`.
- **"Cross-signing by the compromised authority does not restore trust."**
  - Placed outside both levels because it applies to the whole answer.
  - Worked example: the instinct is to have the compromised intermediate cross-sign its replacement so clients transition smoothly. But an attacker holding that key can produce the same signature for an intermediate of their choosing. A signature from a compromised key proves nothing to anyone who knows it is compromised.
  - The general principle, shared with root compromise in `s2.01`: **authenticity flows downward from a trusted anchor, and a compromised authority cannot vouch for its own successor.**

> - Validation scenarios
>   - Same revocation submitted twice with the same key.
>   - Two revocations for one issuer committed out of timestamp order.
>   - Publisher crash after object write and before the snapshot row.
>   - Two publishers racing for the same issuer.
>   - A new CRL that would drop an unexpired serial.
>   - OCSP request for a serial the issuer never issued.
>   - Responder snapshot unavailable during a store outage.
>   - Responder certificate within `24 h` of expiry.
>   - CDN purge failure with a `5 min` `max-age`.
>   - Control-plane outage beyond the 8 h staple lifetime: strict clients reject unless another still-valid acceptable status source exists.

**What this list is**
Ten tests, each mapping to a claim made earlier. Every design claim has a scenario that would falsify it.

1. **Same revocation submitted twice with the same key.** Tests idempotency. The assertion is one revocation row, one generation, and the original `202` replayed.
2. **Two revocations for one issuer committed out of timestamp order.**
   - Tests the consistent-prefix claim directly. Worked example of running it: make transaction A start first and commit second. The assertion is that generations reflect *commit* order, so no CRL built through generation `g` can later be found to have missed something numbered `g` or below.
3. **Publisher crash after object write and before the snapshot row.**
   - The unreferenced-artifact case. The assertion is that the pointer did not move, the previous CRL still serves, and a rerun at the same `crl_number` succeeds idempotently.
4. **Two publishers racing for the same issuer.**
   - Tests the fence and the two ordering preconditions. The assertion is that exactly one commits and the loser's stale-fence commit is refused, leaving litter rather than a regressed pointer.
5. **A new CRL that would drop an unexpired serial.**
   - Tests the additive-diff guard. Worked example: delete a revocation row and trigger a build. The assertion is that publication is *blocked* — not that a warning is logged.
6. **OCSP request for a serial the issuer never issued.**
   - Tests the strengthened `good` rule. The assertion is `unknown`, not `good`. An implementation that only tracks revocations fails this test and passes every other one.
7. **Responder snapshot unavailable during a store outage.** Tests the fail-safe. The assertion is `tryLater`, never `good`.
8. **Responder certificate within 24 h of expiry.**
   - Tests the highest-radius failure's prevention. The assertion is that rotation has already happened — auto-rotation at 50% of a 30-day life means reaching 24 hours from expiry is itself a failure of the rotation path.
9. **CDN purge failure with a 5-minute `max-age`.**
   - Tests that staleness is bounded even when the purge does not work. The assertion is that the canary ETag check detects the divergence and that the edge self-corrects within 5 minutes.
10. **Control-plane outage beyond the 8 h staple lifetime.**
    - The scenario with its own stated expectation: **"strict clients reject unless another still-valid acceptable status source exists."**
    - Worked example: the outage runs 10 hours. Stapled responses expired at hour 8. A strict client with a still-valid CRL fetched before the outage keeps working; one with no other source fails closed.
    - The point of running it is to confirm the failure is the **declared** one rather than something discovered. A strict client rejecting after 8 hours is correct behavior; a strict client silently accepting an expired staple would be a serious bug that nothing else in the test list would catch.

**The shape worth copying**
Every scenario names an observable outcome — a specific status, a blocked publish, a refused commit — rather than "it should work". That is what turns the list into a test plan and lets you answer "how would you know?" for any claim in the design.

## Recall card

Five one-liners and a four-sentence closing script, memorized rather than improvised.

---

> - "Revocation is an ordered generation per issuer; a CRL is a signed snapshot through one generation."

**What this says in plain words**
Two definitions in one sentence, and together they make every published artifact a precise claim.

**Why it matters**

- **"Ordered generation per issuer"** — each revocation gets a number, allocated under the issuer's row lock, so the sequence has no gaps and no ties.
- **"A signed snapshot through one generation"** — a CRL is not "the revocations as of roughly now", it is exactly everything numbered `g` or below.
- Worked example of what the alternative loses: a CRL built as "everything revoked before 09:00" can be silently incomplete, because a transaction that started at 08:59:58 may commit at 09:00:01 with an earlier timestamp. Nothing detects it afterwards, because the claim cannot be checked.
- The transferable idea: **a monotonic counter allocated at commit gives a boundary you can name; a timestamp read before commit does not.**

---

> - "Publish, verify, then advance the pointer; never the other way round."

**What this says in plain words**
Three steps in a fixed order, and the order is the entire safety argument.

**Why it matters**

- The pointer is what verifiers follow. It must never name an artifact that has not been checked.
- Worked example of the sequence working: the publisher writes CRL 48 to its immutable key, re-downloads it, finds a truncated upload that fails signature verification, and stops. The pointer still names CRL 47, which is valid and serving. Nobody noticed.
- Worked example of the reverse: advance the pointer and then verify. For the seconds or minutes between, every verifier fetching gets a corrupt CRL — and for a hard-fail verifier that means rejecting every certificate from that issuer.
- The bonus property worth adding if asked: **rollback is the pointer staying where it is.** There is no rollback procedure to execute under pressure, because a failed publish simply does nothing.

---

> - "A store outage answers `tryLater`; `good` only ever comes from a signed snapshot."

**What this says in plain words**
The fail-safe, stated as an absolute.

**Why it matters**

- `good` is a positive claim and requires positive evidence. The absence of a serial from an empty set is not evidence.
- Worked example of the failure this forbids: the snapshot fails to load, so the revocation set is empty, so a naive responder answers `good` for everything. An internal failure becomes a fleet-wide revocation bypass, delivered with valid signatures.
- The generalization to say alongside it: **nothing in this system retries its way to `good`.** Not the responder, not the publisher, not the verifier. A positive answer comes from data, never from exhaustion.

---

> - "The propagation bound is the validity of what verifiers already hold, not how fast we publish."

**What this says in plain words**
The single most useful sentence in the file. Revocation speed is not a property of your publisher.

**Why it matters**

- Worked example with the numbers: 5 minutes of publish lag plus 5 minutes of CDN age plus up to 24 hours of held-artifact validity. The publisher contributes 0.7% of the bound.
- Worked example of the wrong conclusion it prevents: an operator reports "we revoked it and purged the CDN within five minutes". True, and it changes nothing for any verifier holding a signed artifact that has not yet expired — which is most of them.
- The lever it points at: shorten `nextUpdate`, and accept that doing so removes the slack that absorbs publisher and CDN failures. That trade is the design's central tension, and this sentence is where it becomes visible.

---

> - "Short-lived certificates are the honest control for soft-fail clients; CRL and stapled OCSP cover the rest."

**What this says in plain words**
For verifiers that will not check, expiry is the only mechanism that still functions. For the rest, the two publication channels.

**Why it matters**

- **"Honest"** is the load-bearing word. Worked example of the dishonest position: claiming a bounded revocation time for a fleet in which a third of verifiers never fetch anything. For those, revocation is theater — you can revoke, publish perfectly, purge every cache, and the effect is exactly zero.
- Expiry cannot be blocked, cached around, or soft-failed. It is the one control that does not require the verifier's cooperation.
- The clause after the semicolon keeps the answer balanced: CRL and stapled OCSP are not abandoned, they cover the population that does check.

---

> - Wrap-up script
>   - "The design is an append-only revocation ledger with a per-issuer generation, feeding signed CRL snapshots on object storage plus CDN and precomputed OCSP responses served from memory, with every key in the HSM."

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence one: the whole architecture in four clauses — one source of truth, two publication channels, and the custody rule.

**Why it matters**

- **"Append-only ... per-issuer generation"** — the ledger and its ordering.
- **"Signed CRL snapshots on object storage plus CDN"** — the offline-verifiable channel.
- **"Precomputed OCSP responses served from memory"** — the fresh channel, with "from memory" naming why it survives a database outage.
- **"Every key in the HSM"** — both the CRL key and the responder key, neither of which exists in service memory.
- The shape to convey: one truth, two representations. They can differ in *how recently* they were built and never in *what* was revoked.

---

> -   - "The key invariant is publish-then-advance: a snapshot is written and verified before the pointer moves, and a new CRL may only drop expired serials."

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence two: two invariants, one about publication and one about contents.

**Why it matters**

- **Publish-then-advance** protects against publishing something broken.
- **"A new CRL may only drop expired serials"** protects against publishing something that silently un-revokes.
- Worked example of the second: a migration bug deletes 400 revocation rows. The next build produces a CRL missing 400 serials the previous one contained. The additive-diff guard compares, finds removals the expiry rule does not justify, and blocks publication.
- The two together cover both failure directions: an artifact that is *wrong*, and an artifact that is *correct-looking and too small*. The second is the more dangerous one, because nothing about it looks broken.

---

> -   - "The main tradeoff is validity versus availability: a shorter `nextUpdate` bounds a compromise faster but turns every freshness failure into a hard-fail outage."

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence three: the one dial, and the fact that it cuts both ways.

- **Bought by shortening:** a tighter compromise window. Worked example: dropping OCSP validity from 8 hours to 1 hour cuts an attacker's stapling window eightfold.
- **Paid by shortening:** every publisher hiccup, CDN failure, and HSM throttle becomes an outage for hard-fail verifiers. Worked example: a 24-hour CRL survives 23 consecutive hourly publish failures; a 5-minute CRL survives four.
- **"Validity is slack"** is the compact way to say it. The same number is the exposure window and the operational buffer, and there is no setting that is good at both.
- Which is why the resolution is per cohort rather than global: short where a staple holds a local copy, long where an appliance fetches hourly at best.

---

> -   - "My weakest assumption is that verifiers actually fetch before `nextUpdate`; I would validate fetch telemetry per consumer cohort before promising any propagation bound."

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence four: the flaw, and one specific action before making any promise.

**Why it matters**

- Every number in the design assumes verifiers fetch. For a real population they do not.
- Worked example: a wallet-based client configured without revocation checking accepts a revoked certificate until it expires, no matter what is published. Its bound is the certificate TTL and nothing else.
- **"Before promising any propagation bound"** is the right sequencing. A bound quoted without knowing who fetches is a claim about the mechanism, not about reality.
- **Fetch telemetry per consumer cohort from CDN logs** is the concrete action, and it is well chosen: you cannot instrument a verifier you do not control, but you can see whether it ever fetched. A cohort with zero fetches in 30 days has no revocation coverage, and that is a finding you can act on.
- Ending on "measure before you promise" is a good last impression: it says the weakness was not merely identified but given a cheap, immediate test.

|---|---|---|
| Generation | A per-issuer number allocated at commit under a row lock. | Makes a snapshot a precise, checkable claim. |
| Consistent prefix | "Everything through generation `g`". | A timestamp cutoff cannot promise this. |
| Publish-then-advance | Writing and verifying before the pointer names an artifact. | The pointer never names something unchecked. |
| Rollback by inaction | A failed publish leaving the pointer where it was. | No procedure to execute under pressure. |
| `tryLater` | "I cannot answer right now." | The only safe reply when the snapshot is missing. |
| Positive evidence for `good` | Requiring a signed snapshot, not an absent negative. | An empty set is not proof of anything. |

| Propagation bound | How long a revoked certificate can still be accepted. | Dominated by held-artifact validity, not publisher speed. |

> - [RFC 5280](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5280): CRL profile, `thisUpdate` / `nextUpdate`, CRL Number, `cRLSign`, reason codes, and the rule that an entry stays until one scheduled CRL after expiry (§3.3, §5).

**What this is**
The standard defining the certificate and CRL profile the whole internet uses.

**Which claims it backs**
Five, and the last one is the most valuable citation in the file.

- **The CRL profile** — the structure of the artifact itself.
- **`thisUpdate` / `nextUpdate`** — the two timestamps that define a CRL's validity window, and therefore the propagation bound.
- **CRL Number** — the monotonic identifier the publish procedure requires to increase.
- **`cRLSign`** — the Key Usage permitting a key to sign revocation lists.
- **Reason codes** — the `CRLReason` values, including `keyCompromise` and `cessationOfOperation`.
- **"The rule that an entry stays until one scheduled CRL after expiry."**
  - Worked example of why citing this specifically is worth a section reference: the removal rule in the ledger section looked like an unusually cautious invention. It is not — it is what the standard requires, and the design's contribution is only the observation that a fixed one-interval grace cannot satisfy it through a publisher outage.
  - Being able to say "that is RFC 5280 §5, not my preference" turns a debatable design choice into a compliance requirement.

> - [RFC 6960](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6960): OCSP `CertID`, `good` / `revoked` / `unknown`, `tryLater`, delegated responder with `id-kp-OCSPSigning`, `id-pkix-ocsp-nocheck`, nonce extension.

**What this is**
The OCSP standard.

**Which claims it backs**
Essentially the whole responder section: the request structure, the four statuses, the delegated responder and its Extended Key Usage, the `nocheck` extension, and the nonce extension.

**The one thing to note about what it does *not* back**
The strengthened `good` rule — requiring issued-set membership as well as absence from the revocation set — is *stricter* than the RFC, and the source says so explicitly in the status-semantics bullet. Worked example of why that flag matters: a candidate claiming "the RFC requires checking the issued set" would be wrong. Saying "the RFC's `good` means not-revoked; I am proposing a stronger rule because not-revoked alone would vouch for serials that were never issued" is accurate and is the better answer.

---

> - [RFC 5019](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5019): lightweight OCSP profile
>   - `GET` requests, precomputed responses, HTTP caching bounded by `nextUpdate`.

**What this is**
The lightweight OCSP profile, written for exactly this deployment shape: high volume, precomputed responses, HTTP caching.

**Which claims it backs**
Three, and together they are the whole reason the read path scales.

- **`GET` requests** with the request base64url-encoded into the path, so responses are cacheable by ordinary HTTP infrastructure.
- **Precomputed responses** — the profile explicitly anticipates responders that sign in advance.
- **HTTP caching bounded by `nextUpdate`** — the rule that a cached response must not outlive its own validity.

**Why this citation carries weight**
Worked example: ignoring nonces and serving cached responses could be presented as a shortcut. Citing RFC 5019 shows it is a standardized profile designed for this case, which moves it from "we cut a corner" to "we implement the profile intended for high-volume responders".

> - [RFC 8954](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8954): OCSP nonce update
>   - Why nonce-requiring clients cannot use cached responses.

**What this is**
The update to the OCSP nonce extension.

**Which claim it backs**
The one that matters: **nonce-requiring clients cannot use cached responses.** This is not an implementation limitation to work around — it is inherent, and the standard says so.

**Why it is cited separately rather than folded into RFC 6960**
Because it is the specific authority for the nonce-policy bullet's most consequential line: that redirecting a nonce-requiring client to a non-nonce response and calling it equivalent is wrong. Worked example of the exchange it settles: "could you just include a nonce in the precomputed response?" — no, and here is the document explaining why the two are incompatible by construction.

---

> - [RFC 6066](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6066) and [RFC 7633](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7633): TLS `status_request` (stapling) and the TLS Feature extension (must-staple).

**What these are**
Two standards cited together because they are the two halves of stapling.

- **RFC 6066** defines `status_request`, the TLS extension that carries a stapled OCSP response in the handshake. This is the mechanism.
- **RFC 7633** defines the TLS Feature extension, placed in a *certificate*, which is what must-staple is. This is the enforcement.

**Why the pairing is worth noticing**
Worked example of the division: stapling without must-staple is an optimization — clients benefit when a staple is present and fall back when it is not. Must-staple turns absence into a hard failure. One is a performance and privacy improvement; the other is a security control with a rollback cost measured in reissued certificates. Citing them separately keeps that distinction visible.

**What these are**
Three siblings, each owning something this file uses without re-deriving.

1. **`s2.01` — the issuer hierarchy and serial namespace.**
   - The serial namespace is the critical one. Worked example: this design revokes *by serial*, and a CRL identifies certificates by serial alone. That only works because `s2.01` guarantees serial uniqueness within an issuer. If two certificates from one issuer shared a serial, revoking one would revoke both — and this file's entire addressing model would be unsound.
2. **`s2.02` — old-key CRL signing after rotation, and intermediate compromise remediation.**
   - **Old-key CRL signing** is the direct connection: after a CA rotates, the old key stays authorized for CRL signing until its last leaf expires. That is `s2.02`'s retirement tail and this file's signing requirement, describing the same thing from two sides.
   - **Intermediate compromise remediation** is what pushback Q8 points at: revoking the intermediate is this file's job, and replacing it through an uncompromised trust path is `s2.02`'s.
3. **`s3.01` — the `202` work-request contract.**
   - Specified once and reused by both revocation and forced publish, which is why this file's API table can list `GET /v1/operations/{id}` without defining its state machine.

**Why the sibling set matters**
This file sits in the middle of the `s2` group: `s2.01` makes certificates, `s2.02` distributes and rotates them, and this one un-makes them. Each pointer marks a boundary where another file owns the detail, which is what lets this one stay at a consistent depth instead of expanding wherever the subject got interesting.
