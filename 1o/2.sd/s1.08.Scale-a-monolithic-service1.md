## Interview framing

The level, the evidence, why the problem is hard, and the sentences to open with.

---

> - Target: OCI IC3 ownership of a bounded platform component.
>   - `IC3 baseline` is what must land in every phase.
>   - `IC4 stretch` adds architecture changes under stronger constraints.
>   - These are preparation labels, not Oracle's official rubric.

**What this says in plain words**
You answer as an engineer who owns one clearly-bordered piece of infrastructure, including its failures and its pager. `IC3 baseline` is the floor; `IC4 stretch` is depth added when pushed. Both labels are a study device, not a company grading scale.

**Why it matters**

1. **Bounded controls scope.** Inside the box: diagnosing the bottleneck, extracting one capability, and migrating safely. Outside: reorganizing teams, rewriting the framework, or splitting every domain.
2. **The baseline is a checklist.** Skip the rollback gate and you dropped the item that makes the migration safe.
3. **The labels are yours.** Saying "this is the IC4 answer" claims knowledge of their rubric you do not have.

---

> - Evidence: retain the first-hand report attribution in [the bank](2.sd.md#1infra).
>   - The architecture below is a proposed interview answer.
>   - It does not claim to reproduce Oracle's implementation or an interviewer's accepted answer.

**What this says in plain words**
Someone reported being asked this in a real Oracle interview. The design here is this preparation's own answer, not a leaked design.

**Why it matters**
High confidence the question gets asked, no claim that this approach is what the interviewer wanted.

---

> - Why this is hard
>   - Naive: split the monolith into services along the team chart and put a gateway in front.

**What this says in plain words**
The obvious design: draw service boundaries where the organizational boundaries already are, and route between them with a gateway.

**Why it matters**
It is what most organizations actually do, and the team chart is not a technical boundary.

- Worked example of the mismatch: three teams own ordering, billing, and reporting. Splitting along those lines produces three services that all read and write the same orders table, because the domain does not divide the way the organization does. The split created network hops without creating independence.
- Naming it first shows you know the common answer and are choosing differently on purpose.

---

> -   - First break: the database was the bottleneck, so the same queries now arrive over more network hops with no shared transaction, and p99 gets worse.

**What this says in plain words**
Three consequences of splitting without diagnosing, and they compound.

1. **The same queries still run.** The database's load is unchanged, because the work did not disappear; it moved to a different process.
2. **More network hops.** Worked example: an operation that was three in-process method calls becomes three network calls, each adding a millisecond or two at the median and much more at the tail.
3. **No shared transaction.**
   - Worked example: previously one transaction wrote an order and decremented inventory atomically. Split across two services, each writes its own database in its own transaction. A failure between them leaves an order with no inventory decrement, and repairing that requires machinery that did not exist before.
4. **So p99 gets worse.** The split cost latency, cost atomicity, and relieved nothing.

**Why it matters**
This is the specific failure the whole question is about: a reorganization presented as a performance fix, which makes performance worse.

---

> -   - Second break: write ownership moves during migration, both executors publish results for the same job, and rollback is impossible once only the new store holds recent writes.

**What this says in plain words**
Three failures of the migration itself.

1. **Write ownership moves mid-migration.**
   - Worked example: during a cutover window, some writes go to the monolith and some to the new service. Two authoritative writers exist for the same entity, and their states diverge with no rule for merging them.
2. **Both executors publish for the same job.**
   - Worked example: the old executor and the new worker each run report job 8891. Both finish and both write a result. Which one the client sees depends on timing, and the two may differ.
3. **Rollback becomes impossible once only the new store has recent writes.**
   - The sharpest of the three. Worked example: the new service has been authoritative for six hours and has taken 40,000 writes the old store never saw. Routing traffic back to the monolith now means serving data that is six hours stale, and the 40,000 writes are stranded. The rollback lever exists and pulling it causes a different outage.

---

> -   - Minimum primitives
> -     - A measured profile
> -     - One routing seam
> -     - A transactional outbox
> -     - A single authoritative writer per entity
> -     - A staged cutover with a rollback gate.

**What this says in plain words**
Five parts you cannot drop.

1. **A measured profile.** Evidence naming the resource that is actually exhausted.
2. **One routing seam.** A single place where traffic can be directed to the old or new implementation.
3. **A transactional outbox.** A way to commit an intent and a business change together, so the intent survives even if publishing fails.
4. **A single authoritative writer per entity.** At any instant, exactly one component may write a given entity.
5. **A staged cutover with a rollback gate.** Phases, each with a defined condition for proceeding and a defined way back.

**Why it matters**
The first is listed first for a reason: it is the one most often skipped, and without it the other four are applied to the wrong problem.

---

> - 🗣 “A process split changes failure and ownership boundaries; it does not remove a bottleneck you have not measured.”

**What this says in plain words**
A quotable one-liner; the 🗣 marker means say it roughly verbatim. Two claims:

1. What splitting actually changes: where failures are contained and who owns what.
2. What it does not change: a bottleneck nobody measured.

**Why it matters**
It reframes the question in ten seconds, and it is a genuinely contrarian opening for a question that seems to be asking for microservices.

- Worked example of the reframing in use: an interviewer asks how to scale a monolith. A candidate who immediately starts drawing services has accepted a premise. A candidate who says this sentence has established that the first deliverable is a diagnosis, and the interviewer now has to say what the bottleneck is, which is information you need anyway.

---

> - Opening script
>   - “I will start with diagnosis, because a split that does not target the measured bottleneck only adds hops.”

**What this says in plain words**
The first sentence, and it is a refusal to start designing.

**Why it matters**
It is a risk worth taking. Worked example of the risk: an interviewer wanting to see an architecture may find this evasive. Worked example of the payoff: it is the correct engineering position, and it sets up every later decision as targeted rather than speculative. The phrasing, "only adds hops", gives the reason immediately so it does not read as stalling.

---

> -   - “I assume a transactional API with expensive report generation running in the request process against one relational database.”

**What this says in plain words**
The assumed system in one sentence: a transactional API, expensive reports running inline, one relational database.

**Why it matters**
The prompt names no domain, so you supply one. Worked example of why: "scale a monolith" has no answer without knowing what it does. Choosing a concrete, common shape lets everything downstream be specific, and stating it as an assumption invites correction.

---

> -   - “The first extraction is derived execution, the reports, because it buys independent compute without splitting transactional ownership.”

**What this says in plain words**
Extract the report computation first, with the reason attached: it gains independent scaling without dividing who owns the data.

**Why it matters**
This is the central design choice of the whole answer.

- Worked example of the distinction: reports *read* orders and *write* a result file. They do not own orders. Moving that computation elsewhere changes where CPU is spent and changes nothing about who may write an order.
- Worked example of the contrast: extracting order management itself would move write ownership, which brings distributed transactions, dual-write windows, and the rollback problem from the second break.
- The general rule worth stating: move computation before moving ownership, because computation can be moved back and ownership cannot.

---

> -   - “The contract is a `202` job API backed by a transactional outbox, so accepted work survives a queue outage.”

**What this says in plain words**
Report submission returns `202`, meaning accepted, and the job plus an outbox event are committed together, so an accepted job is not lost if the queue is unavailable.

**Why it matters**
Worked example: the queue is down for 20 minutes. Submissions still succeed, because they only need the database. The outbox accumulates rows, and the relay publishes them when the queue returns. The queue's outage costs report latency, not accepted work.

---

> -   - “The deep dive is the staged migration and the exact point after which rollback needs data movement.”

**What this says in plain words**
The deep dive is the migration, and specifically identifying the moment when rolling back stops being free.

**Why it matters**
Worked example of why the exact point matters: while ownership stays in the monolith, rollback is a routing change taking seconds. The moment the new service accepts its first authoritative write, rollback requires moving that data back. Those are two entirely different operations, and knowing which side of the line you are on is the difference between a safe migration and a trapped one.

---

> -   - “I will close with failure modes, admission control, and when full domain extraction is actually justified.”

**What this says in plain words**
Announces the ending: failures, how overload is bounded, and the conditions under which the bigger split is warranted.

**Why it matters**
The third item concedes that full extraction is sometimes right, which keeps the answer from reading as reflexive conservatism. Worked example of the condition: when the boundary is stable and the benefit is independent evolution rather than performance, the cost may be justified.

## Requirements — IC3 baseline

The numbers warning, the clarifying table, functional scope, the assumed system, the numeric target table, invariants, and non-goals.

---

> - Numerical targets are interview assumptions unless an adjacent primary source states otherwise.

**What this says in plain words**
Every number here was chosen to make the design concrete. None came from Oracle unless a link says so.

**Why it matters**
Worked example: at 5,000 requests per second with reports consuming the entire interactive CPU budget, extraction is justified. At 50 requests per second, the reports are an inconvenience and a thread-pool limit would fix it.

---

> | Question | Assumed answer | Why it changes the design |
> |---|---|---|
> | What is the symptom? | Interactive p99 breaches `300 ms` during report-heavy periods | Points at coupling between report CPU and request threads, not raw throughput |
> | Where is the bottleneck? | Request-process CPU from reports; database is near but under capacity | Extraction targets compute; if the database were saturated, workers would only move the problem |
> | Which domain? | Order management with on-demand reports; the prompt names none | Gives a concrete authoritative entity and a derived output to separate |
> | Must data ownership split? | No; one relational database stays the transactional owner | Rollback stays a routing change; no distributed transactions |
> | Multi-tenant? | Yes; tenant context is explicit on every request and job | Idempotency keys, quotas, and authorization are tenant-scoped |
> | Deployment coupling? | Reports and interactive code ship in one binary today | Extraction also buys independent deploys, which counts in the decision |
> | External side effects in reports? | None beyond writing a result object | Duplicate execution is wasteful, not harmful; publication decides the winner |

**What this says in plain words**
Seven questions, the assumed answer, and what each decides.

- **Row: What is the symptom?**
  - Not "it is slow", but a specific measurement: interactive p99 breaches 300 milliseconds, and only during report-heavy periods.
  - Worked example of why the qualifier matters: latency bad at all times suggests a general capacity problem. Latency bad only when reports run identifies the coupling directly, and the correlation is the diagnosis.
  - "not raw throughput" is the distinction: the system handles the request rate fine. It is the interference that hurts.
- **Row: Where is the bottleneck?**
  - Request-process CPU, with the database near but under capacity.
  - The third column is the most important sentence in the table: if the database were saturated, extraction would move the same queries to another process and relieve nothing.
- **Row: Which domain?**
  - Order management with on-demand reports, chosen because the prompt names none.
  - Worked example of why this choice is good: it gives a clear authoritative entity, the order, and a clear derived output, the report. Those two roles are exactly what the extraction argument needs.
- **Row: Must data ownership split?**
  - No, and this single answer removes distributed transactions and keeps rollback a routing change.
- **Row: Multi-tenant?**
  - Yes, with tenant context explicit on every request and job, which scopes idempotency keys, quotas, and authorization.
  - Worked example of why jobs need it explicitly: a job executes later, in a different process, possibly hours after submission. The tenant context has to travel with it, verified, rather than being inferred from an ambient session that no longer exists.
- **Row: Deployment coupling?**
  - Reports and interactive code ship together today, so extraction also buys independent deploys.
  - Worked example of the current cost: a one-line fix to report formatting requires deploying the entire application, including the transactional request path, with all the risk that carries. That is a real cost, separate from performance, and it counts in the decision.
- **Row: External side effects in reports?**
  - None beyond writing a result object, and this answer is what makes the whole design tractable.
  - Worked example of the difference it makes: if a report emailed customers, running it twice would email twice, which is harmful and irreversible. With no external effects, running twice merely wastes CPU, and the publication step picks one winner.

---

> - Functional scope
>   - Preserve the public synchronous API and its semantics.
>   - Submit reports as asynchronous jobs with a stable ID and a status resource.
>   - Execute reports in an isolated worker pool with bounded admission.
>   - Migrate execution in stages with parity checks and a defined rollback.
>   - Keep authorization and tenant context intact across the new seam.

**What this says in plain words**
Five capabilities, and the first and last are constraints rather than features.

1. **Preserve the public API and its semantics.**
   - Worked example of what this rules out: a redesign that requires every client to adopt a new interface. Clients are other teams and other companies, and coordinating their migration would take longer than the entire project.
2. **Reports become asynchronous jobs with a stable ID and a status resource.** This is a semantic change for report submission specifically, and it is the one change clients must absorb.
3. **Execute in an isolated pool with bounded admission.** Both words: isolated so it cannot take CPU from requests, and bounded so the backlog cannot grow without limit.
4. **Migrate in stages with parity checks and a defined rollback.**
5. **Keep authorization and tenant context intact across the seam.**
   - The one most often lost in an extraction. Worked example: authorization was enforced by a filter in the monolith's request pipeline. Report execution moves to a worker that never passes through that pipeline, so the check silently disappears and a worker will generate any report it is asked for.

---

> - Assumed system
>   - A transactional application with synchronous API traffic.
>   - Expensive report generation currently runs in the request process.
>   - One relational database owns business transactions.
>   - No requirement to split every domain into a service.

**What this says in plain words**
Four assumptions restated compactly so the interviewer can challenge any one in a single sentence.

**Why it matters**
The fourth is a scoping statement disguised as an assumption: the question asks which components to separate, and this establishes that the answer may be "one".

---

> | Requirement | Target | Enforced by |
> |---|---|---|
> | Peak interactive throughput | `5,000 requests/s` | Horizontal app tier behind the facade; bounded pools |
> | Interactive latency | p99 < `300 ms` | Reports removed from request threads; per-endpoint SLO alarms |
> | Report submission latency | p99 < `200 ms` | One transaction writes job plus outbox; no execution inline |
> | Report completion | p99 < `60 s` under admitted load | Worker pool sized above arrival rate; admission cap on backlog |
> | Interactive availability | `99.95%` | Queue and workers are off the interactive path; facade fails open to the monolith |
> | Migration data safety | 0 committed transactional writes lost from executor migration | Database ownership unchanged; compatible schema and fenced result publication |
> | Rollback readiness | Route new jobs back within 5 min in a rehearsed drill | Cached routing generation and fenced executor claims |
> | Static stability | 30 min routing-authority outage budget | Versioned cached assignments; no ownership changes until authority recovers |

**What this says in plain words**
Eight promises, each with a number and a mechanism.

- **Row: Interactive latency, p99 under 300 milliseconds.** Enforced by removing reports from request threads, which is the entire point of the extraction, plus per-endpoint alarms so a regression is attributed correctly.
- **Row: Report submission latency, p99 under 200 milliseconds.**
  - "no execution inline" is the mechanism stated as a prohibition. Submission writes two rows and returns; it never starts the report.
- **Row: Report completion, p99 under 60 seconds under admitted load.**
  - "under admitted load" is the defeater, and it is what makes the promise keepable. Worked example: if arrivals exceed the pool's service rate, completion time grows without bound, and no pool size fixes that. The promise holds for work that was admitted, and admission control is what makes that set bounded.
- **Row: Interactive availability, 99.95%.**
  - "facade fails open to the monolith" is the key mechanism. Worked example: the facade cannot reach the routing table or is otherwise degraded, so it sends traffic to the monolith, which is the path that worked before the migration started. The new component's failure mode is to behave as though it were not there.
- **Row: Migration data safety, zero committed transactional writes lost.**
  - The strongest row in the table, and it is achievable precisely because ownership does not move. Worked example: the monolith remains the only writer throughout, so no executor migration can lose a committed write, because no executor ever writes transactional data.
- **Row: Rollback readiness, within 5 minutes in a rehearsed drill.**
  - "rehearsed" is the operative word. Worked example of the alternative: a rollback procedure documented and never practised. During an incident someone discovers the cached routes take 10 minutes to propagate, or that an executor was never fenced. A drill converts a plan into a measured capability.
- **Row: Static stability, a 30-minute routing-authority outage budget.**
  - "no ownership changes until authority recovers" is the safety rule: during the outage the facade keeps routing as it was, and nobody may move a cohort, because the record of who owns what cannot be updated.

---

> - Invariants
>   - Exactly one authoritative writer owns a business entity at a time.
>   - A committed report job is eventually observable and executable.
>   - An event retry cannot repeat a non-idempotent business effect.
>   - An authorization rule does not disappear when a path moves to a new process.

**What this says in plain words**
Four statements true at every instant.

1. **Exactly one authoritative writer per entity.** The invariant the whole migration design protects.
2. **A committed job is eventually observable and executable.**
   - Worked example of the guarantee: the job row committed, so it exists. The outbox row committed in the same transaction, so the intent exists. Even if the queue is down for hours, the relay will publish it and a worker will run it. "Committed" means the work will happen, not that it happened.
3. **A retry cannot repeat a non-idempotent business effect.**
   - Worth reading carefully: it does not say retries are prevented. Retries are expected. It says the *effect* cannot repeat, which is what idempotent execution and conditional publication provide.
4. **An authorization rule does not disappear when a path moves.**
   - Worked example of the violation: the check lived in a request filter, and the new worker path does not pass through it. Nothing errors. The rule simply is not enforced any more, and no test notices because the tests exercise the old path.

---

> - Non-goals
>   - Splitting every bounded context into a service.
>   - Distributed transactions across services.
>   - Rewriting the application framework or storage engine.
>   - Multi-region active-active for the monolith.

**What this says in plain words**
Four exclusions.

1. **Splitting every bounded context.** A bounded context is one coherent area of the domain with its own model. The non-goal says the answer is not "one service per context".
2. **Distributed transactions across services.**
   - Worked example of what is excluded: a protocol making a write in service A and a write in service B commit or abort together. It is slow, it couples availability, and this design avoids needing it by not splitting ownership.
3. **Rewriting the framework or storage engine.** The change must fit inside the existing technology.
4. **Multi-region active-active.** A different and much larger problem.

**Why it matters**
The first is the one that protects the whole answer. Worked example: an interviewer asks why you are not proposing microservices. The prepared response is that splitting every context is an explicit non-goal, because the measured problem is compute contention in one capability, and the rest of the system has no measured problem to solve.

| Deployment coupling | Unrelated code shipping in one binary, so any change risks all of it. | A real cost of the monolith, separate from performance. |
| External side effect | An action outside the system, such as sending an email or charging a card. | Their absence is what makes duplicate execution merely wasteful. |
| Bounded admission | Refusing new work when the backlog exceeds what can be served in time. | Makes the completion-time promise keepable. |
| Fail open | Falling back to the previous working path when the new component cannot decide. | How the facade avoids becoming a new point of failure. |
| Fenced publication | Rejecting a result from an executor that no longer owns the job. | Stops two executors both publishing. |
| Rehearsed rollback | A rollback procedure that has actually been practised and timed. | Converts a documented plan into a measured capability. |
| Static stability | Continuing to operate correctly without the control plane, from cached state. | Bounded here at 30 minutes. |
| Authoritative writer invariant | Exactly one component may write a given entity at a time. | The property the migration design protects. |
| Idempotent effect | An effect that repeating does not change. | What makes an expected retry harmless. |
| Bounded context | One coherent area of a domain with its own model. | Splitting every one of them is an explicit non-goal. |
| Distributed transaction | A protocol making writes in separate systems commit or abort together. | Avoided entirely by not splitting ownership. |

## Capacity and consequence — IC3 baseline

Four estimates. Each ends in the decision it forces, and the last one is a decision about not proceeding.

---

> - CPU example
>   - `5,000 requests/s × 20 ms CPU/request = 100 CPU-seconds/s`.
>   - At a `60%` target utilization, budget about `167 effective cores` before resilience headroom.
>   - This is a workload estimate, not a hardware benchmark.
>   - If measured work is waiting on a database, extra application cores may not help.
>   - Decision: horizontal app scaling is justified only if the profile shows CPU-bound handlers
>     - Otherwise the money goes to the database or the query plan.

**What this says in plain words**

1. **100 CPU-seconds per second.** 5,000 requests × 20 milliseconds of CPU each.
   - The unit is worth pausing on: 100 CPU-seconds of work arriving every second means you need at least 100 cores fully busy to keep up.
2. **About 167 effective cores at 60% utilization.** 100 ÷ 0.6.
   - Worked example of why not 100% : a queueing system at full utilization has unbounded waiting time. At 60%, there is slack to absorb bursts and the latency tail stays reasonable. Running at 95% would meet the average throughput and blow the p99 target.
3. **A workload estimate, not a hardware benchmark.** It says how much CPU work arrives, not how many of any particular machine's cores that needs.
4. **If work is waiting on the database, more cores do not help.**
   - Worked example: a handler takes 200 milliseconds, of which 20 is CPU and 180 is waiting for a query. Doubling application cores lets you run more handlers concurrently, all of which wait on the same database. Throughput is unchanged and the database is now more contended.

**Why it matters — the decision**
Horizontal scaling is justified only if the profile shows CPU-bound handlers, and otherwise the money goes to the database.

- This is the file's thesis applied to the simplest case. The arithmetic produces a number, and the number is immediately made conditional on evidence nobody has produced yet.

---

> - Concurrency
>   - At `200 ms` average response time, Little's Law gives about `1,000 requests in flight`.
>   - Database connections need not equal request concurrency.
>   - Size the pool against database service time and capacity.
>   - Decision: cap the pool well below in-flight requests, for example `1,000` in flight against an order-of-magnitude `100` connections, and queue requests in the app tier with a deadline rather than at the database.

**What this says in plain words**

1. **About 1,000 requests in flight.** Little's Law states that the average number of items in a system equals the arrival rate times the average time each spends there: 5,000 per second × 0.2 seconds = 1,000.
2. **Connections need not equal concurrency.**
   - The instinct is that 1,000 concurrent requests need 1,000 connections. They do not, because a request spends only part of its time using the database.
   - Worked example: of the 200 milliseconds, perhaps 20 are spent in the database. So on average only 10% of in-flight requests hold a connection, which is about 100.
3. **Size the pool against database service time and capacity.**

**Why it matters — the decision**
Two parts, and the second is the one that shows operational experience.

- **Cap the pool well below in-flight requests.** 100 connections against 1,000 in-flight requests.
  - Worked example of oversizing: a pool of 1,000 connections against a database that performs best at 100 concurrent queries. At peak, 1,000 queries arrive simultaneously, the database thrashes on context switching and lock contention, and every query slows. Throughput falls as concurrency rises, which is the characteristic shape of an oversized pool.
- **Queue in the app tier with a deadline, not at the database.**
  - Worked example of the difference: with a small pool, excess requests wait in the application for a connection, where you control the wait, can apply a deadline, and can shed load. With a large pool they wait inside the database, where you control nothing, cannot apply a deadline, and cannot shed.
  - The principle: queue where you can manage the queue.

---

> - Report extraction
>   - Assume `1%` of requests launch a `2 s` CPU report.
>   - `50 reports/s × 2 s = 100 CPU-seconds/s` for reports alone.
>   - An asynchronous worker pool can isolate that load from interactive requests.
>   - If a pool processes only `40 reports/s`, backlog grows by `10/s` indefinitely.
>   - Queueing requires an admission limit, not just a broker.
>   - Decision: reports equal the entire interactive CPU budget, so they get their own pool sized above `50/s` with headroom, plus a per-tenant admission cap keyed to backlog age.

**What this says in plain words**
The estimate that justifies the whole design.

1. **50 reports per second.** 1% of 5,000.
2. **100 CPU-seconds per second for reports alone.** 50 × 2 seconds.
3. **Which is exactly the interactive CPU budget.**
   - Worked example of what this means in the monolith: the machine is doing 200 CPU-seconds of work per second, half of it reports, in one process, sharing threads. The reports are not a background nuisance; they are half the machine, and they are competing with the requests a user is waiting on.
4. **A pool at 40 reports per second grows backlog by 10 per second, indefinitely.**
   - Worked example of "indefinitely": after an hour the backlog is 36,000 reports. At 40 per second it would take 15 minutes to drain if arrivals stopped, and they do not. The backlog and the completion time both grow without limit.
5. **Queueing requires an admission limit, not just a broker.**
   - The sentence to remember. A queue does not fix an arrival rate above the service rate; it converts an immediate failure into an unbounded delay, which is usually worse because it is less visible.

**Why it matters — the decision**
A dedicated pool sized above 50 per second with headroom, plus a per-tenant admission cap keyed to backlog age.

- "keyed to backlog age" rather than depth. Worked example: a backlog of 500 jobs draining in 10 seconds is fine. A backlog of 100 jobs draining in 10 minutes is not. Age is directly comparable to the 60-second completion target; depth is not.
- "per-tenant" so one tenant's burst is capped before it consumes the whole pool.

---

> - Investigate first
>   - Endpoint traces.
>   - CPU profiles.
>   - Query plans and lock waits.
>   - Connection-pool wait time.
>   - Tail-latency correlation with background jobs.
>   - Decision: no extraction is proposed without a profile that names the resource
>     - The estimate above is what the profile must confirm.

**What this says in plain words**
Five measurements, then a decision that is really a precondition.

1. **Endpoint traces.** Which endpoints are slow, and where the time goes within each.
2. **CPU profiles.** Which code consumes CPU. Worked example of what this settles: whether report generation actually dominates, or whether it is, say, serialization across all endpoints.
3. **Query plans and lock waits.** Whether the database is slow because of a bad plan or because of contention, which have different fixes.
4. **Connection-pool wait time.**
   - The most diagnostic single metric here. Worked example: if requests spend 150 of their 200 milliseconds waiting for a connection, the bottleneck is pool size or database capacity, and no amount of report extraction changes it.
5. **Tail-latency correlation with background jobs.** The direct test of the stated symptom: does p99 rise when reports run?

**Why it matters — the decision**
"No extraction is proposed without a profile that names the resource", and the final clause is the honest one: the estimate is a hypothesis the profile must confirm.

- Worked example of the discipline in the room: you have just spent five minutes computing that reports equal the interactive CPU budget. Rather than treating that as established, you say it is what the profile must show, and that if the profile instead shows connection-pool waiting, the whole design changes.
- That reads as engineering judgement rather than hedging, because you produced the number first and then said what would falsify it.

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| CPU-second | One second of one core's full attention. | The unit that makes "100 CPU-seconds per second" mean 100 busy cores. |
| Effective cores | Cores available for useful work after utilization targets. | 167 at 60% utilization, not 100. |

## Architecture and flow — IC3 baseline

Diagram first, then the cheaper fixes to try before extracting, then each component with its mechanism and price, then the extraction boundary, then one job traced end to end.

---

> ```text
> Migration routing table (control plane) --cached routes--> facade
>
> Clients -> existing API / routing facade -> transactional monolith -> primary DB
>                         |                                        |
>                         |                               report job + outbox
>                         |                                        |
>                         +-> report status API             outbox relay
>                                                                  |
>                                                            durable queue
>                                                                  |
>                                                           report workers
>                                                                  |
>                                                         immutable results
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
One horizontal request path and one vertical job path, with configuration arriving from above.

**Reading the request path**

1. **Clients → existing API / routing facade.** The facade sits where the API already was, so clients see no change.
2. **→ transactional monolith → primary DB.** Unchanged. The monolith is still the authoritative writer.

**Reading the job path**

1. **Monolith → report job + outbox.** Both written in one database transaction.
2. **→ outbox relay → durable queue → report workers → immutable results.** Each step asynchronous.

**Reading what is not there**

- **There is no arrow from the facade to the workers.** The facade routes requests; jobs reach workers through the queue. The request path and the execution path are connected only through the database.
- **There is no arrow from the workers back to the primary database for writes.** Workers read it and write results elsewhere.

**Why it matters**
The shape encodes the central claim: the interactive path is exactly what it was, plus one hop, and the report path hangs off the side. A failure anywhere below the outbox costs report freshness and touches nothing a user is waiting on.

---

> - Baseline improvements before extraction
>   - Remove measured inefficient queries.
>   - Add justified indexes.
>   - Bound connection pools and request queues.
>   - Externalize session state if it prevents horizontal scaling.
>   - Cache only reads with an explicit staleness contract.

**What this says in plain words**
Five cheaper things to do first, each qualified.

1. **Remove *measured* inefficient queries.** Not suspected ones.
2. **Add *justified* indexes.** Worked example of why the qualifier: every index speeds some reads and slows every write to that table, and consumes space. An index added on suspicion is a permanent cost against a hypothetical benefit.
3. **Bound connection pools and request queues.** The concurrency decision from the estimates, applied without any architectural change.
4. **Externalize session state *if* it prevents horizontal scaling.**
   - Worked example: sessions held in each instance's memory mean a user must return to the same instance, so instances cannot be added or removed freely. Moving session state to a shared store makes the tier genuinely stateless. If sessions are already external, this step is unnecessary.
5. **Cache only reads, with an explicit staleness contract.**
   - Two constraints. Worked example of the second: a cache with a 60-second lifetime means users may see data 60 seconds old. That has to be stated and accepted, not introduced silently because it improved a graph.

**Why it matters**
Listing these before the architecture is a credibility move: several of them might solve the problem entirely, and they cost days rather than months.

---

> - Routing facade owns the seam.
>   - Mechanism: stateless routing by tenant cohort from a cached assignment
>     - Public API unchanged.
>   - Cost: one more hop on every request and a component that must fail open to the monolith.

**What this says in plain words**
The facade routes by tenant cohort using cached assignments, and it is stateless.

**Why it matters**
Both halves of the cost are real and both are managed.

- **One more hop on every request.** Worked example: a stateless routing decision costs perhaps a millisecond, against a 300-millisecond target. Acceptable, and it is a real addition to every request, not only the ones being migrated.
- **Must fail open to the monolith.**
  - Worked example: the facade cannot reach the routing table and has no cached assignment for a tenant. Failing closed would reject the request. Failing open sends it to the monolith, which is where it would have gone before any of this existed.
  - The principle: a component introduced to enable a migration must never be able to cause an outage by itself, and the way to guarantee that is for its failure mode to be the pre-migration behavior.

---

> - Migration routing table (control plane) owns cohort assignments.
>   - Mechanism: small store of `(tenant, context, cohort) → owner, epoch`
>     - The facade polls and caches last-known-good.
>   - Cost: a route change is visible only after the poll interval
>     - Static stability is bought with bounded staleness.

**What this says in plain words**
A small table mapping a tenant, a context, and a cohort to an owner and an epoch. The facade polls it and caches the last version that parsed.

**Why it matters**
The epoch is the part that matters most, and its use appears later.

- Worked example of the mapping: tenant `acme`, context `reports`, cohort `canary-1` maps to owner `report-workers` at epoch 12. Change the owner back to `monolith` and the epoch becomes 13. That single increment is what invalidates every claim held by the previous owner.
- The cost is the poll interval. Worked example: a 30-second poll means a rollback takes up to 30 seconds to reach every facade instance, which is well inside the 5-minute rollback target.

---

> - Monolith remains the authoritative writer.
>   - Mechanism: job creation and outbox insert commit in one database transaction.
>   - Cost: job submission still consumes monolith capacity
>     - Only execution moves.

**What this says in plain words**
The monolith still owns all writes, and job submission plus the outbox row are one transaction. The cost is that submission still runs in the monolith.

**Why it matters**
The cost line is an honest accounting of what was and was not achieved.

- Worked example: submitting a report still costs the monolith a request and two row inserts, perhaps 5 milliseconds. Executing it cost 2 CPU-seconds. So 99.75% of the cost moved and 0.25% stayed.
- Saying this plainly is better than implying reports left entirely, because an interviewer will notice submission is still there.

---

> - Outbox relay owns publication.
>   - Mechanism: polls committed outbox rows in order, publishes, marks sent.
>   - Cost: at-least-once publication
>     - Every consumer must be idempotent.

**What this says in plain words**
A process reads committed outbox rows in order, publishes each to the queue, and marks it sent. The cost is that a message may be published more than once.

**Why it matters**
The duplication is unavoidable and the reason is worth having ready.

- Worked example: the relay publishes row 8891 to the queue, and the process dies before marking it sent. On restart it reads row 8891 again, still unsent, and publishes again. The queue now holds two copies.
- Avoiding this would require publishing and marking sent atomically, across two systems, which is the distributed transaction the design explicitly excludes. So the duplicate is accepted and every consumer is made idempotent instead.

---

> - Durable queue owns buffering.
>   - Mechanism: partitioned by tenant with retention longer than the worst expected worker outage.
>   - Cost: visibility is not execution
>     - Duplicates and expired leases are normal.

**What this says in plain words**
The queue buffers work, partitioned by tenant, retained longer than the longest expected worker outage.

**Why it matters**
"visibility is not execution" is the precise statement of what a queue does and does not provide.

- Worked example: a message becomes visible to a worker, which takes it and then pauses for garbage collection. The message's visibility timeout expires and it becomes visible again to a second worker. Two workers now hold the same message, and the queue behaved exactly as specified.
- Worked example of the retention rule: if workers can be down for 4 hours in the worst case, retention must exceed 4 hours, or messages expire unprocessed during exactly the outage the queue exists to survive.

---

> - Report workers own derived output.
>   - Mechanism: claim with a lease and attempt number, write an immutable result, publish through a conditional state transition.
>   - Cost: workers read the primary database, so a report surge is still a database load unless a read model exists.

**What this says in plain words**
Workers claim a job with a lease and an attempt number, write an immutable result, and publish it with a conditional transition.

**Why it matters**
The cost line is the most important admission in the section.

- Worked example: the extraction moved 100 CPU-seconds per second of report computation out of the monolith. Those reports still query the primary database for the data they summarize. The database load is unchanged.
- So the extraction solved the CPU contention and did not solve any database contention. That is consistent with the diagnosis, which said the database was near but under capacity, and it is exactly why the diagnosis mattered.
- "unless a read model exists" names the remedy: a separate derived copy the reports read, which the alternatives table lists with its own staleness cost.

---

> - Extraction boundary
>   - Move expensive report execution first.
>   - Keep report submission/status in the existing authoritative service initially.
>   - Workers own derived output, not unrelated transactional tables.
>   - This reduces transaction-splitting risk while buying independent compute scaling.

**What this says in plain words**
Four rules defining exactly where the line is drawn.

1. **Move execution first.** The expensive part, and the part with no ownership implications.
2. **Keep submission and status where they are, initially.**
   - Worked example of why: submission writes a job row, which is transactional data. Moving it would move write ownership, which is the thing being avoided. "initially" leaves the door open once the boundary has proven stable.
3. **Workers own derived output, not unrelated transactional tables.**
   - Worked example of the rule being violated: a worker that also updates an `orders.last_reported_at` column. That is a write to a transactional table, so the worker is now a second writer for orders and the invariant is broken by one convenient column.
4. **The summary: less transaction-splitting risk, same compute benefit.**

**Why it matters**
The boundary is chosen by asking what does *not* have to move, rather than by drawing a service around a capability. That is the difference between this answer and the naive one.

---

> → Authorize report submission and validate input.  
> → In one database transaction, create the job and its outbox event.  
> → Return `202` with the stable job ID.  
> → Relay the committed event to the queue.  
> → Worker claims execution and creates output with stable job/attempt identity.  
> → Publish the result reference using a conditional state transition.  
> → Client polls or receives an authorized completion notification.

**What this says in plain words**
One job traced end to end. Walked concretely:

Tenant `acme` submits a monthly order report at 14:00:00.

1. **Authorize and validate.**
   - The caller is authorized for this tenant and this report type, and the parameters are valid. Both checks happen before anything is written.
2. **One transaction: job row plus outbox event.**
   - Job `rj-8891` is inserted as `ACCEPTED` at version 1, and outbox event 44,120 is inserted referencing it. Either both commit or neither does.
3. **Return `202` with the job ID.**
   - Elapsed: about 5 milliseconds. The client has a stable identifier and a status URL.
4. **Relay the committed event to the queue.**
   - Within 100 milliseconds the relay picks up event 44,120, publishes it, and marks it sent.
5. **Worker claims execution with a stable job and attempt identity.**
   - A worker claims `rj-8891` as attempt 1, with a lease and the current routing epoch. It runs for 2 seconds and writes an immutable result keyed by `(rj-8891, attempt 1)`.
6. **Publish the result reference with a conditional transition.**
   - The worker attempts to move the job from `RUNNING` to `SUCCEEDED`, conditional on the version, the attempt, the epoch, and a live lease. All four match, so the transition succeeds and the result reference is set.
7. **Client polls or is notified.**
   - The status resource now returns `SUCCEEDED` with a signed, short-lived result URL.

**Why it matters**
The trace shows the three identities doing their separate work: the job identifier is stable across retries, the attempt number distinguishes executions, and the routing epoch fences an executor that lost ownership.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Routing facade | A stateless component directing traffic to the old or new implementation. | The seam, and it must fail open. |
| Fail open | Falling back to the previous working path when the new component cannot decide. | Stops the facade from being able to cause an outage. |
| Tenant cohort | A group of tenants moved together during a migration. | The unit of routing and of canary rollout. |
| Routing epoch | A number increased on every assignment change, including rollback. | Fences an executor that no longer owns a cohort. |
| Last-known-good | The most recent configuration that parsed and validated. | Keeps a bad push from leaving the facade with nothing. |
| Transactional outbox | An intent row committed with the business change and published later. | Makes accepted work survive a queue outage. |
| At-least-once publication | Every event published one or more times, never zero. | The unavoidable consequence of not having a distributed transaction. |
| Visibility timeout | The period a queue hides a message after delivery, before making it visible again. | Expiry is normal, and produces two workers on one message. |
| Queue retention | How long the queue keeps messages before discarding them. | Must exceed the worst expected worker outage. |
| Lease | A time-limited claim on a job, renewed while work continues. | Detects a worker that stopped without releasing. |
| Attempt number | A counter distinguishing executions of the same job. | Keys the immutable result and fences a stale publish. |
| Immutable result | Output written once and never modified. | Lets a losing attempt's output be discarded rather than reconciled. |
| Conditional state transition | A write applying only if the expected version, attempt, epoch, and lease still match. | Selects exactly one winning attempt. |
| Read model | A separate derived copy of data, optimized for reads. | The remedy for reports still loading the primary database. |
| Staleness contract | A stated bound on how old cached or derived data may be. | Required before any cache or read model is introduced. |
| Externalized session state | Session data held in a shared store rather than instance memory. | Precondition for a genuinely stateless tier. |

## API and data model — IC3 baseline

The interface table, the identity table, the reference SQL, what it omits, and the conventions.

---

> | Surface | Contract | Conflict/error behavior |
> |---|---|---|
> | Existing synchronous API | Retain current external semantics | Facade routes by cohort; no client migration |
> | `POST /v1/report-jobs` | Returns `202`, job ID, status URL | Tenant-scoped idempotency key plus request hash; same key with different payload returns `409` |
> | `GET /v1/report-jobs/{id}` | State, progress, result reference | Cross-tenant ID returns `404`; progress is bounded, not exact |
> | `DELETE /v1/report-jobs/{id}` | Requests cancellation | Best effort before publication; `409` once a result is published; idempotent if already cancelled |
> | `GET /v1/report-jobs` | Cursor-paginated status listing | Query by tenant and creation time; cursor is opaque and tenant-bound |
> | Operator `POST /v1/migrations` | Assign a cohort to an executor | Idempotent by `(context, cohort, target)`; returns `202` with an operation ID |
> | Operator `POST /v1/migrations/{id}:rollback` | Route a cohort back | `409` if data compatibility no longer permits rollback |

**What this says in plain words**
Seven surfaces: one unchanged, four for report jobs, and two for operators.

- **Row: Existing synchronous API.**
  - Listed first, with the contract "retain current external semantics", because the most important property of the whole design is what does *not* change.
- **Row: `POST /v1/report-jobs`.**
  - Returns `202` with a job identifier and a status URL.
  - Idempotency key plus request hash, and a mismatch returns `409`.
  - Worked example of the hash earning its place: a client retries with key `k-77` but a different date range. Without the hash, the server returns the original job, and the client receives a report for the wrong period believing it asked for this one. The hash detects the reuse and refuses.
- **Row: `GET /v1/report-jobs/{id}`.**
  - "progress is bounded, not exact" is an honest contract. Worked example: progress may say roughly 60% and jump to complete, because the worker cannot cheaply know exactly how much work remains. Promising exact progress would require instrumentation the job does not have.
  - `404` for a cross-tenant identifier, so existence is not confirmed.
- **Row: `DELETE /v1/report-jobs/{id}`.**
  - Three behaviors in one row, and they are genuinely different.
    - Best effort before publication: the job may already be executing, and cancelling it is an attempt.
    - `409` once a result is published: the work is done and the result exists, so cancellation is meaningless.
    - Idempotent if already cancelled: a second delete succeeds quietly.
- **Row: `GET /v1/report-jobs`.**
  - "cursor is opaque and tenant-bound". Worked example of why: a cursor encoding a raw row identifier could be edited to walk into another tenant's rows. Making it opaque and binding it to the tenant closes that.
- **Row: Operator `POST /v1/migrations`.**
  - Idempotent by `(context, cohort, target)`, so re-issuing the same assignment does not create a second migration.
- **Row: Operator rollback.**
  - `409` if data compatibility no longer permits rollback.
  - The most significant line in the table. Worked example: the system knows whether the new owner has taken authoritative writes. If it has, rollback is not a routing change any more, and the API refuses rather than allowing an operator to cause a worse outage during an incident.
  - The rollback gate is enforced by the interface, not by a runbook someone reads under pressure.

---

> | State | Key | Fields/invariant |
> |---|---|---|
> | Job | `(tenant_id, job_id)` | Request hash, state, version, attempt, result reference |
> | Create dedup | `(tenant_id, idempotency_key)` | Same request returns same job; changed payload conflicts; retained past the retry horizon |
> | Outbox | `event_id` | Job ID and committed transition; same transaction as job creation |
> | Worker claim | `(job_id, attempt)` | Lease/owner and fencing version if shared writes are possible |
> | Result | `(tenant_id, job_id, attempt)` | Immutable output; only winning attempt becomes visible |
> | Ownership route | `(tenant_id, context, cohort)` | Owner service, routing epoch, migration state |

**What this says in plain words**
Six kinds of state, and the attempt number appearing in two keys is the design's concurrency answer.

- **Row: Job.** One row per job, carrying a version for compare-and-set and an attempt number.
- **Row: Create dedup.** "retained past the retry horizon" repeats the rule that the record must outlive the longest client retry.
- **Row: Outbox.** "same transaction as job creation" stated as an invariant of the state itself.
- **Row: Worker claim, keyed by job and attempt.**
  - Worked example of why attempt is in the key: job `rj-8891` may be attempted three times. Each attempt is a separate claim with its own lease and owner, so the history of who tried what is preserved and a stale attempt is identifiable.
- **Row: Result, keyed by tenant, job, and attempt.**
  - "only winning attempt becomes visible" is the crucial property. Worked example: attempts 1 and 2 both complete and both write results. Both rows exist. Only the one referenced by the job row is visible to the client, and the other is orphaned and cleaned up.
  - This is why results are immutable and keyed by attempt: two executions do not collide, they coexist, and publication picks one.
- **Row: Ownership route.** Owner, epoch, and migration state per tenant, context, and cohort.

---

> - Reference DDL
>   - Keys and uniqueness match the state table above and the [companion schema](../../1sde/1.backend/2.rest102.md#infra-8).
>   - Queue messages and their visibility timeouts live in the durable queue; SQL holds jobs, dedup, the outbox, claims, result references, and routes.

**What this says in plain words**
The SQL holds the control state. Queue internals live in the queue, and result bytes live in object storage.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE report_jobs (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, job_id text NOT NULL, request_hash text NOT NULL,
>   state text NOT NULL CHECK (state IN ('ACCEPTED','RUNNING','SUCCEEDED','FAILED','CANCELLED')),
>   version bigint NOT NULL,                  -- CAS target for every transition
>   attempt integer NOT NULL DEFAULT 0 CHECK (attempt >= 0),
>   result_ref text,                          -- set only by the winning attempt's CAS
>   created_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, job_id),
>   CHECK (state <> 'SUCCEEDED' OR result_ref IS NOT NULL)
> );
> CREATE INDEX report_jobs_list ON report_jobs (tenant_id, created_at, job_id);
> CREATE INDEX report_jobs_running ON report_jobs (tenant_id, job_id) WHERE state = 'RUNNING';
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The job table, with one constraint that encodes the whole publication rule.

- **Five states**, covering the full lifecycle including both terminal failure modes.
- **`version bigint`** with "CAS target for every transition". Compare-and-set: an update applies only if the version still matches what was read.
- **`attempt integer DEFAULT 0`.** Zero means never attempted, and the first claim makes it 1.
- **`result_ref` nullable**, with "set only by the winning attempt's CAS".
- **`CHECK (state <> 'SUCCEEDED' OR result_ref IS NOT NULL)`.**
  - Read it as: if the state is succeeded, there must be a result reference.
  - Worked example of what it prevents: a bug marks a job succeeded without setting the reference. A client sees success and has nothing to download, and the job cannot be retried because it is terminal. The constraint makes that row impossible to write.
- **`report_jobs_list` on `(tenant_id, created_at, job_id)`.** Serves the paginated listing, ordered by creation time, with the job identifier as a stable tiebreak and cursor.
- **`report_jobs_running`, a partial index** covering only running jobs.
  - Worked example: after a year there may be millions of job rows and perhaps 50 running. The reclaim sweeper and the operational dashboard both want exactly those 50, and this index gives them without a scan.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE report_job_dedup (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, idempotency_key text NOT NULL,
>   request_hash text NOT NULL, job_id text NOT NULL,
>   expires_at timestamptz NOT NULL,          -- past the client retry horizon
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, idempotency_key),
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, job_id) REFERENCES report_jobs
> );
> CREATE INDEX report_job_dedup_expiry ON report_job_dedup (expires_at);
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The deduplication table for submissions.

- **The primary key is the enforcement.** A second insert with the same key fails, the service catches it, and returns the existing job.
- **`request_hash`** stored so the mismatch case is detectable.
- **`expires_at`** with the retry-horizon comment, and an index so cleanup finds expired rows without scanning.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE job_outbox (
>   event_id bigint GENERATED ALWAYS AS IDENTITY PRIMARY KEY,
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, job_id text NOT NULL,
>   transition text NOT NULL, job_version bigint NOT NULL,   -- committed in the job's transaction
>   created_at timestamptz NOT NULL, sent_at timestamptz,
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, job_id) REFERENCES report_jobs
> );
> CREATE INDEX job_outbox_unsent ON job_outbox (event_id) WHERE sent_at IS NULL;
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The outbox: one row per transition that needs publishing.

- **`event_id` as a generated identity.** Monotonically increasing, which gives the relay a natural order to publish in.
- **`job_version`** recorded on the event, so a consumer can tell which version of the job the event describes.
  - Worked example: two events for one job, at versions 3 and 5. A consumer that receives them out of order can see which is newer rather than applying whichever arrived last.
- **`sent_at` nullable**, with a partial index on unsent rows.
  - Worked example: a year of jobs produces millions of outbox rows, nearly all sent. The relay's query reads only the unsent ones, so it stays fast regardless of history, which is exactly the property a poll loop needs.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE worker_claims (
>   job_id text NOT NULL, attempt integer NOT NULL CHECK (attempt > 0),
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, owner_id text NOT NULL,
>   owner_epoch bigint NOT NULL,              -- routing epoch; fences an executor that lost ownership on rollback
>   lease_expires_at timestamptz NOT NULL, claimed_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (job_id, attempt),
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, job_id) REFERENCES report_jobs
> );
> CREATE INDEX worker_claims_lease ON worker_claims (lease_expires_at);
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
One row per attempt, recording who claimed it, under which routing epoch, and until when.

- **`attempt CHECK (> 0)`.** Claims start at 1, while the job row's attempt starts at 0, so an unclaimed job is distinguishable from a first claim.
- **`owner_epoch`** with "fences an executor that lost ownership on rollback".
  - The connection between the routing table and the execution path. Worked example: a worker claims job `rj-8891` under routing epoch 12. An operator rolls the cohort back, making the epoch 13. The worker finishes and tries to publish. Its claim carries epoch 12, which no longer matches, so the publication fails.
  - The rollback did not have to find and stop the worker. Incrementing one number invalidated its claim.
- **`lease_expires_at`** with an index, which is the reclaim sweeper's queue: find claims whose lease has passed and whose job is still running.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE job_results (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, job_id text NOT NULL, attempt integer NOT NULL,
>   object_ref text NOT NULL, size_bytes bigint NOT NULL CHECK (size_bytes >= 0),
>   sha256 text NOT NULL, produced_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, job_id, attempt),
>   FOREIGN KEY (job_id, attempt) REFERENCES worker_claims
> );
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
One row per produced result, keyed by attempt so competing attempts coexist.

- **`object_ref`** points at the bytes in object storage; the row is a reference, not the content.
- **`sha256`** lets the content be verified, and lets two attempts' outputs be compared for parity during migration.
  - Worked example of the parity use: the old executor and the new worker both produce a result for the same input. Identical hashes prove identical output, which is exactly the shadow-comparison check the migration phase needs.
- **The foreign key to `worker_claims`** means a result cannot exist without a claim that produced it.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE ownership_routes (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, context text NOT NULL, cohort text NOT NULL,
>   owner_service text NOT NULL,
>   routing_epoch bigint NOT NULL CHECK (routing_epoch >= 0),   -- bumped on every assignment and rollback
>   migration_state text NOT NULL
>     CHECK (migration_state IN ('LEGACY','SHADOW','CANARY','MIGRATED','ROLLED_BACK')),
>   updated_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, context, cohort)
> );
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The routing table, and its five migration states are the four-phase migration made queryable.

- **`routing_epoch`** with "bumped on every assignment and rollback", so every ownership change invalidates prior claims.
- **`migration_state` restricted to five values.**
  - `LEGACY`: not started. `SHADOW`: the new executor runs in parallel without publishing. `CANARY`: a small share of real traffic. `MIGRATED`: fully moved. `ROLLED_BACK`: moved and reversed.
  - Worked example of why `ROLLED_BACK` is distinct from `LEGACY`: both mean the monolith is currently serving, and they mean different things historically. A cohort that was rolled back needs investigation before being tried again; one that never started does not.

---

> - Not captured by the DDL
>   - Queue offsets, visibility timeouts, and the `24 h` queue retention live in the queue; result bytes live in object storage behind `object_ref`.
>   - The facade's cached last-known-good route snapshot is process memory, refreshed on the poll interval.
>   - Deviation from the companion: the companion's `orders`/`order_lines` domain tables are replaced by the report-job tables this file's state table names; `ownership_routes` is taken as is; `worker_claims` keeps the state table's `(job_id, attempt)` key with `tenant_id` as a column.

**What this says in plain words**
Three notes: queue internals and result bytes are elsewhere, the facade's cache is process memory, and the companion differences are listed with reasons.

**Why it matters**
The second is worth noting: the facade's routing cache is deliberately not in the database. Worked example of why: a facade that queried the routing table per request would put a database call on every request's path, which is precisely the coupling the design removes.

---

> - Conventions
>   - Job state machine: `ACCEPTED → RUNNING → SUCCEEDED | FAILED | CANCELLED`
>     - Transitions are compare-and-set on `version`.
>   - Result URLs are signed, tenant-scoped, and short-lived.
>   - Every worker call carries the verified tenant context from the job row, never from the queue message alone.

**What this says in plain words**
Three rules applying everywhere.

1. **The state machine, with every transition a compare-and-set on version.**
   - Worked example: a worker reads the job at version 4 and tries to set `SUCCEEDED` conditional on version 4. If a cancellation moved it to version 5 in between, the update affects zero rows and the worker learns it lost rather than overwriting the cancellation.
2. **Result URLs are signed, tenant-scoped, and short-lived.**
   - Three properties. Signed so the URL itself grants access without a separate check. Tenant-scoped so it cannot reach another tenant's object. Short-lived so a leaked URL stops working.
3. **Tenant context comes from the job row, never from the queue message alone.**
   - The most security-relevant convention in the file. Worked example of the attack it closes: an attacker who can publish to the queue sends a message claiming tenant `victim` and a job identifier. A worker trusting the message would generate a report over the victim's data. Reading the tenant from the committed job row means the message is only a pointer, and the authority comes from the database the monolith wrote.
   - The general principle: a message is a hint that work exists, never a source of authority.

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| `202` | The HTTP status meaning accepted for processing, not completed. | The report submission contract. |

## Migration and rollback — IC3 baseline

The first deep dive. Four phases, the rollback procedure, the consistency boundary, and the exact failure.

---

> - Phase 1: establish evidence.
>   - Baseline latency and error metrics by endpoint.
>   - Record database load and queue-free execution behavior.
>   - Define a success threshold for extraction.

**What this says in plain words**
Before changing anything, measure what exists and decide what success means.

1. **Baseline latency and errors by endpoint.**
   - By endpoint, not overall. Worked example of why: overall p99 is 280 milliseconds, which looks fine. The report submission endpoint is at 2,400 milliseconds and everything else is at 90. The aggregate hid both the problem and the fact that everything else is healthy.
2. **Record database load and queue-free execution behavior.**
   - "queue-free" means how reports behave today, running inline. That is the comparison the migration will be judged against.
3. **Define a success threshold before starting.**
   - Worked example of why in advance: after the migration, interactive p99 is 240 milliseconds, down from 310. Is that success? With a threshold agreed beforehand, the answer is already decided. Without one, it becomes an argument between people with different stakes, held after the work is done.

**Why it matters**
Phase 1 produces no code. It is listed as a phase because skipping it is the failure the whole file is about, and because a threshold agreed later is not a threshold.

---

> - Phase 2: introduce the seam.
>   - Add a routing facade or module interface.
>   - Keep public API compatibility.
>   - Make authorization and tenant context explicit across the seam.

**What this says in plain words**
Build the boundary before moving anything across it.

1. **Add a facade or a module interface.**
   - "or module interface" is a real alternative. Worked example: instead of a network facade, define an in-process interface that report execution is called through. The seam exists in the code, and the extraction later becomes swapping one implementation for a remote one.
2. **Keep public API compatibility.** Clients see nothing.
3. **Make authorization and tenant context explicit across the seam.**
   - The step that must happen while both sides are still in one process, and it is where the earlier invariant is protected.
   - Worked example: today authorization is a filter in the request pipeline, and report code relies on it implicitly. Introducing the seam means passing an explicit, verified tenant and authorization context through the interface. It changes nothing functionally, and it makes the dependency visible so it cannot be lost when the far side moves.

**Why it matters**
Phase 2 is the phase that is safe to do and easy to skip. It ships no user-visible change, and it is what makes phase 3 reversible.

---

> - Phase 3: move derived execution.
>   - Submit jobs transactionally in the current owner.
>   - Shadow report generation on deterministic sample inputs.
>   - Compare output hashes or domain-level totals.
>   - Canary real job cohorts only after parity checks.

**What this says in plain words**
Move the execution, proving equivalence before trusting it.

1. **Submit jobs transactionally in the current owner.** The job and outbox row, in the monolith.
2. **Shadow on deterministic sample inputs.**
   - Shadow means the new worker runs the same job as the old executor, and its output is discarded. Nobody sees it.
   - "deterministic" is essential. Worked example: a report including a generated timestamp produces different bytes every run, so comparison is impossible. Sample inputs must be chosen so the same input reliably produces the same output.
3. **Compare output hashes or domain-level totals.**
   - Two levels. Worked example of hashes: identical hashes prove byte-identical output, which is the strongest check and the most brittle, since a formatting change breaks it. Worked example of totals: the sum of order values matches even if the formatting differs, which is weaker and survives cosmetic differences.
4. **Canary real jobs only after parity checks pass.**

**Why it matters**
Shadowing is how you get evidence without risk. Worked example of what it catches: the new worker uses a different database connection with a different default timezone, so dates shift by hours. Every hash differs, and the bug is found while every user is still served by the old executor.

---

> - Phase 4: ramp traffic.
>   - Increase worker share in bounded steps.
>   - Watch interactive latency, job age, and output mismatches.
>   - Stop rollout when any agreed gate fails.

**What this says in plain words**
Move real traffic gradually, watching three things, stopping on any gate failure.

1. **Bounded steps.** Worked example: 1%, then 5%, then 25%, then 100%, with a soak period between each. A step small enough that its failure is survivable, held long enough for a problem to appear.
2. **Three metrics, each detecting a different failure.**
   - **Interactive latency** should improve. If it does not, the extraction is not delivering its purpose.
   - **Job age** should stay bounded. Worked example: if the worker pool is undersized, job age climbs while interactive latency looks great, because the problem moved rather than being solved.
   - **Output mismatches** should stay at zero. Any mismatch means the new executor computes something different.
3. **Stop on any agreed gate failure.** Agreed in phase 1, so the decision is not being made under pressure.

---

> - Rollback
>   - Route new jobs back to the previous executor.
>   - Let in-flight jobs finish or cancel them through explicit state transitions.
>   - Prevent both executors from publishing the same job result.
>   - Preserve new schema fields until every old binary can tolerate them.

**What this says in plain words**
Four steps, and the ordering handles the fact that rollback is not instantaneous.

1. **Route new jobs back.** A routing-table change, which propagates within the poll interval.
2. **In-flight jobs either finish or are cancelled explicitly.**
   - Worked example of why not simply killing them: a job halfway through a 2-second report either completes, and its result is used, or it moves to `CANCELLED` and the client is told. Abandoning it silently leaves a job stuck in `RUNNING` forever, with a client polling a status that never changes.
3. **Prevent both executors publishing the same result.** The epoch fence, expanded in the exact failure below.
4. **Preserve new schema fields until every old binary can tolerate them.**
   - The contract half of expand-migrate-contract. Worked example of violating it: the new version added a column and the rollback drops it. Any data written to that column during the migration is destroyed, and re-attempting the migration has to recreate it.

---

> - Consistency boundary
>   - Database ownership remains unchanged in this baseline.
>   - This makes rollback a routing/execution change.
>   - It does not require merging conflicting transactional writers.
>   - Exact failure: an old executor and a new worker both hold the same job during a rollback window
>     - The routing epoch on the claim makes the stale executor's publish fail its compare-and-set.

**What this says in plain words**
Three statements about why rollback is cheap here, then the one failure that remains.

1. **Ownership never moves.** The monolith writes everything, throughout.
2. **So rollback is a routing and execution change.** No data moves.
3. **And no merging of conflicting writers is needed.**
   - Worked example of what is being avoided: if both the monolith and a new service had taken authoritative writes, rolling back would mean reconciling two divergent histories of the same entities, deciding which writes win, and repairing whatever depended on the losers. That is a data migration under incident pressure, and it is the thing this design is built to never need.

**The exact failure, walked through**

| Time | Event |
|---|---|
| 14:00:00 | Job `rj-8891` is claimed by new worker W under routing epoch 12. |
| 14:00:01 | An operator rolls the cohort back. The routing epoch becomes 13. |
| 14:00:02 | The old executor picks up `rj-8891` and begins running it. |
| 14:00:02 | Worker W is still running. Two executors now hold the same job. |
| 14:00:03 | W finishes and attempts to publish. |

- **W's claim carries epoch 12. The current epoch is 13.** Its conditional transition fails, its result is orphaned, and it is cleaned up.
- **The old executor publishes successfully**, because its claim carries the current epoch.
- Nothing had to find W and stop it. Incrementing one number made its eventual publication fail, whenever it happened to arrive.

**Why it matters**
This is the second break from the interview framing, solved. Both executors did run the job, which wastes CPU. Only one published, which is what correctness requires.

- The general pattern worth naming: rather than preventing concurrent execution, which requires coordination, allow it and make publication exclusive, which requires only a compare-and-set.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline metrics | Measurements taken before any change, per endpoint. | The comparison every later judgement depends on. |
| Success threshold | An agreed definition of what outcome counts as success, decided in advance. | Prevents the result being argued about afterwards. |
| Seam | A boundary in code or network where one implementation can be swapped for another. | Built before anything moves across it. |
| Module interface | An in-process seam, as an alternative to a network facade. | Lets phase 2 ship with no deployment change. |
| Explicit context | Passing verified tenant and authorization data through an interface rather than relying on ambient state. | Stops the check disappearing when the far side moves. |
| Shadow execution | Running the new implementation on real input and discarding its output. | Produces evidence with no risk. |
| Deterministic input | Input that reliably produces the same output. | Required for any output comparison to be meaningful. |
| Parity check | Comparing old and new outputs, by hash or by domain totals. | The gate before real traffic moves. |
| Domain-level total | A business-meaningful aggregate compared instead of exact bytes. | Survives cosmetic differences that break a hash. |
| Canary | Moving a small share of real traffic first. | Bounds the blast radius of a bad rollout. |
| Soak period | Time held at a rollout step so problems have a chance to appear. | A step passed through too quickly proves little. |
| Job age | How long the oldest unfinished job has waited. | Detects the problem moving to the worker pool. |
| Rollout gate | An agreed condition that stops the rollout when it fails. | Agreed in phase 1, applied in phase 4. |
| Explicit state transition | Moving a job to a terminal state rather than abandoning it. | Prevents jobs stuck in `RUNNING` with clients polling forever. |
| Expand, migrate, contract | Add the new form, move to it, and only then remove the old. | Keeps a rollback able to read what the new version wrote. |
| Routing epoch fence | A number on each claim that a rollback invalidates. | Makes a stale executor's publish fail without finding it. |
| Exclusive publication | Allowing concurrent execution but permitting only one result to become visible. | Cheaper than preventing concurrency, and sufficient. |

## Outbox relay and idempotent execution — IC3 baseline

The second deep dive. The claim-and-publish pseudocode, why a queue is not exactly-once, the relay's duplicates, concrete values, and the exact failure.

---

> ```text
> CLAIM_AND_PUBLISH(job_id, worker, epoch):
>     claim = transactionally acquire runnable job with new attempt, owner epoch, and lease
>     require no unexpired competing lease and a nonterminal state
>     if claim fails: return (another attempt owns it or job is terminal)
>     result = execute(job, attempt)          -- writes immutable result keyed by attempt
>     CAS(RUNNING -> SUCCEEDED, expected attempt, owner epoch, live lease, result_ref)
>     if CAS fails: leave result unreferenced for cleanup
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
Six lines, and the structure is claim, execute, publish conditionally, discard on loss.

1. **Transactionally acquire the job with a new attempt, owner epoch, and lease.**
   - All three recorded together in one transaction, so a claim is atomic.
2. **Require no unexpired competing lease and a nonterminal state.**
   - Two conditions. Worked example of the second: a job already `CANCELLED` must not be claimable, or a worker would execute work that was explicitly called off.
3. **If the claim fails, return.** Either someone else owns it or it is finished. Not an error, just a lost race.
4. **Execute, writing an immutable result keyed by attempt.**
   - Keyed by attempt, so two concurrent attempts write two rows rather than overwriting each other.
5. **Compare-and-set from `RUNNING` to `SUCCEEDED`, checking four things.**
   - Expected attempt, owner epoch, live lease, and setting the result reference. All four must hold.
   - Worked example of each catching something different: the attempt catches a superseded execution, the epoch catches a rollback, the live lease catches a worker that paused past its claim, and setting the reference is the actual publication.
6. **If the compare-and-set fails, leave the result unreferenced.**
   - The loser does nothing destructive. It does not delete, does not retry, does not overwrite. Its output simply is not pointed at, and a cleanup process removes it later.
   - Worked example of why not deleting immediately: the losing worker might be the one confused about the state, and deletion is irreversible. Orphaning is safe, and cleanup can be deliberate.

---

> - Queue visibility is not exactly-once execution.
>   - Workers can receive duplicates.
>   - A lease can expire while a paused worker later resumes.
>   - Conditional publication selects one winning result.
>   - Non-idempotent external effects need their own effect identity and contract.

**What this says in plain words**
Four statements, and the fourth is the limit of what this design covers.

1. **Workers receive duplicates.** Normal queue behavior, not a malfunction.
2. **A lease can expire while a paused worker resumes.**
   - Worked example: a worker pauses for 6 seconds during a garbage collection. Its 4-second lease expires, the job is reclaimed, and another worker runs it. The first worker then wakes up with no idea any time passed and continues as though it still owns the job.
   - There is no way to prevent this. A process cannot detect that it was paused, and it cannot be told, because it was not running.
3. **Conditional publication selects one winner.** The design's answer: do not prevent the duplicate, make only one publication succeed.
4. **Non-idempotent external effects need their own identity and contract.**
   - The scope limit. Worked example: if the report emailed a customer, the conditional publication would not help, because the email was sent during execution, before publication. Both workers would have sent it.
   - This is why the requirements asked whether reports have external side effects, and why the answer being "none" makes the design work. With side effects, each effect needs its own idempotency key and a contract with the system performing it.

---

> - Outbox relay can publish twice after a crash.
>   - Consumers still need idempotency.
>   - See [transactional outbox guidance](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/prescriptive-guidance/latest/cloud-design-patterns/transactional-outbox.html).

**What this says in plain words**
The relay publishes and then marks the row sent. A crash between those two steps means the next run publishes again.

**Why it matters**
The duplication is structural, not a bug to fix.

- Worked example: the relay publishes event 44,120 to the queue successfully. The process is killed before the `sent_at` update commits. On restart the row is still unsent, so it publishes again. The queue now holds two copies of the same event.
- Making this atomic would require a transaction spanning the database and the queue, which is the distributed transaction the design excludes. So the duplicate is accepted, and idempotent consumers absorb it.
- The citation matters because this is a named, documented pattern rather than an improvisation, which makes it recognizable to an interviewer.

---

> - Concrete values
>   - Relay poll interval `100 ms`
>   - Outbox age alarm at `30 s`.
>   - Proposed lease is 4 s for a workload with 2 s p99 report time, renewed every 2 s.
>   - Tail pauses can exceed that lease, so expiry reclaim and result fencing remain mandatory.
>   - Reclaim in one transaction that advances the attempt and persists retry dispatch intent.
>   - Queue retention `24 h`, longer than the worst expected worker outage.

**What this says in plain words**
Six numbers, each with its reasoning.

1. **Relay poll interval 100 milliseconds.** Fast enough that submission-to-queue latency is negligible against a 60-second completion target.
2. **Outbox age alarm at 30 seconds.**
   - Age, not depth. Worked example: 10,000 unsent rows draining in 2 seconds is fine; 5 rows unsent for 10 minutes means the relay is stuck. Age distinguishes them.
3. **Lease 4 seconds for a 2-second p99, renewed every 2 seconds.**
   - Two ratios. The lease is twice the expected duration, so normal work never expires. Renewal is at half the lease, so a single missed renewal does not lose the claim.
4. **Tail pauses can exceed the lease, so reclaim and fencing remain mandatory.**
   - The honest admission. Worked example: a 6-second garbage collection pause exceeds a 4-second lease. Lengthening the lease to 30 seconds would reduce this and would mean a genuinely dead worker's job sits unreclaimed for 30 seconds. There is no lease length that both reclaims quickly and never expires early, so fencing is required regardless.
5. **Reclaim in one transaction that advances the attempt and persists retry dispatch intent.**
   - Both together. Worked example of splitting them: the attempt is advanced, fencing the old worker, and the process crashes before recording that a retry should be dispatched. The job is now un-owned and nothing will ever dispatch it, so it sits in `RUNNING` forever.
6. **Queue retention 24 hours, longer than the worst expected worker outage.**
   - Worked example: workers are down for 6 hours. With 24-hour retention, every message is still there and the backlog drains. With 1-hour retention, 5 hours of jobs were silently discarded by the queue.

---

> - Exact failure
>   - A worker pauses past its lease, a second worker completes the job, then the first resumes and writes its result.
>   - The first worker's publish carries a stale attempt and fails the compare-and-set
>   - Its result object is orphaned and reclaimed.

**What this says in plain words**
The central concurrency failure, and how it resolves.

**Walked through as a timeline:**

| Time | Worker A | Worker B | Job state |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14:00:00 | claims `rj-8891` as attempt 1, lease to 14:00:04 | | `RUNNING`, attempt 1 |
| 14:00:01 | pauses (garbage collection) | | `RUNNING`, attempt 1 |
| 14:00:05 | still paused | | lease expired |
| 14:00:05 | | reclaims as attempt 2 | `RUNNING`, attempt 2 |
| 14:00:07 | | completes, publishes | `SUCCEEDED`, attempt 2 |
| 14:00:08 | resumes, completes, writes result for attempt 1 | | `SUCCEEDED`, attempt 2 |
| 14:00:08 | attempts compare-and-set with attempt 1 | | fails |

- **A's result row exists**, keyed by `(rj-8891, attempt 1)`. It is valid output; it is simply not the published one.
- **A's compare-and-set fails** because the job's attempt is 2, not 1.
- **A's result is orphaned and cleaned up.**

**Why it matters**
Three properties are worth naming out loud.

1. **Nothing was corrupted.** Two results exist and exactly one is visible.
2. **A never had to detect its own pause.** It could not have. The check happens at publication, where the current state is available.
3. **The cost is one wasted execution**, 2 CPU-seconds, which the requirements established is acceptable because reports have no external side effects.

- The general pattern, worth stating: when you cannot prevent concurrent execution, make the *effect* exclusive rather than the *execution*. It requires only a conditional write, and it is robust against pauses, partitions, and clock disagreement, none of which the worker itself can observe.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | A transactional record that a worker owns a job, with an attempt, epoch, and lease. | Acquired atomically, or not at all. |
| Lease | A time-limited claim, renewed while work continues. | Detects a worker that stopped without releasing. |
| Lease renewal ratio | Renewing at half the lease length so one missed renewal is survivable. | 2 seconds against a 4-second lease. |
| Nonterminal state | A job state from which execution is still valid. | Prevents claiming a cancelled or finished job. |
| Immutable result keyed by attempt | Output written once, under the attempt that produced it. | Lets competing attempts coexist rather than overwrite. |
| Conditional publication | A compare-and-set checking attempt, epoch, lease, and setting the result. | Selects exactly one winner. |
| Orphaned result | Valid output that no job row references. | What a losing attempt leaves behind, cleaned up later. |
| Stop-the-world pause | A process pause, typically for garbage collection, that the process cannot observe. | Why a lease can expire under a live worker. |
| Non-idempotent external effect | An action outside the system that repeating changes, such as sending an email. | Outside this design's protection; needs its own effect identity. |
| Effect identity | An identifier the external system uses to recognize a repeated request. | The only way to make an external effect safe under retry. |
| Relay duplicate | An event published twice because a crash fell between publishing and marking sent. | Structural, because publishing and marking are in different systems. |
| Outbox age | How long the oldest unsent event has waited. | Distinguishes a busy relay from a stuck one. |
| Reclaim | Advancing a job to a new attempt after its lease expires. | Must persist retry dispatch intent in the same transaction. |
| Retry dispatch intent | The durable record that a reclaimed job should be re-queued. | Without it, a reclaimed job can be un-owned forever. |
| Queue retention | How long the queue keeps messages before discarding them. | Must exceed the worst expected worker outage. |
| Exclusive effect | Allowing concurrent execution while permitting only one result to take effect. | Cheaper and more robust than preventing concurrency. |

## Failure, operations, and security

A nine-row failure table, then what to observe, retry discipline, tenant isolation, and rollout safety.

---

> | Failure | Blast radius | Detection | Degraded behavior | Recovery |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Queue unavailable | New report execution | Outbox age | Interactive work continues; job remains accepted/pending | Relay after queue recovery within retention limits |
> | Workers unavailable | Report cohort | Oldest job age | Bound new submissions; status remains readable | Restore workers and drain backlog fairly |
> | Duplicate delivery | One job | Duplicate identity/claim | Reuse or discard redundant attempt | Publish only a valid winning attempt |
> | Report queries overload DB | Transactional workload | DB I/O and lock waits | Throttle report reads | Use a replica/snapshot only with accepted staleness |
> | New executor produces wrong output | Canary cohort | Parity/reconciliation | Stop new routing | Roll back and repair affected results |
> | Partial rollout with old schema | A version cohort | Version-specific errors | Keep compatible fields and responses | Expand-contract migration |
> | Retry storm | Shared dependencies | Retry/original request ratio | Backoff, deadlines, and load shedding | Restore capacity before increasing retries |
> | Migration routing table (control plane) unavailable | Route changes only | Facade poll failures | Facade routes on cached last-known-good assignments | Refresh routes; reconcile any cohort moved during the outage |
> | Primary DB failover | All writes, in-flight jobs | Connection errors, replication lag | Reads may serve from replica with stated staleness; submissions fail fast | Reconnect; relay resumes from committed outbox; leases expire and jobs re-claim |

**What this says in plain words**
Nine failures, with radius, detection, degraded behavior, and recovery.

- **Row: Queue unavailable.**
  - Radius is new report *execution*, not submission. Worked example: the queue is down for 20 minutes. Clients submit reports successfully and receive `202` with job identifiers. The jobs sit as accepted. When the queue returns, the relay publishes the backlog and they run. This is the outbox delivering exactly what it was built for.
  - "within retention limits" is the caveat: if the outage exceeds retention, published messages could expire, which is why retention exceeds the worst expected outage.
- **Row: Workers unavailable.**
  - "Bound new submissions" is admission control appearing operationally: if nothing can execute, accepting unlimited work builds a backlog nobody can drain.
  - "status remains readable" matters because status is served from the database, which is unaffected.
  - "drain backlog fairly" means per-tenant fairness during recovery, so the tenant that submitted most does not consume the entire restored capacity.
- **Row: Duplicate delivery.** Radius one job, absorbed by the attempt and claim mechanism.
- **Row: Report queries overload DB.**
  - The failure the architecture section admitted was not solved by extraction.
  - Worked example: workers run 50 concurrent report queries against the primary, and the transactional workload slows. The extraction moved CPU out of the monolith and moved database load nowhere.
  - Recovery offers a replica "only with accepted staleness", which is a product decision rather than a technical one.
- **Row: New executor produces wrong output.**
  - Radius is the canary cohort, which is why canarying exists.
  - Recovery includes "repair affected results", acknowledging that some clients already received wrong reports and reissuing them is part of the work.
- **Row: Partial rollout with old schema.**
  - Worked example: half the instances are new and write a new column; the old half reads rows and fails on the unexpected shape. The failures are version-specific, which is the detection signal.
- **Row: Retry storm.**
  - Detection is the ratio of retries to original requests, not the absolute rate. Worked example: 50,000 requests per second is meaningless alone; a ratio of 4:1 means three quarters of the load is retries and the system is amplifying its own failure.
  - Recovery says "restore capacity before increasing retries", which is the correct order and the opposite of the instinct.
- **Row: Migration routing table unavailable.**
  - Radius is route changes only, because the facade holds cached assignments.
  - Recovery reconciles any cohort moved during the outage, settling the drift.
- **Row: Primary DB failover.**
  - The largest radius in the table: all writes and every in-flight job.
  - "submissions fail fast" is deliberate. Worked example: during failover, submissions cannot commit. Failing immediately lets clients retry with their idempotency key; hanging would consume client threads and turn a 30-second failover into a client-side outage.
  - The recovery line shows three mechanisms converging: the relay resumes from the committed outbox, so nothing accepted is lost; leases expire, so abandoned jobs are reclaimed; and jobs re-claim, so execution continues. Each was designed for its own reason, and together they make a failover self-healing.

---

> - Observe
>   - Interactive p99 separately from job completion p99.
>   - Queue age, not just queue depth.
>   - Outbox age and duplicate rate.
>   - Database resource usage by caller class.
>   - Output mismatch/reconciliation counts.

**What this says in plain words**
Five measurements, each answering a question the others cannot.

1. **Interactive p99 separately from job completion p99.**
   - The whole point of the extraction is that these are now different things. Worked example of combining them: one latency metric including 2-second reports and 90-millisecond requests shows a p99 dominated by reports, and improving interactive latency would be invisible in it.
2. **Queue age, not just depth.** Age is comparable to the completion target; depth is not.
3. **Outbox age and duplicate rate.**
   - The duplicate rate is the more interesting one. Worked example: a rising duplicate rate means the relay is crashing between publishing and marking sent. The system absorbs it correctly, so nothing errors, and only this counter reveals that a process is repeatedly dying.
4. **Database resource usage by caller class.**
   - The metric that tests the weakest assumption. Worked example: if reports account for 70% of database time, extraction relieved CPU and the database remains the real constraint, which is exactly what the profile was supposed to determine.
5. **Output mismatch and reconciliation counts.** The parity signal, kept as a standing metric rather than only a migration-time check.

---

> - Retry discipline
>   - Clients retry submission with the same idempotency key under backoff and jitter
>   - The request hash catches payload drift.
>   - Workers retry transient execution failures up to a bounded attempt count
>   - The job then lands in `FAILED` with a reason, not an infinite loop.
>   - The relay retries publication indefinitely with backoff, because the outbox row is the source of truth and duplicates are tolerated downstream.

**What this says in plain words**
Three retry policies, deliberately different, and the differences are the content.

1. **Clients: same key, backoff and jitter, with the hash catching drift.** Bounded by the client's own patience.
2. **Workers: bounded attempts, then `FAILED` with a reason.**
   - Worked example of why bounded: a report references a deleted dataset and fails every time. Unbounded retries would consume a worker slot forever. After, say, 5 attempts the job becomes `FAILED` with the reason, the client sees a real answer, and the slot is freed.
3. **The relay: indefinite retries, with the reason given.**
   - "because the outbox row is the source of truth and duplicates are tolerated downstream" is the justification, and both halves are needed.
   - Worked example of why giving up is not an option: the outbox row represents a committed, accepted job. Abandoning it means a client was told `202` for work that will never happen, with nothing recording the abandonment.
   - Retrying forever is safe only because duplicates are absorbed downstream. Without idempotent consumers, indefinite retries would be indefinite duplication.

**Why it matters**
Three components, three policies, each derived from what the work represents. A single fleet-wide retry policy would be wrong for at least two of them.

---

> - Tenant isolation
>   - Pass verified tenant context to workers.
>   - Per-tenant admission caps and queue shares keep one tenant's report burst from starving others.
>   - Workers use least-privilege access to report inputs and result storage.
>   - Signed result URLs remain scoped and short-lived.
>   - Never rely solely on the facade for authorization if a worker API is independently reachable.

**What this says in plain words**
Five rules, and the last is the one extractions most often get wrong.

1. **Pass *verified* tenant context to workers.** From the committed job row, not from the queue message.
2. **Per-tenant admission caps and queue shares.**
   - Worked example: one tenant submits 10,000 reports at once. Without per-tenant shares, they occupy the entire pool and every other tenant's reports wait hours. With shares, that tenant gets its allocation and others are unaffected.
3. **Least-privilege access to inputs and result storage.**
   - Worked example: a worker needs to read orders for its tenant and write one result object. Giving it broad database credentials means a compromised worker reads everything, and the extraction has created a new, less-protected path to the same data.
4. **Signed result URLs, scoped and short-lived.**
5. **Never rely solely on the facade for authorization if a worker API is independently reachable.**
   - The classic extraction security failure. Worked example: authorization lives in the facade, and the worker's API is on the internal network with no checks, on the reasoning that only the facade calls it. Anything else on that network can now request any report for any tenant. The facade is a convenience, not a boundary, unless the network makes it the only path.
   - The principle: authorization belongs at the component that performs the action, not only at the component that usually routes to it.

---

> - Rollout and rollback safety
>   - Schema changes follow expand, migrate, contract
>   - Old binaries must tolerate new columns before contract.
>   - Route one canary cohort at a time
>   - The routing epoch on claims fences a stale executor after rollback.
>   - Rollback is a control-plane route change with no data movement while ownership stays in the monolith.

**What this says in plain words**
Three rules.

1. **Expand, migrate, contract, with old binaries tolerating new columns before contract.**
   - Worked example of the ordering failing: the new version adds a column and the old version's queries break on it. During a rolling deploy both versions run, so half the fleet errors. Expanding first means the old version ignores the column, which it must be verified to do before anything else proceeds.
2. **One canary cohort at a time, with the routing epoch fencing stale executors.**
3. **Rollback is a route change with no data movement, while ownership stays in the monolith.**
   - The qualifier is the whole sentence. Worked example: today rollback is a routing-table update propagating in 30 seconds. If a future phase moves order writes to a new service, rollback becomes moving data back, and it is no longer a control-plane operation.
   - Stating the condition alongside the claim is what makes it usable: an operator knows exactly which regime they are in, and the rollback API enforces it with a `409`.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Blast radius | How much of the system one failure affects. | Separates one job from all writes. |
| Outbox age | How long the oldest unsent event has waited. | Detects a stuck relay during a queue outage. |
| Oldest job age | How long the longest-waiting job has been queued. | Comparable to the completion target. |
| Fair draining | Recovering a backlog with per-tenant shares rather than in arrival order. | Stops one tenant consuming restored capacity. |
| Caller class | Which kind of client is responsible for a share of database load. | Tests whether reports are the real database constraint. |
| Accepted staleness | An agreed bound on how old replica-served data may be. | Required before reports read a replica. |
| Parity / reconciliation count | The number of output mismatches between executors. | The standing signal that the new executor is correct. |
| Version-specific errors | Failures appearing only on one binary version during a rolling deploy. | The detection signal for a schema-compatibility break. |
| Retry ratio | Retries as a multiple of original requests. | Reveals amplification that an absolute rate hides. |
| Retry storm | Retries multiplying load exactly when capacity has dropped. | Fixed by restoring capacity first, not by tuning retries. |
| Cached last-known-good routes | The facade's locally held assignments, used when the routing table is unreachable. | Keeps a control-plane outage off the request path. |
| Fail fast | Returning an error immediately rather than hanging. | Lets clients retry instead of consuming their threads. |
| Bounded attempt count | A limit on worker retries before a job is failed with a reason. | Prevents a permanently-failing job occupying a slot forever. |
| Indefinite relay retry | Retrying publication forever, justified by the outbox being the source of truth. | Safe only because duplicates are absorbed downstream. |
| Verified tenant context | Tenant identity read from the committed job row, not from a message. | A message is a pointer, never authority. |
| Per-tenant queue share | A guaranteed allocation of execution capacity per tenant. | Stops one tenant's burst starving the rest. |
| Least privilege | Granting only the access a component needs for its job. | Stops an extraction creating a broader path to the same data. |
| Facade-only authorization | Relying on the routing layer for checks the worker does not repeat. | A boundary only if the network makes it the sole path. |
| Expand, migrate, contract | Add the new form, move to it, then remove the old. | Keeps both versions working during a rolling deploy. |

## Alternatives and IC4 stretch

The option table, four stretch items, the weakest assumption, and the over-engineering warning.

---

> | Option | What it is | Strength | Weakness | Choose when |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Tune modular monolith | Improve code/queries in one deployment | Low operational complexity | Shared scaling/deployment | Bottleneck is local and ownership is clear |
> | Horizontal app scaling | More stateless instances | Simple compute expansion | Database may remain bottleneck | CPU-bound request handling |
> | Async worker extraction | Move long-running execution | Protects interactive latency | Queue and retry semantics | Baseline expensive report workload |
> | Read model/replica | Separate derived reads | Isolates read cost | Staleness and replication lag | Reports tolerate a stated snapshot delay |
> | Full domain extraction | Move API and data ownership | Independent evolution | Distributed transactions and migration | Boundary is stable and ownership benefits justify cost |

**What this says in plain words**
Five options, ordered from least to most invasive, which is the order they should be considered in.

- **Row: Tune modular monolith.**
  - The cheapest option, and the one most often skipped. Worked example of it being sufficient: a report query missing an index takes 2 seconds and takes 40 milliseconds with one. The entire problem disappears for a day's work and no architectural change.
  - Weakness is honest: everything still scales and deploys together.
- **Row: Horizontal app scaling.**
  - Simple, and its weakness is the trap the estimates section examined: more instances issuing the same queries do not help if the database is the constraint.
- **Row: Async worker extraction.** The chosen baseline. Its weakness is the queue and retry semantics that the deep dives are entirely about.
- **Row: Read model / replica.**
  - "Choose when: reports tolerate a stated snapshot delay" is a product condition, not a technical one.
  - Worked example: a monthly report over last month's orders tolerates a 30-second replica lag trivially. A real-time inventory report does not, and no amount of engineering changes that.
- **Row: Full domain extraction.**
  - The weakness is the whole second break, and "Choose when" names two conditions that must both hold: the boundary is stable, and the benefit is ownership rather than performance.
  - Worked example of the boundary condition: a domain whose model changes monthly is a bad candidate, because every change now requires coordinating two services and two deployments.

---

> - IC4 stretch — extract transactional ownership only when justified.
>   - Backfill the new store from a consistent snapshot.
>   - Capture subsequent changes with ordered source versions.
>   - Reconcile counts and business invariants.
>   - Stop or fence old writes at cutover.
>   - Route to the new authoritative writer.
>   - A read-only shadow does not prove write-path correctness.

**What this says in plain words**
If ownership must move, five steps in order, plus a warning.

1. **Backfill from a consistent snapshot.**
   - *Consistent* matters. Worked example: copying rows while writes continue gives a copy where order 100 exists and its line items do not, because they were written between the two reads. A snapshot at a single point in time avoids that.
2. **Capture subsequent changes with ordered source versions.**
   - Everything written after the snapshot must be applied, in order, with versions so a replay does not overwrite something newer.
3. **Reconcile counts and business invariants.**
   - Both. Worked example: row counts match, and the sum of order totals differs by a cent because of a rounding difference in the new store's decimal handling. Counts alone would have passed.
4. **Stop or fence old writes at cutover.**
   - The moment of the switch, and it must be exclusive. Worked example of overlap: both stores accept writes for 5 seconds. Those 5 seconds of divergence have to be found and merged afterwards, by hand, during a cutover.
5. **Route to the new authoritative writer.**

**The warning, and it is the sharpest line in the section:**

- **"A read-only shadow does not prove write-path correctness."**
- Worked example: the new service has shadowed reads for two weeks with perfect parity. That proves it can compute the same answers from the same data. It proves nothing about whether its write path enforces the same constraints, handles the same concurrency, or maintains the same invariants under contention, because none of that was ever exercised.
- Read parity is evidence about reads. Write correctness needs the write path exercised, which means either a dual-write phase with comparison or a genuinely reversible cutover.

---

> - IC4 stretch — rollback after new writes
>   - Routing back is insufficient if only the new store has recent committed writes.
>   - Require reverse replication/reconciliation or a planned forward-repair strategy.
>   - Declare the point after which rollback needs data movement.

**What this says in plain words**
Three sentences about the point of no return.

1. **Routing back is insufficient once the new store holds writes the old one never saw.**
   - Worked example: the new service has been authoritative for 6 hours and taken 40,000 writes. Routing back makes the monolith authoritative again, serving data 6 hours stale, and the 40,000 writes are invisible. Customers see orders vanish.
2. **So either reverse replication, or a planned forward repair.**
   - Reverse replication means continuously copying new-store writes back to the old store, so the old store stays current and rollback stays a routing change. It costs a permanent replication pipeline for the migration's duration.
   - Forward repair means accepting that rollback loses data and having a documented plan to reconcile afterwards.
3. **Declare the point after which rollback needs data movement.**
   - The actionable instruction. Worked example: "rollback is a routing change until the first authoritative write lands in the new store at 09:00 on Tuesday; after that it requires running the reverse-replication drain, which takes about 40 minutes."
   - Written down in advance, so the decision during an incident is looking up a fact rather than making an assessment.

---

> - IC4 stretch — 10× load
>   - `50,000 requests/s` implies about `1,670 cores` for interactive work and `500 reports/s`
>   - The single primary becomes the bottleneck before the app tier does.
>   - Shard the transactional store by tenant and give reports a replica or snapshot read model with a stated freshness.
>   - Partition the queue by tenant so one tenant's backlog cannot delay another's.

**What this says in plain words**
At ten times the load, the constraint moves.

1. **1,670 cores and 500 reports per second.** Ten times the earlier figures.
2. **The single primary becomes the bottleneck before the app tier does.**
   - The key observation. Worked example: the application tier scales by adding instances, which is an ordinary operation. The single primary database cannot be scaled the same way, so at some load it becomes the limit regardless of how many application instances exist.
   - So at 10× the answer is no longer "extract compute", because compute was never the problem at that scale.
3. **Shard the transactional store by tenant, and give reports a read model.**
   - Sharding splits the write load across databases, with tenant as the natural boundary since tenants do not share entities.
   - The read model removes report queries from the primary entirely, which is the load the baseline extraction admitted it did not solve.
4. **Partition the queue by tenant.**
   - Worked example: one tenant submits 100,000 reports. In a single-partition queue, every other tenant's jobs sit behind them in arrival order. Partitioned by tenant, each tenant's backlog is its own and drains at its own share.

---

> - IC4 stretch — cost and blast radius
>   - Ten serial independent `99.9%` dependencies would yield roughly `0.999^10 ≈ 99.0%` path availability.
>   - Independence is an illustrative assumption
>   - Real failures can be correlated.
>   - Avoid splitting one request into unnecessary synchronous hops.
>   - Cell the facade and worker pools per cohort so a bad deploy strands one cohort, not every tenant.
>   - Worker cores cost the same as monolith cores
>   - The saving is tail-latency isolation and independent deploys, not raw CPU.

**What this says in plain words**
Four points, and the first is the arithmetic against over-splitting.

1. **Ten serial 99.9% dependencies give about 99.0%.**
   - Worked example of what that means: 99.9% is about 43 minutes of downtime per month. 99.0% is about 7 hours. Splitting one request into ten synchronous hops multiplies the failure probability, so a request touching ten services is roughly ten times as likely to fail as one touching a single service.
2. **Independence is illustrative; real failures correlate.**
   - Worked example: all ten services share one network, one identity provider, and one cloud region. Their failures are not independent, so the true figure could be better, if one shared cause dominates, or worse, if a shared dependency takes all ten down at once.
3. **Avoid unnecessary synchronous hops.** The direct conclusion.
4. **Cell the facade and worker pools per cohort.**
   - Worked example: a bad worker deploy rolled fleet-wide stops every tenant's reports. Celled per cohort, it stops one cohort's, and the rest continue while you diagnose.
5. **Worker cores cost the same as monolith cores.**
   - The honest accounting, and it is the most valuable sentence here. Worked example: 100 CPU-seconds per second of report work costs the same whether it runs in the monolith or in workers. The extraction did not reduce the compute bill by a cent.
   - What was actually bought: tail-latency isolation, so reports cannot degrade interactive requests, and independent deploys, so report changes ship without redeploying the transactional path.
   - Anyone claiming an extraction saves money on compute has not done this arithmetic.

---

> - Weakest assumption
>   - Report CPU, not the database, is the measured bottleneck
>   - If the database is saturated, workers only move the same queries to another process.
>   - Mitigation: profile before extraction and shadow the workers against a replica to see whether database load, not CPU, is what drops.

**What this says in plain words**
The assumption the entire design rests on, stated as the thing most likely to be wrong.

1. **The assumption:** the bottleneck is report CPU in the request process.
2. **If it is wrong, the extraction achieves nothing.**
   - Worked example: the database is at 95% utilization. Reports move to workers. The workers issue the same queries, the database is still at 95%, interactive latency is unchanged, and you have added a queue, a relay, a worker fleet, and a migration for no benefit.
3. **Two mitigations, and the second is clever.**
   - **Profile before extraction.** The obvious one.
   - **Shadow the workers against a replica to see whether database load, not CPU, is what drops.**
     - Worked example of the experiment: run the workers in shadow, pointed at a replica. The monolith's CPU drops, because reports are no longer executing there. Now look at the primary database. If its load also dropped, the reports were loading it and a read model is the real fix. If its load is unchanged, the reports were CPU-bound in the application and the extraction is correct.
     - This is a genuine experiment with a falsifiable prediction, run before committing, and it distinguishes the two hypotheses that the whole design depends on telling apart.

---

> - Over-engineering warning
>   - “Microservices make it scalable” is not a diagnosis.
>   - Do not split write ownership to solve a compute problem.

**What this says in plain words**
Two warnings, and the first is a statement about reasoning rather than architecture.

1. **"Microservices make it scalable" is not a diagnosis.**
   - Worked example of the pattern: a system is slow, microservices are known to be associated with scale, therefore microservices. No resource was named, no measurement was taken, and no prediction was made about what would improve.
   - A diagnosis names the exhausted resource and predicts what changes when it is relieved. Everything else is a preference.
2. **Do not split write ownership to solve a compute problem.**
   - The specific misapplication this file exists to prevent.
   - Worked example: the measured problem is report CPU. Splitting order-management write ownership into a separate service does not reduce report CPU at all, and it introduces distributed transactions, a dual-write window, and a rollback that requires data movement. Every cost, none of the benefit.
   - The matching rule: move computation to solve a compute problem, and move ownership only to solve an ownership problem.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Modular monolith | One deployment with clear internal module boundaries. | The cheapest option, and often sufficient. |
| Read model | A separate derived copy of data optimized for reads. | Removes report queries from the primary, at the cost of staleness. |
| Snapshot delay | How far behind a replica or derived copy runs. | Must be stated and accepted before reports use it. |
| Full domain extraction | Moving both the API and the data ownership of a domain. | Justified by ownership benefits, never by compute. |
| Consistent snapshot | A copy taken at a single point in time, without partial writes. | Prevents a backfill containing half-written entities. |
| Ordered source version | A monotonic version on each change, so replay cannot overwrite something newer. | Required to apply changes after a backfill. |
| Invariant reconciliation | Comparing business properties, not only row counts. | Catches differences counts cannot see. |
| Write fencing at cutover | Making the old store stop accepting writes before the new one starts. | Prevents a divergence window that must be merged by hand. |
| Read-only shadow | Running the new implementation for reads only, comparing outputs. | Proves nothing about write-path correctness. |
| Reverse replication | Copying the new store's writes back to the old one during a migration. | Keeps rollback a routing change after ownership moves. |
| Forward repair | Accepting that rollback loses data, with a documented plan to reconcile. | The alternative to reverse replication. |
| Point of no return | The declared moment after which rollback requires data movement. | Written down in advance so incident decisions are lookups. |
| Serial dependency availability | Multiplying the availability of every component a request must traverse. | Ten 99.9% hops give about 99.0%. |
| Correlated failure | Failures sharing a cause, so they are not independent. | Makes the multiplication illustrative rather than exact. |
| Cell | An independent slice of the fleet serving one cohort. | Contains a bad deploy to one cohort. |
| Tail-latency isolation | Preventing one workload's slowness from affecting another's. | What the extraction actually buys, since cores cost the same. |
| Falsifiable experiment | A test with a prediction that would disprove the hypothesis. | Shadowing against a replica to see which load drops. |
| Diagnosis | Naming the exhausted resource and predicting what relieving it changes. | What "microservices make it scalable" is not. |

**Why the fourth column is the one to score**

- **Row: Requirements.**

## Interview pushback and validation

Eight challenges, each with a baseline and a stretch answer, then eight things to test.

---

> - Q1: Why not split every component immediately?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Measure the bottleneck and extract a bounded expensive capability.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Include deployment ownership and cross-team coordination cost in the decision.

**What is really being asked**
Whether you will defend the restraint, since the question invited microservices and you proposed one extraction.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Measure first, then extract one bounded capability.

- Worked example of splitting everything: ten services drawn along the team chart, all still reading the same orders table. The database load is unchanged, requests now cross ten network boundaries, and any operation spanning two of them has lost its transaction. Every cost was paid and nothing was relieved.
- "bounded expensive capability" names the two properties that make report execution a good candidate: its edge is clear, and it is where the measured cost is.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
The decision includes costs that are not technical.

- **Deployment ownership.** Worked example: a service needs an on-call rotation, a deployment pipeline, alerting, and someone who understands it at 3 a.m. Ten services need ten of those, and an organization that cannot staff that has created ten things nobody owns.
- **Cross-team coordination.** Worked example: a change spanning two services requires two teams, two reviews, two deployments, and an ordering between them. What was a single pull request becomes a project.
- The strong framing: these costs are permanent and recur on every change, while the performance benefit is one-time. That asymmetry is what makes extraction a decision rather than a default.

---

> - Q2: The database is the bottleneck. Will workers solve it?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Not if workers issue the same expensive queries concurrently.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Use workload isolation, indexing, or a derived read model under a stated freshness contract.

**What is really being asked**
The direct test of the weakest assumption, and the interviewer is checking whether you will notice your own design fails under it.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
No, and possibly worse.

- Worked example: reports move to workers. The 100 CPU-seconds per second of application CPU leaves the monolith, and the same queries still hit the database. Its load is unchanged.
- Worked example of worse: previously reports ran on request threads, so the application's own concurrency limits incidentally throttled them. A dedicated worker pool may issue *more* concurrent report queries than before, increasing database contention while looking like an improvement in the application's metrics.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Three remedies, in increasing order of cost.

1. **Workload isolation.** Separate connection pools or resource groups so report queries cannot consume the transactional workload's share.
2. **Indexing.** The cheapest real fix if the queries are inefficient rather than merely numerous.
3. **A derived read model under a stated freshness contract.**
   - Worked example: reports read a replica or a purpose-built summary table, and the contract says data may be up to 60 seconds old. The primary sees no report queries at all.
   - "stated freshness contract" is the condition: this is only available if someone agrees the reports may be slightly stale, which is a product decision.

---

> - Q3: The job row commits but queue publication fails.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - A transactional outbox preserves the intent for later publication.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Relay duplicates require idempotent consumer effects and reconciliation.

**What is really being asked**
The dual-write problem: two systems must both be updated, and there is no transaction spanning them.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
The outbox converts a dual write into a single write plus an asynchronous delivery.

- Worked example: the job row and the outbox row commit together in the database. Publishing to the queue then fails. The outbox row is still there, unsent, and the relay will publish it when the queue returns. Nothing was lost, because nothing depended on the queue being available at commit time.
- The insight worth stating: you cannot make a database write and a queue publish atomic, so you make the *intent* atomic with the business change and accept that delivery happens later.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
The outbox solves loss and introduces duplication.

- Worked example: the relay publishes and dies before marking the row sent. On restart it publishes again. Two identical messages are in the queue.
- So consumers must be idempotent, and reconciliation must exist to verify that events and effects correspond.
- The framing: the outbox trades an availability coupling for a duplication requirement, which is a good trade only because duplicates here are wasteful rather than harmful.

---

> - Q4: We now require strict atomic updates across two extracted services.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Reconsider the boundary or keep those writes in one owner.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Only adopt distributed coordination or compensation after defining the exact invariant.

**What is really being asked**
A requirement that would force distributed transactions. Will you build them, or question the boundary?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
The requirement is evidence the boundary is wrong.

- Worked example: two services must update atomically. That means the two pieces of data participate in one invariant, which means they belong to one owner. The split cut through something that should not have been cut.
- The cheapest correct answer is to move the boundary, not to build machinery that makes a bad boundary survivable.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
If the boundary genuinely cannot move, define the invariant precisely before choosing a mechanism.

- Worked example of why the invariant must come first: "the order and the inventory must be consistent" is not precise enough to design against. "Inventory must never go negative, and an order may briefly exist before its inventory is decremented, provided it is decremented within 30 seconds or the order is cancelled" is a specification, and it happens to be satisfiable by compensation rather than by a distributed transaction.
- Compensation means doing the second step later and undoing the first if it fails. It is cheaper than a distributed transaction and it is only correct for invariants that tolerate a temporary inconsistency, which is exactly why the invariant has to be stated first.

---

> - Q5: Can you roll back after moving write ownership?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Only if the old owner has the required current data and is safely reactivated.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Define reverse replication or forward repair before cutover.

**What is really being asked**
Whether you understand that the rollback lever stops working at a specific moment.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Only under two conditions, and both are easy to lose.

1. **The old owner has the required current data.** Worked example: the new store took 40,000 writes the old one never saw. Reactivating the old owner serves stale data and strands those writes.
2. **It can be safely reactivated.** Worked example: the old owner was decommissioned, its schema was contracted, or its deployment was removed. It may not be runnable at all.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Decide before cutover, because deciding afterwards is deciding during an incident.

- **Reverse replication** keeps the old store current, so rollback stays a routing change. It costs a replication pipeline maintained for the migration's duration.
- **Forward repair** accepts that rollback loses recent writes and documents how they will be recovered or reconciled.
- Either is defensible. Having neither is what turns a rollback into a second outage, and the choice must exist in writing before the first authoritative write lands in the new store.

---

> - Q6: Why does a durable queue still need admission control?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Arrival above service rate makes backlog and completion time grow without bound.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Allocate per-tenant shares and shed expensive jobs before violating global latency targets.

**What is really being asked**
A queue is durable and buffers work. Why is that not sufficient?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
A queue changes where excess work waits, not whether it can be served.

- Worked example: 50 reports per second arrive, the pool serves 40. Backlog grows by 10 per second. After an hour it is 36,000 jobs. Completion time for a newly-submitted job is now 15 minutes and rising, against a 60-second target.
- Nothing failed. Every job was durably accepted and will eventually run. The promise was broken quietly rather than loudly, which is worse than a rejection because nobody is notified.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Two mechanisms beyond a simple cap.

1. **Per-tenant shares.** Worked example: one tenant submits 10,000 reports. Without shares they occupy the entire backlog and every other tenant waits behind them. With shares, each tenant's work drains at its own allocation.
2. **Shed expensive jobs before violating global latency targets.**
   - Worked example: two report types, one costing 200 milliseconds and one costing 30 seconds. Under pressure, refusing the expensive ones preserves the target for the cheap ones, whereas refusing uniformly degrades everyone.
   - The principle: shedding is a choice about what to protect, and choosing deliberately beats letting the queue choose by arrival order.

---

> - Q7 (premise reversal): Why split at all instead of buying a bigger box?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Vertical scaling is the first move when headroom exists, but it leaves report CPU on the request threads, so the tail-latency coupling remains.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - The extraction buys isolation and independent deploys, not raw CPU
>     - Compare the cost of a queue and workers against the cost of the bigger box and its ceiling.

**What is really being asked**
The premise reversal, and the strongest challenge in the set. The design added a queue, a relay, a worker fleet, and a migration. A bigger machine is one purchase order.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Concede that vertical scaling comes first, then name what it does not fix.

- Worked example of it working: the machine has 40% headroom, so double the cores and the CPU contention disappears for a year. That is the correct move, and it takes a week.
- Worked example of what remains: reports still run on request threads in the same process. A single expensive report still occupies a thread a user is waiting behind, and a report bug still degrades interactive latency. The coupling is structural, and more cores make it rarer rather than absent.
- The honest position: vertical scaling buys time and does not change the failure mode.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Name what is actually being bought, and make the comparison explicit.

- **Not raw CPU.** 100 CPU-seconds per second costs the same in workers as in the monolith, as the cost section established.
- **Isolation and independent deploys.** Reports cannot degrade interactive latency, and report changes ship without redeploying the transactional path.
- **Compare against the box's cost and its ceiling.**
  - Worked example: the largest available machine is 4× the current one. That is the ceiling, and at 10× load it is not enough. The extraction's cost is operational complexity that does not have a ceiling.
  - The deciding question: is the problem interference, which extraction fixes and cores do not, or insufficient capacity, which cores fix and extraction does not? The measured symptom, latency breaching only during report-heavy periods, says interference.

---

> - Q8: The facade is a new single point of failure.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - It is stateless and fails open to the monolith, so its outage costs routing flexibility, not availability.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Cache routes as last-known-good so a routing-table outage changes nothing on the data path.

**What is really being asked**
A fair objection: the design added a component every request passes through.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Two properties make it not a point of failure in the usual sense.

1. **Stateless.** Worked example: any instance serves any request, so instances are added and removed freely and losing one costs nothing but capacity.
2. **Fails open to the monolith.**
   - Worked example: the facade cannot determine a route. It sends the request to the monolith, which is where it went before the facade existed. The failure mode is the pre-migration behavior.
   - So the facade's outage costs routing flexibility, meaning you cannot direct traffic to workers, rather than availability.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
The routing table is a dependency, and caching removes it from the request path.

- Worked example: the routing table is down for 30 minutes. The facade routes from its cached assignments and every request is served normally. A facade that queried the table per request would fail every request, which would make the objection correct.
- The general principle: a component on the request path may consult a control plane, and it must never *depend* on it per request. Cache, use last-known-good, and bound the staleness.

---

> - Validation scenarios
>   - Submit timeout followed by same-key retry.
>   - Relay crash after publication.
>   - Duplicate worker claim.
>   - Old and new executor overlap during rollback.
>   - Schema compatibility across mixed binaries.
>   - Report read surge while interactive traffic peaks.
>   - Routing table unavailable during a cohort move.
>   - Primary failover with a job mid-execution.

**What this says in plain words**
Eight tests, each mapping to a mechanism discussed earlier.

1. **Submit timeout followed by same-key retry.** Assert one job exists, and separately that the same key with a different payload returns `409`.
2. **Relay crash after publication.** Kill the relay between publishing and marking sent. Assert the event is published twice and the job executes once.
3. **Duplicate worker claim.** Deliver one message to two workers. Assert one claim succeeds, one result publishes, and the other is orphaned.
4. **Old and new executor overlap during rollback.** The direct epoch-fence test: reproduce the rollback timeline and assert the stale executor's publish fails.
5. **Schema compatibility across mixed binaries.** Run old and new versions simultaneously against one database and assert neither errors on the other's rows.
6. **Report read surge while interactive traffic peaks.** The test of the weakest assumption under load: assert whether interactive latency holds when workers are saturating the database.
7. **Routing table unavailable during a cohort move.** Assert the facade continues on cached routes and that no cohort ownership changes while the table is unreachable.
8. **Primary failover with a job mid-execution.** Assert submissions fail fast, the relay resumes from the committed outbox, the in-flight job's lease expires, and it is re-claimed and completed.

**Why it matters**
Each item names a mechanism that could be wrong and the observation that would reveal it. Four of the eight reproduce failures described earlier in the file.

## Recall card

Five one-liners and a four-sentence closing script, memorized rather than improvised.

---

> - “Diagnose first, then isolate the measured bottleneck.”

**What this says in plain words**
Measure before changing anything, and target what the measurement named.

**Why it matters**
"measured" is the word carrying the weight.

- Worked example of the alternative: reports feel slow, so they are extracted. The database was the constraint, so nothing improves and the system now has a queue, a relay, and a worker fleet to operate.
- The sentence is short enough to open with and it commits you to producing evidence, which is the whole argument of the question.

---

> - “Move derived execution before splitting transactional ownership.”

**What this says in plain words**
Move computation over data someone else owns, before moving who owns the data.

**Why it matters**
It is the ordering rule the entire design follows, and the asymmetry behind it is worth stating.

- Worked example: moving report execution is reversible in seconds by changing a routing table. Moving order ownership is reversible only by moving data back.
- Computation can be moved back. Ownership, once it has taken writes, cannot. So do the reversible thing first, and do the irreversible thing only when its own benefit justifies it.

---

> - “Commit job intent through an outbox and make worker publication idempotent.”

**What this says in plain words**
Two mechanisms: the intent is committed with the business change, and publication is safe to attempt more than once.

**Why it matters**
Each solves one half of a problem the other cannot touch.

- **The outbox solves loss.** Worked example: the queue is down at submission time, and the job is not lost, because it was committed to the database.
- **Idempotent publication solves duplication.** Worked example: the relay publishes twice, two workers execute, and one result becomes visible.
- Neither alone is sufficient: an outbox without idempotent consumers produces duplicated effects, and idempotent consumers without an outbox lose work when the queue is unavailable.

---

> - “Rollback is simple only while data ownership and compatibility remain controlled.”

**What this says in plain words**
Rollback is cheap under two conditions, and the sentence states them rather than the conclusion.

**Why it matters**
The word "only" is the entire value.

- Worked example of the first condition failing: the new service has taken authoritative writes, so routing back serves stale data and strands them.
- Worked example of the second failing: the schema was contracted, so the old binary cannot read rows the new one wrote.
- A claim that rollback is simple, without its conditions, is the claim that causes an operator to pull a lever that no longer works.

---

> - “The facade routes on cached assignments; a control-plane outage never touches the request path.”

**What this says in plain words**
The facade holds its routes locally, so the routing table can be down without affecting requests.

**Why it matters**
It is the static-stability property stated as a boundary.

- Worked example: the routing table is down for 30 minutes. Every request is routed normally from cached assignments. What is impossible is moving a cohort, which is correctly blocked because the record of ownership cannot be updated.
- The qualifier to carry with it: cached assignments are bounded by a validity budget, so "never touches the request path" holds for the stated outage window rather than indefinitely.

---

> - Wrap-up script
>   - “The design keeps one transactional owner, adds a routing seam, and moves report execution to workers fed by a transactional outbox.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence one: three decisions, and the first is the one that sounds like an absence.

**Why it matters**
"keeps one transactional owner" is stated first because it is the choice everything else depends on. Every property of the migration, from rollback cost to the absence of distributed transactions, follows from ownership not moving.

---

> -   - “The key invariant is exactly one authoritative writer per entity, so rollback stays a routing change with no data movement.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence two: the invariant and its consequence, in that order.

**Why it matters**
The consequence is what makes the invariant worth stating.

- Worked example: with one writer throughout, rollback updates a routing table and propagates within the poll interval. With two writers at any point, rollback means reconciling divergent histories under incident pressure.
- An invariant stated without its payoff sounds like discipline for its own sake. Stated with it, it is an engineering decision with a measurable benefit.

---

> -   - “The main tradeoff is at-least-once delivery, which buys durability across a queue outage at the price of idempotent claims and conditional publication.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence three: what you chose and exactly what it costs.

- Bought: accepted work survives a queue outage entirely.
- Paid: every consumer must be idempotent, which means leases, attempt numbers, and conditional publication throughout.

**Why it matters**
The cost is stated as mechanisms rather than as a vague complexity increase. Worked example: the entire outbox deep dive, the claim protocol, and the epoch fence all exist because delivery is at-least-once. That is a substantial amount of design bought by one delivery guarantee.

---

> -   - “My weakest assumption is that report CPU, not the database, is the bottleneck; I would confirm it with a profile before touching the seam.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence four: the flaw, and the specific first action.

**Why it matters**
It closes the loop with the opening sentence, and that symmetry is the answer's strongest structural feature.

- The answer opened with "diagnose first" and closes by naming the diagnosis as unconfirmed.
- Worked example of why it is honest rather than weak: if the database is the constraint, the extraction relieves nothing and the correct design is a read model instead. Saying so demonstrates that the conditionality was understood all along rather than discovered under questioning.

**What these are, and which claim each one backs**

1. **The strangler fig pattern.**
   - Backs the routing-seam approach: a facade in front of a legacy system, behind which capabilities are replaced one at a time until the old system is unused.
   - The name comes from a plant that grows around a tree and eventually replaces it, which is a useful image for the migration: the facade grows around the monolith, and capabilities move out gradually rather than in one rewrite.
   - Worth citing in the room because it establishes that the incremental approach is a named, documented pattern rather than timidity.
2. **The transactional outbox.**
   - Backs both halves of the outbox argument: the intent is committed atomically with the business change, and delivery is at-least-once so consumers must be idempotent.
   - The citation matters because the duplication is the part candidates most often omit, and the documentation states it plainly rather than leaving it as an implementation detail.
3. **The database connection-pool reference.**
   - A sibling file covering pool sizing in detail, listed as "retained LLD background", meaning low-level design context rather than a dependency.
   - It supports the concurrency estimate, where the pool is sized against database capacity rather than request concurrency, and it is where the reasoning behind that sizing lives.

**Why it matters**
Each link is annotated with the specific claim it supports, and the first two are named patterns, which lets you say "this is the strangler fig approach with a transactional outbox" and have the interviewer immediately know the shape of everything you are about to describe.
