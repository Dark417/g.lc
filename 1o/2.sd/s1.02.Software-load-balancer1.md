## Interview framing

This section of the source does four jobs: it states what level of answer you are aiming at, it states where the question came from, it names why the problem is hard, and it gives you the exact sentences to open with. We take them in order.

---

> - Target: OCI IC3 ownership of a bounded platform component.

**What this says in plain words**
You are answering as an engineer who owns one clearly-bordered piece of infrastructure end to end. Not a whole product, not a single function — one component, including its failures and its on-call pager.

**Why it matters**
The word *bounded* controls how much you design. A bounded-component answer draws the box around the load balancer and says out loud what is outside the box: the front door, the backends' internal queues, the certificate authority. An unbounded answer wanders into redesigning DNS and runs out of clock.

- Worked example of the boundary
  - Inside the box: which backend a request goes to, when a backend is removed, how configuration reaches the proxy, how long a drain takes.
  - Outside the box: how client connections reach the proxy fleet in the first place, how the backend's own thread pool is sized, how the private key was generated.
  - If you spend 6 of your 45 design minutes on something outside the box, that is 13% of the interview spent not answering the question.

---

> - `IC3 baseline` is what must land in every phase.
> - `IC4 stretch` adds architecture changes under stronger constraints.
> - These are preparation labels, not Oracle's official rubric.

**What this says in plain words**
There are two tiers of answer in the source file. `IC3 baseline` is the floor — say all of it or you have not answered. `IC4 stretch` is extra depth you add when the interviewer pushes. These labels are a study device invented for this preparation, not an official company grading scale.

**Why it matters**
Three separate reasons, worth splitting:

1. **The baseline is a checklist, not an aspiration.** Every `IC3 baseline` bullet has to appear somewhere in what you say. If you skip the retry contract because you ran long on the diagram, you dropped a required item.
2. **The stretch is additive, never a rewrite.** An `IC4 stretch` answer starts by restating the baseline and then adds the harder case. If your stretch answer contradicts your baseline answer, the interviewer concludes you did not have a position.
3. **The labels are yours, not Oracle's.** Saying "this is the IC4 answer" out loud in the interview would claim knowledge of their rubric that you do not have. Use the labels to study; never cite them to the interviewer.

> - Why this is hard
>   - Naive: round robin over a static backend list behind one proxy process.

**What this says in plain words**
The obvious first design: one proxy program, a hard-coded list of backend servers, and requests handed out strictly in turn — backend 1, backend 2, backend 3, backend 4, backend 1, and so on. *Static* means the list changes only when a human edits a file and restarts the proxy.

**Why it matters**
This is the design an interviewer expects you to produce in 30 seconds and then immediately attack. Naming the naive design first is what earns you the right to say "and here is where it breaks". Skipping straight to the sophisticated design reads as memorisation.

- Worked example of why round robin looks fine at first
  - 4 backends, 10,000 requests per second arriving, 2,500 requests per second to each.
  - Every backend was load-tested to 3,000 requests per second.
  - Utilisation is 83%. Nothing is on fire. Round robin looks correct.
  - It is correct *only while every request costs the same and every backend is equally fast*. The next line is about what happens when that stops being true.

---

>   - First break: one slow backend absorbs its round-robin share and stalls
>     - A failed health probe on every backend removes the whole pool.

**What this says in plain words**
Two distinct failures are packed here. Split them:

1. **A slow backend still gets its full share.** Round robin does not ask "are you busy?" — it just counts. If backend 3 becomes ten times slower, it still receives one request in four, and those requests pile up on it.
2. **A health check that fails everywhere deletes everything.** If the proxy removes any backend whose health probe fails, and a shared dependency makes the probe fail against all of them at once, the proxy removes all of them and serves nothing.

**Worked example for (1): the stall**

- Setup: 4 backends, 10,000 requests per second, round robin, so 2,500 requests per second each.
- Backend 3 gets stuck on a slow database connection. Its average response time goes from 100 milliseconds to 5,000 milliseconds, a 50x slowdown.
- Requests in flight on a backend equals arrival rate times duration. This is Little's Law, defined in the term table.
  - Healthy backend: 2,500 x 0.1 s = **250 requests in flight**.
  - Backend 3: 2,500 x 5.0 s = **12,500 requests in flight**.
- Backend 3 has a connection limit of, say, 1,000. It hits that in under half a second and starts refusing or queueing.
- Meanwhile round robin keeps feeding it 2,500 requests per second, forever, because round robin cannot see any of this.
- Customer-visible result: 25% of all traffic is either very slow or failing, while three machines sit at 83% and could have absorbed more.

**Worked example for (2): the health check that eats the fleet**

- 500 backends. The health endpoint is `GET /healthz`, and somebody adds a database ping inside it.
- The database has a 30-second hiccup.
- All 500 `/healthz` calls fail simultaneously.
- The proxy's rule is three consecutive failures to remove. Probes run every 2 seconds, so after roughly 6 seconds, **all 500 backends are removed**.
- The pool now has zero eligible members. Every request returns an error, even though all 500 machines could still have served the 95% of traffic that never touches that database.
- The database recovers at 30 seconds. The backends need 2 consecutive successes to come back, so the outage lasts about 34 seconds, and the reconnect surge at the end may knock them down again.

→ The health signal removed more capacity than the original fault did.  
⇒ The health system became the outage.  
→ This is why the source later insists on a cap on the ejected fraction.

---

>   - Second break: a timed-out `POST` is retried onto a second backend, a deploy drains a backend that still owns long streams, and a configuration push lands on half the fleet.

**What this says in plain words**
Three more failures, each independent. Split them:

1. **Retrying a write you never got an answer for.** A `POST` (a request that changes data) timed out. You do not know whether the backend did the work and the reply was lost, or whether it never started. If you retry onto a different backend, you may do the work twice.
2. **Draining a backend that is still holding long-lived connections.** You are deploying new code. You tell backend 7 to stop taking new work. But backend 7 is currently streaming a live feed to 200 clients. "Stop taking new work" does not end those.
3. **A configuration change that reaches only some of the fleet.** You push a new routing rule to 10 proxies. 5 acknowledge, 5 do not. Now the same URL behaves differently depending on which proxy the client happened to land on.

**Worked example for (1): the double charge**

- A client sends `POST /payments` with a 2-second timeout.
- The backend receives it, charges the card at 1,900 milliseconds, and starts writing the response at 2,050 milliseconds.
- The client has already given up at 2,000 milliseconds. The proxy sees a timeout and retries onto a second backend.
- The second backend charges the card again.
- The customer is billed twice for one order. The proxy's log shows one failed request and one successful request, so nothing looks wrong from the proxy's side.
- The only safe fix is at the backend: the request carries an idempotency key, and the backend refuses to perform work it has already performed for that key.

**Worked example for (2): the cut stream**

- Backend 7 has 200 open Server-Sent-Events streams, average age 12 minutes, and they can run for hours.
- Deploy says "drain backend 7, 30-second deadline".
- At 30 seconds the remaining streams are cut. 200 clients see a broken connection mid-stream.
- If each client reconnects immediately, the surviving backends receive 200 new connections plus 200 replay requests within the same second.
- The fix is not "a longer deadline forever". It is a stated contract: a maximum drain of 30 seconds, clients must handle reconnect, and if the work genuinely cannot be interrupted, the application must checkpoint so a reconnect resumes rather than restarts.

**Worked example for (3): the half-applied configuration**

- New route: `api.example.com/v2/*` should go to pool `v2-pool`.
- 10 proxies. 5 apply it. 5 are wedged on a slow configuration fetch.
- A client makes 10 requests. Roughly 5 reach `v2-pool` and succeed; roughly 5 reach the old `v1-pool` and return `404`.
- The bug report reads "the API is flaky, about half the calls 404". That is one of the hardest classes of bug to diagnose, because nothing is consistently wrong.
- The fix is to make partial application *visible*: every proxy reports the configuration version it has actually applied, and the operation is not done until all 10 report the same version.

---

>   - Minimum primitives
>     - An immutable configuration snapshot per request
>     - A local eligible set with in-flight counts
>     - Health with hysteresis separate from administrative state
>     - A retry budget.

**What this says in plain words**
These are the four building blocks. If your answer contains all four, the design holds together. If it is missing one, there is a failure above that you have not covered. Taken one at a time:

1. **An immutable configuration snapshot per request.** When a request arrives, the proxy grabs the current routing rules as a frozen, read-only object and uses that one object for the whole life of the request. *Immutable* means cannot be changed after it is created.
   - **What breaks without it:** configuration updates mid-request. The request resolves route `R` against configuration version 41, then reads the backend list from version 42, where pool `P` no longer exists. You get a crash, or a request routed to a backend the new configuration deliberately removed. With a snapshot, version 42 applies to the *next* request, and the current one finishes cleanly on version 41.
   - **Worked example:** a configuration push happens at 10:00:00.000 while 10,000 requests are in flight. Without snapshots, up to 10,000 requests read a half-updated structure. With snapshots, exactly zero do; they all finish on the old version and request 10,001 starts on the new one.

2. **A local eligible set with in-flight counts.** Each proxy keeps its own small array of the backends it is currently willing to use, and next to each one, a count of how many requests it has personally sent there that have not finished yet.
   - *Local* means this proxy's own view, not a shared global truth.
   - **What breaks without it:** you either ask a central service which backend to use, adding a network round trip to every single request, or you filter the full 500-backend list on every request, which is 500 checks per request times 100,000 requests per second, or 50 million checks per second, per proxy.
   - **Worked example:** with a pre-built eligible array of 480 healthy backends, picking two at random is two array index lookups, on the order of 100 nanoseconds. Re-filtering 500 backends is roughly 500 comparisons, on the order of 2 microseconds — about 20x more work on the hot path of every request.

3. **Health with hysteresis, separate from administrative state.** Two ideas in one line:
   - *Hysteresis* means the rule for going bad is different from the rule for coming back. For example: 3 consecutive failures to remove a backend, but 2 consecutive successes to restore it. It prevents a backend from flapping in and out of the pool on alternate probes.
   - *Separate from administrative state* means "an operator said DRAIN" is stored in a different field from "the probe succeeded". A successful probe must never silently cancel an operator's drain order.
   - **What breaks without it:** a backend with a 50% failure rate joins and leaves the pool every 2 seconds, and each rejoin sends it a fresh burst of traffic it cannot handle. Or worse: you drain backend 7 for maintenance, its health probe passes at the next tick, and the proxy helpfully puts it back into rotation while an engineer is mid-upgrade on it.

4. **A retry budget.** A hard cap on how much retry traffic the proxy may generate, expressed as a fraction of real traffic — for example, retries may be at most 10% of admitted original requests.
   - **What breaks without it:** retries multiply load exactly when the system has least capacity.
   - **Worked example — the retry storm:**
     - Normal state: 10,000 requests per second across 4 backends, 2,500 each, ceiling 3,000 each.
     - One backend dies. The surviving 3 now get 3,333 each, already 11% above their tested ceiling of 3,000.
     - Requests to the dead backend fail. With unlimited retries and 3 attempts each, those 2,500 failed requests per second become 2,500 x 2 extra attempts = 5,000 extra requests per second onto the survivors.
     - Survivors now see (10,000 − 2,500 + 5,000) ÷ 3 = **4,167 requests per second each**, 39% over their ceiling. They start timing out.
     - Their timeouts generate more retries. Within a few seconds every backend is over its ceiling and the whole service is down, from a fault that removed only 25% of capacity.
     - With a 10% budget: extra retry traffic is capped at 1,000 requests per second, so survivors see (7,500 + 1,000) ÷ 3 = 2,833 each, **below** the 3,000 ceiling. 25% of requests fail, which is bad, and 75% succeed, which is the difference between a degradation and an outage.

---

> - 🗣 "Balancing is the easy part; the design is what the proxy does when its evidence about backends is stale, partial, or wrong."

**What this says in plain words**
Choosing a server from a list is trivial. The entire difficulty is that the proxy's information about those servers is always somewhat out of date, always incomplete, and sometimes simply incorrect.

**Why it matters**
This is the sentence that reframes the whole question. Most candidates answer "how do I distribute load", which is a 5-minute answer. The interviewer is asking "how do you behave under uncertainty", which is a 45-minute answer.

- What each of the three words means concretely
  - **Stale:** the in-flight count says backend 3 has 12 requests, but 4 of them finished 200 milliseconds ago and the completion handler has not run yet.
  - **Partial:** this proxy sees only its own traffic. It has 250 requests on backend 3; the other 9 proxies also have around 250 each. The proxy believes backend 3 has 250 in flight; backend 3 actually has 2,500.
  - **Wrong:** the health probe passes because `/healthz` returns `200 OK` from a static handler, while the backend's actual request path is failing 100% of the time on a broken dependency.

---

> - Opening script
>   - "I will start with an L7 HTTP reverse proxy, trace one request, and keep routing configuration separate from connection state."

**What this says in plain words**
Three commitments in one sentence:

1. **L7 HTTP reverse proxy.** The load balancer will read and understand the HTTP request — its host name, its path, its headers — rather than just shovelling bytes. A *reverse proxy* sits in front of servers and answers on their behalf; a *forward proxy* sits in front of clients.
2. **Trace one request.** You will walk the interviewer through the complete life of a single request, step by step, rather than describing boxes.
3. **Keep routing configuration separate from connection state.** Two different kinds of data live in the proxy: rules, which can be rebuilt from a database at any time, and live TCP connections, which cannot. You will not mix them.

**Why it matters**
The last clause is the one that earns points. Configuration is recoverable, connections are not. Every decision about failover, restart, and deployment in the rest of the answer follows from that distinction.

- Worked example
  - A proxy process is killed and restarted. It re-downloads the routing configuration in 500 milliseconds and is functionally identical to before.
  - The 1,200 client TCP connections it was holding are gone permanently. No other proxy can adopt them: the TCP sequence numbers, the TLS session keys, and the half-parsed request buffers existed only in that process's memory.
  - Therefore configuration loss is a 500-millisecond event and connection loss is a client-visible event. Design for them differently.

---

>   - "First I confirm the protocol, whether TLS terminates at the proxy, whether writes are retry-safe, and whether session affinity is required."

**What this says in plain words**
Before designing anything, ask four questions. Each one, answered differently, produces a different design:

1. **Protocol.** Is this HTTP, which the proxy can read, or raw TCP, which it cannot?
2. **TLS termination.** *TLS* is the encryption behind HTTPS. *Terminating* it at the proxy means the proxy decrypts the traffic, so it holds the private key and pays the CPU cost of the handshake, but it can then read the HTTP inside. Not terminating means the encrypted bytes pass straight through to the backend and the proxy can see only the connection, not the content.
3. **Retry safety of writes.** Can a request that changes data safely be sent twice? Only if the backend enforces idempotency.
4. **Session affinity.** Must every request from the same user land on the same backend?

**Why it matters**
Each answer removes or adds a whole subsystem. Guessing instead of asking means you may spend 20 minutes designing HTTP path routing for a service that turns out to be raw TCP with end-to-end encryption, where path routing is physically impossible.

---

>   - "Then I size in-flight requests, buffers, and probe traffic, and define the control-plane API for pools, routes, and drains."

**What this says in plain words**
Next, do the arithmetic — how many requests are simultaneously open, how much memory they occupy, and how much health-check traffic the fleet generates — and then define the management API that operators call to change things.

- The three sizing numbers, defined
  - **In-flight requests:** requests that have started and not yet finished, across the whole fleet at any instant.
  - **Buffers:** the memory the proxy holds per request while relaying bytes between client and backend.
  - **Probe traffic:** the health-check requests the proxies themselves generate. It is real load on the backends and it is easy to forget.
- The three API objects, defined
  - **Pool:** a named group of interchangeable backends, plus the policy for how to balance across them.
  - **Route:** a rule saying requests matching this host and path go to that pool.
  - **Drain:** an operation that tells the fleet to stop sending new work to one backend.

**Why it matters**
Saying these numbers out loud proves you know which resource actually runs out first. Saying the API objects out loud proves you have thought about who operates this thing at 3 a.m.

---

>   - "The deep dive is backend selection under skew, health with hysteresis, the retry contract, and draining."

**What this says in plain words**
The 15 minutes of genuine technical depth will cover four things: how to pick a backend when they are not equally loaded (*skew* means uneven distribution), how to decide a backend is sick without flapping, exactly when a retry is allowed, and how to remove a backend without cutting live traffic.

**Why it matters**
Announcing the deep-dive agenda up front lets the interviewer redirect you early. If they say "skip draining, I want to hear about configuration distribution", you have just saved yourself from spending 5 minutes on the wrong topic.

---

>   - "I will close with failure behavior when health evidence is unreliable, and what changes at ten times the load."

**What this says in plain words**
The final segment covers two things: what the proxy should do when it cannot trust its own health data, and which part of the system breaks first if traffic grows tenfold.

**Why it matters**
Both are senior signals. Junior answers assume the monitoring is correct. Senior answers plan for the monitoring being wrong. And "what breaks at 10x" tests whether you know which resource is the actual limit, rather than assuming everything scales linearly.

- Worked example of the 10x question
  - At 100,000 requests per second with 2 KB payloads, the fleet moves 400 MB/s of I/O, counting both legs. See the capacity section.
  - At 1,000,000 requests per second, that is 4 GB/s, which is 32 Gbit/s, which exceeds a single 25-Gbit network card.
  - But if the payloads are 200 bytes instead of 2 KB, bandwidth is only 3.2 Gbit/s and the limit is instead **packet rate**: 1,000,000 requests per second times roughly 4 packets each times 2 legs equals 8 million packets per second, which is where kernel networking, not bandwidth, becomes the wall.
  - The same 10x produces two completely different answers depending on payload size. That is why the source says to measure the limiting resource rather than quoting one requests-per-second number.

> - Clock note
>   - If the interviewer states 45 minutes, drop the intro to 2 minutes and compress each phase proportionally.

**What this says in plain words**
If you get 45 minutes instead of 60, cut the introduction first and shrink everything else by the same ratio. Whatever the interviewer says about timing overrides your plan.

**Worked example of proportional compression**

- 45 minutes is 75% of 60. After cutting the intro from 5 minutes to 2, you have 43 minutes to fit 55 minutes of plan, which is about 78%.
- Requirements: 6 minutes to 5. Estimates: 6 to 4. Architecture: 13 to 10. Deep dive: 15 to 12. Operations: 5 to 4. Wrap: 4 to 3. Questions: 6 to 5.
- The item that shrinks most is estimates, because you can state two numbers and their consequence in 4 minutes. The item that shrinks least is the deep dive, because that is what is being graded.

| L7 (layer 7) | Balancing at the application layer: the proxy parses HTTP, so it can see host, path, headers, and method. | The chosen design. |
| OSI layer | A seven-level model of networking. Layer 3 is IP, layer 4 is TCP or UDP, layer 7 is the application protocol such as HTTP. The L numbers come from here. | Explains what L4 and L7 are numbered after. |
| TCP connection | A long-lived, ordered, two-way byte channel between two machines, set up by a three-way handshake. | The unit an L4 balancer distributes, and the state a proxy cannot hand to a peer. |
| TLS | Transport Layer Security, the encryption underneath HTTPS. | Deciding where it is decrypted is one of the four opening questions. |
| TLS termination | Decrypting TLS at the proxy, so the proxy holds the private key and can read the HTTP inside. | Required for L7 routing on HTTPS traffic; costs CPU. |
| Round robin | Assigning requests to backends strictly in turn. | The naive design that the first break attacks. |
| Static backend list | A backend list that changes only when a human edits configuration and restarts the process. | Part of the naive design. |
| Health probe / health check | A request the proxy sends to a backend purely to ask whether it is alive. | The mechanism that removes bad backends, and the mechanism that can remove all of them. |
| Hysteresis | Using a different threshold to leave a state than to enter it, for example 3 failures to eject but 2 successes to readmit. | Stops a marginal backend flapping in and out of the pool. |
| Flapping | Rapidly alternating between healthy and unhealthy. | The failure hysteresis exists to prevent. |
| Administrative state | The operator's declared intent for a backend: ACTIVE, DRAINING, or DISABLED. Stored separately from observed health. | A passing probe must never undo an operator's drain. |
| Eligible set | The subset of a pool's backends this proxy is currently willing to send new requests to. | The array that selection samples from. |
| In-flight count | How many requests this proxy has sent to a given backend that have not finished yet. | The load signal used by power-of-two-choices selection. |
| Immutable snapshot | A frozen, read-only copy of the configuration, created once and never modified. | Guarantees one request sees one consistent configuration. |
| Configuration push | Distributing a new version of routing rules to the whole proxy fleet. | Landing on only half the fleet is the second break. |
| Drain | Telling the fleet to stop sending new work to a backend while letting existing work finish. | How you remove a backend for deployment without cutting traffic. |
| Connection draining | The full sequence: stop new assignments, let in-flight requests finish, close idle connections, force-close at a deadline. | What actually happens to live traffic during a deploy. |
| Retry budget | A cap on retry traffic as a fraction of real traffic, for example 10%. | Stops retries from turning a partial failure into a total one. |
| Retry storm | Retries multiplying after a failure until the retries themselves become the load that keeps the system down. | The worked example above, and the reason budgets exist. |
| Idempotency | A property where performing the same operation twice has the same effect as performing it once. | The only thing that makes retrying a `POST` safe. |
| `POST` | The HTTP method that normally means create or change something. Not safe to repeat by default. | The retry-safety question is about `POST`-like requests. |
| Little's Law | In a stable system, items in flight equals arrival rate times average time each item takes. | Used to compute 2,500/s x 5 s = 12,500 in flight. |
| Skew | Uneven distribution of load across backends that are supposed to be equal. | What power-of-two-choices selection exists to handle. |
| Control plane | The part of the system that manages configuration: admin API, validation, distribution. Not on the request path. | Separated from the data plane throughout this design. |
| Data plane | The part that actually carries user requests. | Must keep working when the control plane is down. |
| Blast radius | How much of the system one failure damages. | Used to judge whether a design's failures are contained. |
| Server-Sent Events (SSE) | A long-lived HTTP response that streams updates to a client for minutes or hours. | The example of traffic that a 30-second drain will cut. |
| Provenance | The record of where a claim came from. | Keeps the study file honest about what was actually reported. |

## Requirements — IC3 baseline

This is the section where you decide what you are building before you build it. The source compresses seven design-changing questions into a seven-row table. Each row is a fork in the road.

---

> - Numerical targets are interview assumptions unless an adjacent primary source states otherwise.

**What this says in plain words**
Every number in this file was invented by the author to make the design concrete. None of them were measured at Oracle. If a number sits next to a link to official documentation, that number came from the documentation; otherwise it is an assumption.

**Why it matters**
An interviewer will sometimes ask "where did 100,000 requests per second come from?" The correct answer is "I assumed it; if the real number is 10,000 the fleet is 10 proxies instead of 10 and the packet-rate concern goes away." The wrong answer is to imply you measured it.

---

> | Question | Assumed answer | Why it changes the design |
> | L4 or L7? | L7 HTTP reverse proxy; L4 forwarding as the alternative | Decides whether routing sees host and path and whether TLS terminates at the proxy |

**What this says in plain words**
The first question is whether the load balancer understands HTTP or only understands connections. The assumed answer is that it understands HTTP.

**This is the hardest idea in the file, so here it is mechanically.**

- **What actually travels on the wire**
  - When a browser talks to a server, the data is wrapped in layers, like envelopes inside envelopes.
    - Outermost: an Ethernet frame (layer 2), addressed to a network card.
    - Inside that: an IP packet (layer 3), addressed to an IP address such as `10.2.0.7`.
    - Inside that: a TCP segment (layer 4), addressed to a port such as `443`, and carrying a sequence number so bytes arrive in order.
    - Inside that: TLS records (layer 6-ish), which are encrypted.
    - Inside those, once decrypted: the HTTP request (layer 7) — `GET /v2/orders/914 HTTP/1.1`, `Host: api.example.com`, `Cookie: session=abc`.
- **What an L4 balancer can see**
  - Source IP and port, destination IP and port, the TCP flags, and the raw encrypted bytes.
  - It cannot see the path `/v2/orders/914`. It cannot see the `Host` header. It cannot see the method `GET`. Those are inside the encryption, and even without encryption they are inside a protocol the L4 balancer is not parsing.
  - Its entire decision is made **once per connection**, at the moment the TCP handshake arrives. After that it is a byte pipe: it rewrites addresses and forwards packets.
- **What an L7 balancer can see**
  - Everything above, plus the full HTTP request, because it terminated TLS and parsed the request.
  - Its decision is made **once per request**, and one connection can carry thousands of requests.

**The mechanical difference, stated as a table**

| Property | L4 | L7 |
|---|---|---|
| Decision granularity | Once per TCP connection | Once per HTTP request |
| Can route on host or path | No | Yes |
| Can retry a failed request | No — a failed connection is just a reset | Yes — it knows what the request was |
| Can add headers, rewrite paths, enforce per-request limits | No | Yes |
| Needs the TLS private key | No, encrypted bytes pass through | Yes, if it terminates TLS |
| CPU cost per request | Near zero; often kernel-level or hardware | Parsing plus TLS; measurable per request |
| Added latency | Tens of microseconds | Typically a millisecond or a few |
| Protocols it works for | Anything over TCP or UDP: databases, message brokers, custom binary | Only the protocols it can parse: HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2, gRPC |

**Worked example — the cost of the difference**

- 100,000 requests per second arrive over 5,000 long-lived connections. That is 20 requests per second per connection.
- **L4:** 5,000 balancing decisions total, made at connection setup. If one connection's client is a batch job sending 10,000 requests per second while another sends 1, the L4 balancer cannot tell and cannot rebalance. One backend gets 10,000 requests per second and another gets 1.
- **L7:** 100,000 balancing decisions per second, so the batch job's requests are spread over every backend in the pool. Load is even. The price is that the proxy parsed 100,000 HTTP requests and decrypted 100,000 request bodies.
- Cost estimate for the price: if parsing plus TLS record processing is 20 microseconds of CPU per request, 100,000 requests per second needs 2 CPU-seconds per second, meaning 2 full cores just to look at the traffic, before any forwarding.

**What breaks if you choose wrong**

→ You choose L4 for an HTTP API that needs `/v1` and `/v2` on different pools.  
⇒ The routing requirement is physically unimplementable; you would need a separate IP address and port per version.  
→ You choose L7 for a PostgreSQL connection pool.  
⇒ The proxy cannot parse the PostgreSQL wire protocol, so it has nothing to route on, and you paid for a parser you cannot use.

---

> | Does TLS terminate at the proxy? | Yes, with certificate references from the PKI service | Proxies hold private keys and handshake CPU becomes a sizing input |

**What this says in plain words**
The proxy will decrypt HTTPS traffic itself. It does not store the private keys in its own configuration database; it stores a *pointer* to them and fetches the real key material from a dedicated secrets service. Two consequences follow: the proxy becomes a machine worth attacking, and encryption CPU becomes part of how you size the fleet.

**Why it matters**, split into three:

1. **Routing needs it.** You cannot route on the URL path if the path is encrypted. Terminating TLS is the price of L7 routing on HTTPS.
2. **Key custody becomes your problem.** Every proxy that terminates TLS can, in principle, read every request in plaintext. That makes the proxy fleet part of the security boundary. Storing a reference rather than the key bytes means a database leak does not hand over the keys.
3. **The handshake dominates CPU, not the data.** A TLS handshake involves asymmetric cryptography, which is roughly a thousand times more expensive per operation than the symmetric encryption used for the actual data.

**Worked example — handshake CPU**

- Assume one RSA-2048 handshake costs about 1 millisecond of one CPU core, and symmetric encryption runs at about 1 GB/s per core.
- Steady state: 5,000 connections, each reused for 10 minutes. New handshakes = 5,000 ÷ 600 s ≈ **8 per second**, costing 0.008 cores. Invisible.
- Reconnect storm: one proxy of 10 dies, and its 5,000 clients all reconnect within 2 seconds. That is **2,500 handshakes per second**, costing 2.5 cores, spread over 9 surviving proxies.
- Meanwhile the data path at 400 MB/s costs about 0.4 cores.
- Conclusion the source draws: size CPU for the reconnect storm, not the steady state. The storm is 300x the steady handshake rate.

---

> | Are writes safe to retry? | Only where the backend enforces idempotency | Bounds the retry contract to connect failures and idempotent requests |

**What this says in plain words**
The proxy may only re-send a data-changing request if the backend has promised to detect and ignore duplicates. Otherwise the proxy is restricted to retrying failures where it knows for certain the request never started.

**Why it matters**
There are exactly three things the proxy can know after a failure, and they lead to different decisions:

| What the proxy observed | What it knows | Retry allowed? |
|---|---|---|
| TCP connect refused, or DNS failure | The request bytes were never sent. The backend did nothing. | Yes, always safe. |
| Request sent, connection reset before any response byte | Unknown. The backend may have processed it fully. | Only if the backend enforces idempotency. |
| Response headers started arriving, then the connection broke | The backend definitely did the work. Some of the response is already downstream. | No. You cannot replay a response that has partly left. |

**Worked example — why the middle row is the dangerous one**

- 1,000 `POST /transfers` per second. A backend is restarting.
- 200 requests per second hit the middle case: sent, no response.
- Without idempotency, retrying all 200 means roughly 200 duplicate transfers per second. Over the 15 seconds of the restart, that is **3,000 duplicate money movements**.
- With an idempotency key: the client sends `Idempotency-Key: 7f3a...`, the backend records it in the same database transaction as the transfer, and the retry returns the *original* result instead of transferring again. Duplicates: zero.
- Note where the work is. The proxy cannot provide this. The *backend* provides it, and the proxy is only allowed to retry because the backend does.

---

> | Is session affinity required? | No by default; opt-in per route | Affinity concentrates load and does not preserve backend memory |

**What this says in plain words**
By default, two requests from the same user may land on different backends. A route can opt in to pinning a user to one backend, but that is an exception, not the norm.

**Why it matters**, split into two:

1. **Affinity concentrates load.** Once a user is pinned, the balancer can no longer move their traffic. If one user is 30x heavier than average, the backend they are pinned to carries that weight until they leave.
2. **Affinity does not make your backend stateful safely.** People reach for affinity because a backend keeps something in memory — a session, a cache, a partially-built object. But if that backend dies, the memory is gone anyway. Affinity gives you the *illusion* of durable state without the durability.

**Worked example — the hot session**

- 10 backends, 10,000 requests per second, so 1,000 each with a ceiling of 1,500.
- Session affinity is on, keyed by user ID. 100,000 users, so about 10,000 users per backend, averaging 0.1 requests per second each. Even enough.
- One customer runs an automated integration that sends 800 requests per second from one session.
- Their backend now carries 1,000 + 800 = **1,800 requests per second** against a ceiling of 1,500. It saturates. The other 9 backends sit at 1,000.
- The balancer cannot help, because moving that user would violate affinity.
- Without affinity, those 800 requests per second split across 10 backends as 80 each, and every backend sits at 1,080 out of 1,500.

**Worked example — the illusion of preserved state**

- A shopping cart is held in backend 4's memory, and affinity keeps the user on backend 4.
- Backend 4 is restarted for a deploy at 02:00.
- The cart is gone. The user sees an empty cart regardless of affinity.
- The actual fix is to put the cart in Redis or a database, at which point affinity is no longer needed at all.

---

> | Fleet shape? | `10` proxies, `500` backends, one region, multiple fault domains | Sets probe traffic and how much capacity one failure domain removes |

**What this says in plain words**
Assume 10 proxy machines in front of 500 application servers, all in one geographic region, but spread across several independent failure zones. A *fault domain* is a group of machines that can fail together because they share a power feed, a rack, or a network switch.

**Why it matters**, split into two:

1. **It determines probe traffic.** Probe count is proxies x backends. 10 x 500 = 5,000 probe targets, and at one probe every 2 seconds that is 2,500 probes per second. If the fleet were 100 proxies and 5,000 backends, it would be 250,000 probes per second, which is real load and would force a different health design.
2. **It determines your capacity headroom.** If the 500 backends sit in 3 fault domains, losing one domain removes about 167 backends, or 33% of capacity.

**Worked example — headroom arithmetic**

- 500 backends, 3 fault domains, 167 per domain.
- Peak load 100,000 requests per second, so each backend handles 200 requests per second at peak.
- Lose one domain: 333 backends remain, so each must handle 300 requests per second.
- If a backend's tested ceiling is 250 requests per second, the design fails at exactly the moment a domain is lost.
- To survive it, you must run at 2/3 utilisation or lower in normal times: 500 backends each at 200 requests per second against a ceiling of 300. That is the concrete meaning of "keep enough capacity to lose one planned failure domain".

---

> | Who distributes connections to proxies? | A redundant front door, managed or ECMP/VIP | Keeps the proxy fleet horizontally scalable without owning routing |

**What this says in plain words**
Something has to spread client connections across the 10 proxies, because clients only know one address. That something is outside your design. It could be a cloud provider's managed load balancer, or a network-level mechanism.

- **The two network-level mechanisms named**
  - **VIP (virtual IP):** one IP address that is not permanently bound to one machine. Whichever proxy currently "owns" it answers for it, and ownership can move on failure. Clients see one stable address.
  - **ECMP (equal-cost multi-path):** the router has several equally good paths to the same destination address and hashes each connection's 5-tuple (source IP, source port, destination IP, destination port, protocol) to pick one. Every packet of one connection hashes the same way, so the connection stays on one proxy.

**Why it matters**
If you design this layer yourself, you have quietly taken on router configuration, failover detection, and IP address management — a second full system — and you will run out of clock. Naming it as a dependency and moving on is the correct scope decision.

**Worked example — what ECMP does when a proxy dies**

- 10 proxies, ECMP hashing over 10 next-hops, 5,000 connections spread roughly 500 each.
- Proxy 7 fails. The router now has 9 next-hops.
- Naive ECMP re-hashes everything modulo 9 instead of modulo 10. Roughly 90% of the *surviving* connections land on a different proxy than before, and since the new proxy has no TCP state for them, those connections break too.
- One proxy failure (10% of capacity) caused roughly 90% of connections to reset. This is the same "modulo rehash" problem that consistent hashing solves — see the selection section.
- Modern implementations use resilient hashing to avoid this, which is exactly why the source says to lean on a managed or well-implemented front door rather than build one.

---

> | Multi-tenant? | Yes; routes and pools are tenant-owned resources | Configuration is tenant-scoped and shared ejection has a blast radius |

**What this says in plain words**
Several different customers' configurations live in the same load balancer fleet. Every route and every pool belongs to exactly one tenant. *Tenant* means an isolated customer or account.

**Why it matters**, split into two:

1. **Configuration must be scoped.** Tenant A must not be able to point a route at tenant B's pool, and must not be able to see tenant B's backend addresses. That is why every table in the data model has `tenant_id` in its primary key.
2. **Shared resources create shared blast radius.** The proxies' CPU, memory, and buffers are shared. If tenant A's slow clients occupy all the buffers, tenant B's requests fail, even though tenant B did nothing wrong. This is called the noisy-neighbour problem.

**Worked example — the noisy neighbour**

- A proxy has 4 GB of buffer memory, and each slow connection holds 32 KB.
- 4 GB ÷ 32 KB = 131,072 connections' worth of buffers.
- Tenant A has a mobile client bug that opens connections and reads responses at 1 KB/s. Each response of 100 KB then takes 100 seconds, and the buffer is held the whole time.
- Tenant A generates 2,000 such connections per second. In 65 seconds they hold 130,000 buffers and the proxy is out of memory.
- Tenant B, with completely normal traffic, starts failing.
- The fix named later in the source is a per-tenant cap: no tenant may hold more than, say, 20% of buffer memory. Tenant A then fails at 26,000 buffers, and tenants B through E keep working.

---

> - Functional scope
>   - Route by host and path.
>   - Balance across eligible backends.
>   - Remove unhealthy backends with hysteresis.
>   - Drain connections for deployments.
>   - Apply validated configuration without restarting the fleet.
>   - Network layer 4 and layer 7 are unrelated to the depth labels in this file.

**What this says in plain words**
Six statements of what the system must do. Taken one at a time:

1. **Route by host and path.** Look at `Host: api.example.com` and path `/v2/orders`, and use them to choose which pool of backends serves this request.
2. **Balance across eligible backends.** Within the chosen pool, pick one of the backends that is currently usable.
3. **Remove unhealthy backends with hysteresis.** Take sick backends out of rotation, using different thresholds for removal and restoration so they do not flap.
4. **Drain connections for deployments.** Support taking one backend out gracefully, so a code deploy does not kill live requests.
5. **Apply validated configuration without restarting the fleet.** New routing rules must take effect in a running process. Restarting to pick up config would drop every connection on that proxy, every time anyone changed anything.
6. **The L4/L7 naming collision.** The letters "L4" in "layer 4" have nothing to do with the old level labels "L4/L5" used elsewhere in this repository. This note exists purely to stop that confusion.

**What breaks without (5), concretely**

- 10 proxies, 5,000 connections each. A restart-to-reload policy means every configuration change resets 50,000 connections.
- Teams change routes maybe 20 times a day.
- That is 1,000,000 connection resets per day caused by nothing but configuration edits, against a service that has no actual faults.
- With hot reload, the number is zero: a new immutable snapshot is built in memory and the pointer to the active snapshot is swapped atomically.

---

> | Requirement | Target | Enforced by |
> | Peak throughput | `100,000 requests/s` | Fleet sizing on the measured limiting resource; front-door spread |

**What this says in plain words**
The fleet must handle 100,000 requests per second at peak. The way you guarantee it is by measuring which resource runs out first and buying enough of that, plus relying on the front door to spread connections evenly.

**Why it matters**
A target without an enforcing mechanism is a wish. The mechanism here has two halves: correct sizing, and even spread. Both are required.

**Worked example — why spread matters as much as size**

- 10 proxies, each tested to 15,000 requests per second, so nominal capacity is 150,000 requests per second against a 100,000 requests per second target: 50% headroom.
- But the front door balances *connections*, not requests. Suppose connection counts are even at 500 each, while request rates per connection vary 5x.
- Proxy 3 draws the heavy connections and sees 24,000 requests per second. It is 60% over its ceiling and its latency climbs, while proxy 8 sits at 6,000.
- Fleet capacity on paper was 150,000. Usable capacity, given skew, was about 100,000 x (15,000 ÷ 24,000) ≈ 62,500 requests per second before the first proxy degrades.
- The lesson: size for the hottest instance, not the average.

---

> | Backend inventory | `500 backends` across pools | Bounded eligible-array snapshots per pool |

**What this says in plain words**
There are 500 backend servers in total, divided into pools. The way this stays manageable is that each pool's usable backends are kept in a small fixed-size array that the proxy maintains in advance.

**Why it matters**
Selection has to be constant-time. If choosing a backend required scanning 500 entries, the cost of selection would grow with the fleet. With a pre-filtered array, the cost is the same whether there are 5 backends or 5,000.

- Worked example
  - Pool `orders` has 120 members; 6 are ejected and 2 are draining, leaving 112 eligible.
  - The eligible array holds those 112 in slots 0 to 111, with `eligible_count = 112`.
  - Selecting two random backends is `array[rand(112)]` twice. Two operations, independent of pool size.
  - When a backend is ejected, a *new* array of 111 is built and the pointer swapped. The cost of maintaining the array is paid on health changes, which happen a few times a minute, not on requests, which happen 100,000 times a second.

---

> | Added proxy latency | p99 < `5 ms` excluding backend execution | Constant-time selection; bounded buffering; no per-request coordination |

**What this says in plain words**
The proxy itself may add at most 5 milliseconds to 99 out of every 100 requests, not counting the time the backend spends working. Three mechanisms guarantee it: selection is constant-time, memory per request is capped, and the proxy never asks anybody else's permission to route a request.

- **What p50 and p99 mean**
  - Sort all request latencies for a period. The **p50** (median) is the value half of them are below. The **p99** is the value 99% are below.
  - p99 is the number that matters because a page that makes 100 backend calls will hit the p99 case roughly once per page load. Your p99 is your typical user's experience.

**Worked example — why p99 and not average**

- 100,000 requests. 99,000 take 1 millisecond; 1,000 take 200 milliseconds because they waited on a lock.
- Average = (99,000 x 1 + 1,000 x 200) ÷ 100,000 = 2.99 milliseconds. Looks fine.
- p99 = 200 milliseconds. The dashboard showing the average is hiding a 200x problem.
- A page that fans out to 100 services has a roughly 1 − 0.99^100 = **63% chance** of hitting at least one 200-millisecond call. Nearly two thirds of page loads are slow, while the average says everything is fine.

**Why the third mechanism — "no per-request coordination" — is the important one**

- Suppose selection asked a central coordinator "who is least loaded?" over the network.
- Even a fast intra-datacentre round trip is about 0.5 milliseconds, and that is the *best* case.
- At p99 under load, a coordinator round trip is easily 5 to 20 milliseconds, which alone blows the 5-millisecond budget.
- Additionally, 100,000 requests per second against one coordinator is 100,000 queries per second that must itself never fail — you have moved the availability problem, not solved it.
- This is the single strongest argument for local, randomized selection.

---

> | Failure detection | Within `10 s` under normal scheduling | Probe interval `2 s`, timeout `0.5 s`, three failures to eject |

**What this says in plain words**
A dead backend must be noticed within 10 seconds. The arithmetic that achieves it: probe every 2 seconds, give each probe half a second to answer, and remove the backend after 3 consecutive failed probes.

**Worked example — where the 10 seconds goes**

- Worst case: the backend dies immediately *after* a successful probe at t = 0.
- Next probe fires at t = 2.0, times out at t = 2.5. Failure 1.
- Probe at t = 4.0, times out at 4.5. Failure 2.
- Probe at t = 6.0, times out at 6.5. Failure 3 → ejected at **t = 6.5 s**.
- Best case: the backend dies just before a probe, so failures land at 0.5, 2.5, 4.5 → ejected at **t = 4.5 s**.
- So the honest detection range is 4.5 to 6.5 seconds, and the 10-second target leaves roughly 3.5 seconds of slack for a delayed thread or a congested network.

**What it costs to make this faster**

- Halve the interval to 1 second: detection becomes 2.25 to 3.25 seconds, but probe traffic doubles from 2,500 to 5,000 probes per second.
- Drop to 1 failure instead of 3: detection becomes 0.5 to 2.5 seconds, but a single dropped packet now ejects a healthy backend. At 500 backends and a 0.1% packet loss rate, you would spuriously eject roughly 2.5 backends per second.
- This tradeoff — speed of detection against false positives — is the entire reason for the 3-failure threshold.

---

> | Regional request availability | `99.95%` | Redundant front door and proxy capacity sufficient after the largest planned fault-domain loss; cached configuration |

**What this says in plain words**
Out of every 10,000 requests, at most 5 may fail. Three things deliver that: the front door has no single point of failure, the fleet still has enough capacity after losing its largest failure zone, and proxies keep serving from cached configuration when the configuration service is down.

**Worked example — what 99.95% permits**

- 99.95% availability leaves 0.05% failures.
- At 100,000 requests per second, that is 50 failed requests per second, continuously.
- Over 30 days, 99.95% allows about **21.6 minutes** of total downtime.
- One 20-minute outage consumes essentially the entire month's budget. This is why the design spends so much effort on not turning small faults into big ones.

---

> | Drain deadline | At most `30 s` before terminating remaining connections | Drain operation with a deadline and forced close |

**What this says in plain words**
When a backend is being removed, it gets at most 30 seconds for its existing work to finish. After that, whatever is still open is cut.

**Why it matters**
A deadline is what makes a deploy predictable. Without one, a single long-lived stream can block a deployment indefinitely.

- Worked example
  - Rolling deploy of 500 backends, 10 at a time, 50 batches.
  - With a 30-second drain, the worst case is 50 x 30 s = 25 minutes of drain time for the deploy.
  - Without a deadline, one backend holding a 4-hour stream blocks its batch for 4 hours and the deploy never completes.
  - The tradeoff is explicit: a bounded, sometimes-rude 30 seconds beats an unbounded, polite forever.

---

> | Retry amplification | Retries at most `10%` of admitted original requests | Per-proxy retry budget |

**What this says in plain words**
Retry traffic can never exceed one tenth of real traffic. Each proxy tracks its own ratio and refuses to retry once it is over budget.

**Worked example — the budget in operation**

- A proxy admits 10,000 original requests per second. Its budget is 1,000 retries per second.
- Normal failure rate 0.1% = 10 failures per second = 10 retries per second. Budget usage 1%.
- A backend fails, and the failure rate jumps to 20%: 2,000 failures per second want retries.
- The proxy retries the first 1,000 per second and returns the original failure for the other 1,000, with a hint to the client to retry later.
- Backends therefore see at most 11,000 requests per second instead of 12,000, and crucially the number is *bounded* regardless of how bad the failure gets.
- Compare with no budget: at a 100% failure rate and 2 retries each, backends would see 30,000 requests per second — 3x normal load at the moment they are least able to serve it.

---

> | Configuration convergence | Acknowledged fleet-wide within `60 s` | Versioned push; `appliedVersion` visible per proxy |

**What this says in plain words**
A configuration change must reach every proxy within a minute, and you can see per proxy which version it has actually applied.

**Why it matters**
Convergence is observable, not assumed. The mechanism is that each proxy reports `appliedVersion`, and the operation is complete only when every proxy reports the new number.

- Worked example
  - Operator pushes version 42 at 10:00:00.
  - At 10:00:05, 8 of 10 proxies report `appliedVersion: 42`; two still report 41.
  - `GET /v1/operations/{id}` shows `acked_proxies: 8, total_proxies: 10`. The operation is `RUNNING`, not `SUCCEEDED`.
  - At 10:00:41 the last proxy acknowledges. Now it is `SUCCEEDED`.
  - Without `appliedVersion`, the API would have returned success at 10:00:00 and the operator would debug the "flaky 404s" from the earlier example with no idea that two proxies were behind.

---

> - Invariants
>   - A request uses one internally consistent route/configuration snapshot.
>   - No new request is deliberately assigned to a drained backend.
>   - Retries cannot assume an unobserved response means the backend did no work.
>   - Numerical targets above are interview assumptions unless a reference explicitly supplies them.

**What this says in plain words**
An *invariant* is a statement that must be true at all times, no matter what else happens. There are three real ones here plus a disclaimer:

1. **One request, one configuration version.** A request never reads half of version 41 and half of version 42.
2. **Drained backends get no new work.** "Deliberately" is doing work in that sentence: a race can still send one request to a backend that started draining a microsecond ago; what is forbidden is *choosing* a known-draining backend.
3. **Silence is not proof of inaction.** If the proxy did not see a response, it may not conclude the backend did nothing.

**Why each one matters**

- Invariant 1 prevents the crash-and-misroute class of bug from the configuration snapshot explanation.
- Invariant 2 is what makes a deploy safe. If it does not hold, "drain" means nothing and every deploy is a small outage.
- Invariant 3 is what prevents double charges. It is the most commonly violated invariant in real systems, because "it timed out, just retry" feels obviously correct and is obviously wrong.

**Worked example of the race in invariant 2**

- At t = 0.000000, the proxy selects backend 7 from the eligible array.
- At t = 0.000003, a drain notification for backend 7 arrives on another thread and a new eligible array without backend 7 is published.
- At t = 0.000010, the request is dispatched to backend 7.
- Invariant 2 is not violated, because the selection was made against a snapshot in which backend 7 was legitimately eligible.
- The system copes because backend 7, once draining, still *serves* in-flight requests for the drain window. The drain is graceful precisely so that this race is harmless.

---

> - Non-goals
>   - Reimplement Internet routing.
>   - Migrate a live TCP connection after proxy process loss.
>   - Automatically retry arbitrary application writes.

**What this says in plain words**
Three things you are explicitly not doing, and saying so is part of the answer:

1. **Not rebuilding Internet routing.** You are not designing BGP, anycast, or DNS. The front door is a dependency.
2. **Not migrating live connections.** If a proxy process dies, its TCP connections die with it. You will not attempt to hand a half-open connection to a different machine.
3. **Not retrying arbitrary writes.** The proxy will not silently re-send data-changing requests that have no idempotency guarantee.

**Why stating non-goals is worth clock time**

- Each one closes a rabbit hole the interviewer might otherwise push you into.
- Each one is also a *defensible* claim, not laziness. Number 2 in particular is technically justified.

**Worked example — why connection migration is not merely hard but essentially impractical here**

- To move a live TCP connection to a different machine you would need to transfer, atomically and before the next packet arrives: the send and receive sequence numbers, the unacknowledged send buffer, the congestion window, the TLS session keys and record sequence counters, and any partially parsed HTTP request.
- Packets arrive every few microseconds on a busy connection. The transfer would need to complete within that window, across a network, for all 5,000 connections the dead proxy owned, at a moment when the proxy is already dead and cannot send you its state.
- The honest answer is that the client reconnects. Given a 200-millisecond reconnect and 5,000 connections, the user-visible cost is one failed request each — vastly cheaper than the machinery to avoid it.

## Capacity and consequence — IC3 baseline

The rule this section follows is stated in the repository's authoring rules: **every estimate must end with the decision it forces.** A number that does not change a design choice is a number you should delete. There are four estimates here, and each exists to make one sizing decision.

---

> - Request/response bytes
>   - At an assumed combined `2 KB/request`, traffic is `200 MB/s`.

**What this says in plain words**
Assume an average request plus its response add up to 2 kilobytes of data. At 100,000 requests per second, that is 200 megabytes of data every second.

**The arithmetic, shown**

- 2 KB × 100,000 per second = 200,000 KB/s = 200 MB/s.
- In bits, which is how network cards are rated: 200 MB/s × 8 = 1.6 gigabits per second.

---

> - Each payload crosses both a client-facing and backend-facing leg.
> - Account for both legs when sizing NIC and packet-processing capacity.
> - Decision: size on `400 MB/s` of proxy I/O, not `200 MB/s`, and treat packet rate as the likely first limit for small requests.

**What this says in plain words**
Three separate ideas:

1. **A proxy touches every byte twice.** The request arrives from the client, and the proxy sends it on to a backend. The response comes back from the backend, and the proxy sends it to the client. The same bytes cross the proxy's network card twice.
2. **So size on 400 MB/s, not 200 MB/s.** Doubling is not pessimism; it is counting correctly. 200 MB/s of user traffic is 400 MB/s of proxy network work, or 3.2 gigabits per second.
3. **For small requests, packets run out before bytes do.** A card rated at 10 gigabits per second looks like enormous headroom against 3.2 Gb/s. But the cost of handling a packet is mostly fixed regardless of how big the packet is, so the limit you hit is packets per second, not bytes per second.

**Worked example of the packet limit**

- Suppose the average request is small: 500 bytes, not 2 KB. Then bytes fall to 50 MB/s — a quarter of the load.
- Packet count does not fall with it. A 500-byte HTTP request is still one data packet each way, plus acknowledgements. Call it 4 packets per request across both legs, conservatively.
- 100,000 requests/s × 4 packets = 400,000 packets per second fleet-wide. Across 10 proxies that is 40,000 packets/s each.
- A commodity kernel network stack costs roughly a few microseconds of CPU per packet. At 3 microseconds, 40,000 packets/s = 0.12 seconds of CPU per second, or 12% of one core — just to move packets, before any HTTP parsing.
- Scale the request rate 10× and that becomes 120% of one core on packet handling alone. That is why the source says packet rate is "the likely first limit for small requests": the bandwidth graph looks calm while the CPU graph saturates.

**What breaks without this**
You size the fleet from the bandwidth figure, provision for 1.6 Gb/s, and then find in production that proxies are CPU-bound at 40% of the bandwidth you planned for. The fix is more machines, bought during an outage.

---

> - Proxy concurrency
>   - `100,000 requests/s × 0.1 s average duration = 10,000 requests in flight`.

**What this says in plain words**
If 100,000 requests arrive every second and each one takes 0.1 seconds to finish, then at any instant 10,000 of them are unfinished and sitting inside the proxy fleet.

**Why it matters**
This is Little's Law, and it is the single most useful estimate in the file. It converts a *rate* (requests per second, which costs nothing to hold) into a *population* (requests in flight, which costs memory, file descriptors, and task slots). Memory is sized on the population, never on the rate.

- Little's Law: in-flight = arrival rate × time in system.
- 100,000/s × 0.1 s = 10,000 in flight. Across 10 proxies: 1,000 in flight per proxy.
- If backend latency degrades from 0.1 s to 0.5 s while arrivals stay at 100,000/s, in flight jumps to 50,000 — five times the memory, with no change in traffic. A latency problem becomes a memory problem automatically.

---

> - At `32 KB` of bounded buffering per request, buffers alone require about `320 MB` fleet-wide.
> - Decision: bound request bodies and stream by default
>   - A full-buffering default multiplies memory by body size.

**What this says in plain words**
Two ideas:

1. **The memory bill.** Give each in-flight request a fixed 32-kilobyte buffer for bytes in transit. 10,000 in-flight requests × 32 KB = 320 megabytes across the fleet, or 32 MB per proxy at 10 proxies. That is comfortable.
2. **Streaming versus buffering is the decision the number forces.** *Streaming* means the proxy reads a chunk, writes it onward, and reuses the buffer, so memory per request stays at 32 KB no matter how large the body is. *Full buffering* means the proxy holds the entire body before forwarding any of it, so memory per request equals body size.

**Worked example of why buffering is the dangerous default**

- Streaming, 32 KB per request, 10,000 in flight: 320 MB fleet-wide. Fine.
- Full buffering with 10 MB uploads allowed and only 1% of requests being uploads: 100 concurrent uploads × 10 MB = 1 GB, on top of the 320 MB. Worse than the entire streaming budget, from 1% of traffic.
- Full buffering with unbounded body size: one client streaming a 2 GB body is one process death. No rate limit prevents this, because it is a single request.
- Hence *two* controls, not one: stream by default, and cap body size so the worst case is arithmetic you have already done.

---

> - Health probes
>   - `10 proxies × 500 backends ÷ 2 s = 2,500 probes/s`.

**What this says in plain words**
Every proxy checks every backend independently. With 10 proxies, 500 backends, and one check each every 2 seconds: 10 × 500 = 5,000 checks per cycle, and a 2-second cycle means 2,500 checks per second arriving at the backend fleet.

**Why it matters**
Health checking is traffic, and it grows as the *product* of two numbers, so it grows quadratically as you scale both sides.

- Now: 2,500 probes/s against 100,000 real requests/s = 2.5% overhead. Acceptable.
- Per backend: 2,500 ÷ 500 = 5 probes/s each, one from each of 10 proxies every 2 seconds. Trivial.
- Scale to 100 proxies and 5,000 backends: 100 × 5,000 ÷ 2 = 250,000 probes/s. Each backend now takes 50 probes/s, and the fleet spends more requests on health checks than a small service serves in total.

---

> - Jitter probe schedules to avoid synchronized bursts.

**What this says in plain words**
*Jitter* means adding a small random offset to each timer so checks spread out instead of firing together.

**What breaks without it**
Without jitter, 10 proxies that all started at the same moment probe backend 7 within the same millisecond, then go quiet for 2 seconds. The backend sees a spike of 10 simultaneous probes rather than a smooth 5 per second. With 500 backends and aligned schedules the fleet sees a 5,000-probe spike every 2 seconds — a load pattern that looks like an attack and distorts every latency percentile you measure.

- With jitter: each proxy picks its next probe time as 2 s plus or minus up to 0.2 s at random. The 10 probes for backend 7 arrive spread across a 400-millisecond window. Peak concurrency at the backend is 1 or 2 instead of 10.

---

> - Decision: keep per-proxy probing at this scale
>   - Share coarse health only when `proxies × backends` makes probe traffic a visible backend cost.

**What this says in plain words**
At 2,500 probes/s, let every proxy keep its own independent opinion about every backend. Only when the product gets large should you move to a design where a few dedicated checkers probe and publish results.

**Why it matters — the tradeoff being bought**

- Independent probing makes each proxy's view *first-hand*. If proxy 3 cannot reach backend 7 because of a network problem affecting only proxy 3, proxy 3 stops sending traffic there and the other nine correctly continue. Shared health cannot express that, because it has one global answer to a per-path question.
- Shared health saves probe traffic but adds a dependency: the health-sharing service is now on the path to correct routing, and its outage blinds everyone at once.
- The decision rule is a number, not a preference: keep per-proxy probing while probe traffic is a small percentage of real traffic. 2.5% is small. At 20% the calculus flips.

---

> - TLS
>   - Handshake rate depends on connection reuse.
>   - Benchmark both steady keep-alive and reconnect storms.
>   - Decision: do not infer fleet size from request rate alone
>     - A reconnect storm after a proxy loss is the CPU sizing case.

**What this says in plain words**
TLS is Transport Layer Security, the encryption in HTTPS. Setting up an encrypted connection — *the handshake* — is expensive; sending data over an established one is cheap. So the CPU cost of TLS depends entirely on how often new connections are made, which the request rate does not tell you.

**Worked example — the same request rate, two wildly different CPU bills**

- Case A, steady state with keepalive. 8,000 client connections, each reused for many requests, each reconnecting roughly every 15 minutes. New handshakes = 8,000 ÷ 900 s ≈ 9 per second. At about 1 millisecond of CPU per handshake that is 9 milliseconds of CPU per second — under 1% of one core.
- Case B, one proxy of ten dies. That proxy owned about 800 connections; they all break and reconnect within a couple of seconds, spread across the surviving nine proxies. That is roughly 400 handshakes per second per surviving proxy for a two-second burst, against a steady-state figure near 1 per second. A 400× spike.
- At 1 ms of CPU each, 400 handshakes/s consumes 0.4 of a core on handshakes alone, on a machine already running at its normal utilisation, while also absorbing 11% more requests than before.
- **The decision:** size the fleet so the reconnect storm following a single proxy loss still fits. Size on the steady-state handshake rate instead and the first proxy failure cascades — survivors saturate on handshake CPU, reconnects time out, clients retry, and the storm sustains itself.

**Why "benchmark both" is the actual instruction**
You cannot compute handshake CPU from first principles. It depends on the key type (RSA 2048 costs several times more than ECDSA P-256), the library, whether the CPU has AES instructions, and whether session resumption is enabled. The source gives no number for TLS CPU on purpose — it tells you to measure two named scenarios and size on the worse one.

## Architecture and flow — IC3 baseline

The source draws the diagram before explaining anything. That ordering is deliberate: the interviewer should be able to see the boxes while you talk about them.

---

> ```text
> Data plane (per request):
> Clients -> redundant front-door distribution -> L7 proxy fleet -> backend pools
>                                                 |
>                             local immutable route snapshot
>                             local health and in-flight state
>
> Control plane (per configuration change):
> Admin API -> config DB -> validator -> canary rollout -> proxy acknowledgments
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
There are two completely separate paths through the system.

1. **The data plane** is what a user request travels. Client → front door → one proxy → one backend. It runs 100,000 times per second.
2. **The control plane** is what a configuration change travels. Admin call → database → validator → gradual rollout → confirmations back. It runs maybe 10 times per day.
3. **The two things hanging off the proxy** are what each proxy keeps in its own memory: the route snapshot (which backends exist right now) and its health and in-flight counters (what it personally believes about each backend, and how much work it personally has outstanding).

**Why the split is the most important idea in the diagram**
Because it decides what an outage of the control plane does to users: nothing. The proxy already has its snapshot in memory. It does not consult the database per request, so the database being down does not stop a single user request.

- Worked example: the configuration database is down for 60 minutes. During that hour the fleet serves 100,000 × 3,600 = 360 million requests normally. The only thing that fails is the handful of admin edits attempted in that hour, which return an error the operator can retry.
- Compare the alternative, where the proxy reads routes from the database per request. At 100,000 requests/s that is 100,000 database reads per second, and a database outage is a total outage. The same design decision that removes a scaling problem also removes a whole failure mode.

---

> - Each proxy owns live connection state.
>   - Persistent configuration can be rebuilt.
>   - Established TCP/TLS sessions cannot be reconstructed by another proxy just from configuration.
>   - Cost: losing a proxy resets its connections
>     - Clients must reconnect and in-flight writes become ambiguous.

**What this says in plain words**
Split into three claims:

1. **Configuration is recoverable state.** If proxy 4 restarts, it re-reads the configuration and is back to normal. Nothing was lost, because the truth lives in the database.
2. **Connections are not recoverable state.** A live TCP connection with TLS on top is a pile of per-machine numbers — sequence numbers, unacknowledged bytes, encryption keys, record counters. Proxy 5 cannot adopt proxy 4's connection, because it does not have those numbers and could not install them in the kernel in time even if it did.
3. **So the price is stated openly:** when a proxy dies, every connection it held dies, and any request that was mid-flight has an unknown outcome.

**What "ambiguous" means concretely**

- A client sends `POST /orders` and the proxy forwards it to backend 12. The proxy is killed before the response comes back.
- Three possibilities, indistinguishable from the client's side: the request never reached backend 12; it reached it and failed; it reached it and *succeeded*, and only the response was lost.
- The order may or may not exist. This is why the retry contract exists and why it depends on idempotency: without a server-enforced idempotency key, a blind retry here creates a second order.

---

> - A redundant front door distributes connections across proxy instances.
>   - Managed load balancing is a valid deployment choice.
>   - Discuss VIP failover or ECMP only if the interviewer asks how to build that layer.
>   - Cost: the front door balances connections, not requests, so proxies can still see uneven request load.

**What this says in plain words**
Something has to spread incoming connections across the 10 proxies, because the proxies are the thing being made redundant and a single entry point would defeat that. In a cloud you use the provider's own load balancer for this. You only descend into how that layer is built if asked.

**Why the cost line is the sharp part**
The front door hands out *connections*. The system is measured in *requests*. Those are different units, and the mismatch is a real imbalance.

- Worked example: 8,000 connections spread perfectly across 10 proxies gives 800 each. Perfect by the front door's measure.
- But one client holds 50 of those connections and sends 400 requests per second down each of them, while a typical client sends 5 per second. If those 50 connections happen to land mostly on proxies 2 and 3, proxies 2 and 3 carry several times the request load of proxy 7, with a connection count that looks identical.
- This is exactly why the proxy still needs its own load-aware backend selection. The front door's fairness does not propagate downward.

---

> - The configuration control plane validates and versions routes and pools.
>   - Last-known configuration preserves forwarding during a configuration-service outage.
>   - Expired credentials or a required security revocation can still force rejection.
>   - Cost: a `202` on a change means accepted, not applied
>     - Convergence is observable, not instant.

**What this says in plain words**
Four ideas:

1. **Validated and versioned.** Every configuration change is checked for legality before publication, and each published result carries a version number, so "which configuration is this proxy running" has an exact answer.
2. **Last-known configuration keeps serving.** Covered above: an outage of the control plane does not stop request forwarding.
3. **The one exception is credentials.** A TLS certificate that expires during the outage cannot be replaced, so those connections start failing even though routing still works. Likewise, if a certificate must be revoked for security, "keep serving from the old snapshot" is the wrong behaviour.
4. **`202 Accepted` is a promise to try, not a report of success.** HTTP `202` means the request was accepted for asynchronous processing. The change is now moving through validation and canary rollout across 10 proxies, which takes time.

**Worked example of why `202` matters to the operator**

- At 10:00:00 an operator calls `POST /backends/b7:drain` and gets `202` with operation ID `op-91`.
- At 10:00:01, 2 of 10 proxies have applied it. 8 proxies are still sending new requests to backend b7.
- At 10:00:04 all 10 have acknowledged. Only now is it true that b7 receives no new work.
- If the API had returned `200 OK` instead, the operator would reasonably have powered off b7 at 10:00:01 and killed live requests on 8 proxies. The status code is a safety mechanism, and `GET /operations/op-91` is how the operator learns when the claim is actually true.

---

> - Backends own their own admission limits.
>   - The proxy bounds in-flight work per backend
>   - The backend still needs its own queue limits.
>   - Cost: two limits must be tuned together or the stricter one silently dominates.

**What this says in plain words**
Two places enforce "do not overload this backend": the proxy limits how much it will send, and the backend limits how much it will accept. Both are needed, and they interact.

**Why both are needed**

- The proxy's limit is per proxy, so it cannot see the total. 10 proxies each capped at 30 in-flight per backend permits 300 in flight at a backend that can handle 100. Only the backend knows the true total.
- The backend's limit alone is also insufficient: without a proxy-side bound, the proxy keeps piling requests onto a stalled backend and pays the memory and timeout cost for work that will be rejected anyway.

**Worked example of "the stricter one silently dominates"**

- Backend b7 can serve 100 concurrent requests. Its own admission limit is set to 100.
- Each of 10 proxies caps itself at 5 in flight for b7. Fleet-wide ceiling: 50.
- b7 is now permanently limited to 50 even though it advertises 100. Half its capacity is unreachable, nothing logs an error, and the only symptom is that b7's utilisation graph never rises above 50% while latency elsewhere climbs. This is the quietest failure in the file: a misconfiguration that looks like healthy operation.

---

> → Accept the client connection and authenticate TLS as configured.
> → Pin one validated configuration snapshot for the request.
> → Resolve the route and eligible backend set.
> → Select a backend and reserve local in-flight capacity.
> → Proxy with bounded buffers and an end-to-end deadline.
> → Release the reservation on success, failure, or cancellation.
> → Record a classified outcome for passive health evaluation.

**What this says in plain words — the seven steps, one at a time**

1. **Accept and authenticate.** Complete the TCP connection, complete the TLS handshake, and if the route requires client certificates, verify them.
2. **Pin a snapshot.** Grab a reference to the current immutable configuration and keep using *that one* for the whole request, even if a new configuration is published mid-request.
3. **Resolve the route.** Match the request's host and path to a route, and from the route get the pool, and from the pool get the list of backends currently eligible to receive work.
4. **Select and reserve.** Choose one backend, and immediately increment that backend's in-flight counter so concurrent requests see the load you are about to create.
5. **Proxy with bounds.** Stream the bytes through fixed-size buffers, and run a single deadline that covers the whole request, not one timeout per stage.
6. **Release the reservation — on every exit path.** Success, failure, timeout, or the client hanging up: the counter must come back down.
7. **Classify the outcome.** Record what happened in a form health evaluation can use: a connection failure and an application `500` are different evidence about the backend.

**Why "pin one snapshot" and "release on every path" are the two load-bearing steps**

- Pinning: without it, step 3 could read a backend list containing backend b7 and step 4 could read a list where b7 no longer exists, or where index 3 now means a different machine. The request would behave as if it were two different requests.
- Releasing: step 6 says *success, failure, or cancellation* for a reason. The cancellation path is the one people forget, and forgetting it leaks the counter permanently. The source names this as the exact failure mode in the next section.

**Worked example of an end-to-end deadline versus per-stage timeouts**

- Per-stage timeouts: 2 s to connect, 5 s for headers, 5 s for the body. Worst case the client waits 12 seconds, and no single timeout was violated.
- End-to-end deadline of 5 s: the request is abandoned at 5 seconds regardless of which stage was slow. The client's experience is bounded by a number you actually chose.

## API and data model — IC3 baseline

This section describes the *control plane* only. No user request touches any of it. These are the calls an operator or an automated deploy makes, and the tables behind them.

---

> | Surface | Contract | Failure rule |
> |---|---|---|
> | `PUT /v1/pools/{id}` | Backend membership, weights, health policy | Validate positive weights and bounded timeouts; `If-Match` version, `412` on stale edit |

**What this says in plain words**
`PUT /v1/pools/{id}` replaces the definition of one pool: which backends are in it, how much traffic each should get relative to the others, and the rules for declaring a backend unhealthy. Weights must be positive numbers and timeouts must have limits. The caller must say which version of the pool they are editing, and if that version is no longer current the call is rejected with HTTP `412 Precondition Failed`.

**Why `If-Match` and `412` matter — the lost-update problem**

- Without it: operator A reads pool `p1` (version 5, backends b1–b4), operator B reads the same version 5. A adds b5 and writes. B removes b2 and writes. B's write was computed from version 5, so it silently erases b5. Neither operator sees an error.
- With it: A's write carries `If-Match: 5`, succeeds, and the pool becomes version 6. B's write also carries `If-Match: 5`, does not match version 6, and returns `412`. B re-reads, sees b5, and redoes the edit deliberately.
- The cost is that B has to retry. The benefit is that a fleet routing configuration cannot be silently corrupted by two people working at the same time.

---

> | `PUT /v1/routes/{id}` | Host/path match to a pool | Reject dangling pool references in the same snapshot |

**What this says in plain words**
A route says "requests for host `api.example.com` and path `/orders` go to pool `p1`". If the pool named does not exist in the configuration being published, the whole publication is rejected.

**Why "in the same snapshot" is the precise wording**
The check is not "does the pool exist right now"; it is "does the pool exist in the exact set of configuration being shipped together". Checking against live state would allow a race where the pool is deleted between validation and publication. Validating the snapshot as a unit makes the guarantee hold for the thing actually delivered.

**What breaks without it**
A route pointing at a missing pool means a request matches the route, finds no eligible backends, and returns `503`. It is a configuration typo producing a user-visible outage on one path, and the configuration looked accepted.

---

> | `POST /v1/backends/{id}:drain` | Stop new assignments; return `202` and an operation ID | Idempotency key; a repeat returns the same operation |

**What this says in plain words**
Ask for a backend to stop receiving new requests. The reply is `202 Accepted` plus an ID you can poll. The caller includes an idempotency key, and calling again with the same key returns the *same* operation rather than starting a second one.

**Why the idempotency key is needed here specifically**

- A deploy script calls drain, the response is lost to a network timeout, and the script retries — which is exactly correct behaviour for a script.
- Without the key, that is now two drain operations on the same backend. They may have different deadlines, and "confirm zero owned traffic" becomes ambiguous.
- With the key, the second call returns operation `op-91` again, with its existing progress. The script polls one operation and the deploy stays deterministic.

---

> | `GET /v1/operations/{id}` | Deployment/drain progress | Expose partial rollout rather than claiming atomic fleet activation |
> | `GET /v1/pools/{id}/health` | Per-proxy observations and observed time | Stale health is visibly labeled |

**What this says in plain words**
Two read endpoints, both designed around honesty about partial and stale state.

1. **Operations progress** reports "7 of 10 proxies have acknowledged", not a boolean "applied". A fleet of 10 machines does not change state simultaneously, and an API that pretends otherwise makes deploy scripts unsafe.
2. **Pool health** returns each proxy's own opinion, each stamped with when it was observed. It does not return one merged verdict.

**Worked example of why stamped, per-proxy health is the right shape**

- An operator asks why traffic to backend b7 dropped. The honest answer: "proxies 1, 2, and 4 report b7 unhealthy as of 3 seconds ago; proxies 3 and 5 report it healthy as of 2 seconds ago."
- That immediately suggests a network problem on the path from proxies 1, 2, and 4 rather than a sick backend — a diagnosis a single merged "b7: degraded" would have hidden.
- The timestamp does separate work. A proxy that last reported 40 seconds ago is not reporting healthy; it is not reporting. Labeling staleness prevents reading silence as good news.

---

> | State | Key | Stored fields |
> |---|---|---|
> | Route | `(tenant_id, route_id)` | Host matcher, path matcher, pool ID, version |
> | Pool | `(tenant_id, pool_id)` | Policy, health thresholds, connect/request deadlines |
> | Backend membership | `(pool_id, backend_id)` | Address, capacity weight, administrative state |
> | Runtime health | `(proxy_id, backend_id)` | Consecutive outcomes, ejection deadline, in-flight requests |

**What this says in plain words**
Four kinds of state, each with the key that identifies one item of it.

1. **Route** — keyed by tenant and route ID. A *tenant* is one customer or account. Putting `tenant_id` first in the key is what makes tenants unable to see or reference each other's configuration.
2. **Pool** — keyed by tenant and pool ID. Holds the selection policy and the health thresholds.
3. **Backend membership** — which machines are in the pool, at what weight, and in which administrative state (`ACTIVE`, `DRAINING`, `DISABLED`).
4. **Runtime health** — keyed by *proxy* and backend, because it is one proxy's private opinion. This row is the only one of the four that is not in the database at all.

**Why the fourth row is different, and why that is the interesting design point**
Routes, pools, and membership are *desired* state: a human decided them, they change rarely, they must survive a restart. Runtime health is *observed* state: it changes several times per second per backend, it is meaningless outside the proxy that measured it, and it must be forgotten on restart so a fresh proxy re-learns rather than acting on a stale opinion.

- Arithmetic for why it cannot be relational: 10 proxies × 500 backends = 5,000 rows, each updated on every request outcome. At 100,000 requests/s that is 100,000 writes per second of pure counter churn, for data nobody needs durably. In memory it is a few thousand integers.

---

> - Reference DDL
>   - Keys and uniqueness match the state table above and the [companion schema](../../1sde/1.backend/2.rest102.md#infra-2).
>   - Runtime health `(proxy_id, backend_id)` and the per-proxy immutable snapshot live in proxy memory; only desired configuration and operations are relational.

**What this says in plain words**
DDL means Data Definition Language — the `CREATE TABLE` statements. The note above the SQL says explicitly which state is *not* in SQL, so a reader does not go looking for a health table that deliberately does not exist.

**The four tables, and the one idea each one is really carrying**

1. **`lb_pools`** — the selection policy and health thresholds.
   - `policy text NOT NULL CHECK (policy IN ('P2C','ROUND_ROBIN','WEIGHTED_ROUND_ROBIN'))` restricts the column to three known algorithms. A typo like `'p2c_v2'` is refused by the database rather than reaching a proxy that does not recognise it.
   - `healthy_threshold` and `unhealthy_threshold` are the number of consecutive results needed to readmit or eject a backend, both required to be greater than zero. A zero threshold would mean "eject after no failures", which is nonsense the `CHECK` makes unrepresentable.
   - `max_ejected_pct integer NOT NULL CHECK (max_ejected_pct BETWEEN 0 AND 100)` is the cap that stops health checking from removing the entire pool. It is stored per pool because it is a policy, not a constant.
   - `version bigint NOT NULL CHECK (version > 0)` is the value `If-Match` compares against.
2. **`lb_backends`** — pool membership.
   - `PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, pool_id, backend_id)` plus `UNIQUE (tenant_id, pool_id, address, port)`. The unique constraint is the load-bearing one: the same address and port cannot be registered twice in a pool under two different IDs. Without it, backend `b7` and backend `b7-retry` could both point at `10.0.0.7:8443` and receive double their intended share of traffic, invisibly.
   - `admin_state text NOT NULL CHECK (admin_state IN ('ACTIVE','DRAINING','DISABLED'))`, with the comment `desired; a probe never rewrites it`. This column is human intent. Health observation lives elsewhere precisely so a passing probe cannot resurrect a draining backend.
   - `weight integer NOT NULL CHECK (weight > 0)` — a zero weight would mean division by zero in the selection score.
3. **`lb_routes`** — host and path matching.
   - `UNIQUE (tenant_id, host_match, path_match)` enforces *one owner per match*. Without it two routes could claim `api.example.com/orders`, and which one wins would depend on iteration order — a bug that reproduces differently on each proxy.
   - `FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, pool_id) REFERENCES lb_pools` is the dangling-pool rejection from the endpoint table, enforced by the database rather than by application code that might be bypassed.
   - `credential_ref text, credential_version bigint` with the comment `reference into the secret path; never key bytes`. The table stores a pointer to the private key, never the key itself.
4. **`lb_operations`** — drains and deploys.
   - `UNIQUE (tenant_id, kind, target_id, idempotency_key)` is what makes the repeat-returns-the-same-operation rule true. It is enforced by a constraint, so a race between two concurrent identical calls resolves to one row rather than two.
   - `acked_proxies` and `total_proxies`, with `CHECK (acked_proxies <= total_proxies)`, are the partial-rollout counters behind `GET /operations/{id}`.
   - `CREATE INDEX lb_operations_open ON lb_operations (tenant_id, updated_at) WHERE state IN ('ACCEPTED','RUNNING')` is a *partial index*: it indexes only unfinished operations. The access path it serves is "show me this tenant's in-progress operations", which is the only query that runs frequently. Millions of completed rows stay out of the index, so it remains small permanently.

---

> - Not captured by the DDL
>   - Consecutive outcomes, ejection deadlines, and in-flight counts are per-proxy memory keyed by `(proxy_id, backend_id)`; `GET /pools/{id}/health` reads a proxy-reported projection with its observed time, not a table.
>   - The eligible-array snapshot a request pins is built from these rows per configuration version and swapped atomically inside the proxy.
>   - TLS private keys are fetched by `credential_ref` from the secret path; the database stores the reference and version only.

**What this says in plain words**
Three explicit statements about what is *not* in the database, and where it lives instead:

1. Health counters live in proxy memory. The health endpoint asks the proxies, it does not run a query.
2. The eligible-backend array a request pins is *derived* from these rows, not stored. Each configuration version produces a new array, and the proxy swaps the pointer atomically.
3. Private keys come from a secret distribution system. The database holds only a reference and a version number.

**Why stating all three is worth the lines**
Every one of them is a question an interviewer will otherwise ask, and the answer sounds much weaker when it is produced under pressure. Saying "health is memory, keyed per proxy, and the endpoint is a projection with an observation time" up front demonstrates you decided it rather than discovered it.

---

> - Keep administrative state separate from observed health.
>   - `DRAINING` must not be undone by a successful health probe.
>   - A backend certificate failure must not be overridden by an availability policy.

**What this says in plain words**
Two different things both answer "should this backend get traffic", and they must never be stored in the same field.

1. **Administrative state is intent.** A human said drain this machine. A probe succeeding does not change what the human wants.
2. **Security failures outrank availability preferences.** If a backend presents an invalid certificate, a policy that says "keep serving when things look unhealthy" must not send traffic to it anyway.

**Worked example of the bug this prevents**

- One combined `status` column. Operator sets b7 to `DRAINING` at 10:00:00 to replace its disk.
- At 10:00:02 the health checker's next probe succeeds — of course it does, b7 is still running.
- The checker writes `status = HEALTHY`, overwriting the operator's intent. Traffic resumes. The operator, reading their earlier `202`, pulls the disk.
- Two fields make this impossible: eligibility requires `admin_state = ACTIVE` **and** observed health passing. A probe can only ever move the second one.

---

> - Keep TLS private keys in a controlled secret distribution path.
>   - Route metadata contains a credential reference and version.
>   - Roll credential updates with an overlap window and expiry alarms.

**What this says in plain words**
Private keys are fetched from a dedicated secret system, and the routing configuration only names which key to use. Replacing a key is done with both keys briefly valid at once, and you alarm on how soon certificates expire.

**Why the overlap window is not optional**

- No overlap: at 10:00:00 the new certificate becomes the only valid one. Proxies apply configuration at different times, so between 10:00:00 and 10:00:04 some proxies still present the old certificate while clients have been told to expect the new chain. Handshakes fail for four seconds — a small outage caused by a routine rotation.
- With overlap: both credentials are valid for, say, 24 hours. Proxies converge whenever they converge, every handshake succeeds throughout, and the old credential is removed the next day once `GET /operations/{id}` confirms every proxy is on the new reference.
- Expiry alarms exist because certificate expiry is the one failure that a healthy, unchanged, untouched system produces entirely on its own, on a date decided months earlier. Alarm on days remaining, not on failures.

## Selection and concurrency — IC3 baseline

This is the algorithmic heart of the question. Everything else in the file is structure; this section is the one place where a specific algorithm is chosen over specific named alternatives, for reasons with numbers attached.

---

> - Default: power of two choices over eligible backends.

**What this says in plain words**
For each request, pick two eligible backends at random. Compare how busy they are. Send the request to the less busy of the two. That is the entire algorithm.

**Why it beats the two obvious alternatives — the central argument of this section**

There are three candidate rules. Take 4 backends, one proxy handling 10,000 requests per second, and request durations that vary: most requests take 10 milliseconds, some take 2 seconds.

**Rule 1 — plain round robin: 1, 2, 3, 4, 1, 2, 3, 4.**

- It sends each backend exactly 2,500 requests per second. Perfectly fair by *count*.
- But it is blind to work. Suppose backend C is slow — a garbage-collection pause, a noisy neighbour on its host, a cold cache — and is taking 200 milliseconds per request instead of 10.
- Round robin keeps handing C 2,500 requests per second regardless. By Little's Law, C's in-flight count is 2,500 × 0.2 = 500 requests, against 2,500 × 0.01 = 25 at each healthy backend.
- C's queue grows until it hits its admission limit or runs out of memory, and 25% of all users are stuck behind it. Round robin never notices, because nothing in the algorithm reads load. Its fairness is over the wrong quantity.

**Rule 2 — always pick the least loaded of all 4.**

- This sounds strictly better: it reads load, and it picks the best. It is in fact worse than power-of-two in practice, and the reason is the **herd effect**.
- Concretely. A, B, D each have 10 requests in flight. Backend C has just finished a batch and momentarily has 2 in flight. The proxy's view of load refreshes every 100 milliseconds — it is not free to recompute continuously, and in a real system load information always lags by *something*.
- For those 100 milliseconds, every single arriving request evaluates "least loaded" and gets the same answer: C. At 10,000 requests per second, that is **1,000 consecutive requests all sent to C**.
- C's in-flight count goes from 2 to roughly 1,002 in one tenth of a second. Meanwhile A, B, and D received nothing and drained down toward 0.
- C is now by far the *slowest* backend, because it is the only one with a 1,000-deep queue. Its latency goes from 10 ms to over a second.
- At the next refresh, every proxy sees C as the most loaded and stops sending it anything at all. C drains. A, B, and D — which just absorbed the next burst unevenly — now take their turn being the momentary minimum.
- The result is an oscillation. Load never settles; it sloshes. Every backend alternates between idle and 1,000-deep, and the latency percentiles are dominated by whichever backend is currently holding the herd. The rule that picks the best backend is precisely the rule that destroys it, because *every decision-maker acts on the same stale observation at the same time*.
- Note this gets worse, not better, with 10 proxies: all 10 independently conclude C is least loaded, so C receives 10,000 requests in that window instead of 1,000.

**Rule 3 — power of two choices.**

- Sample two of the four at random, take the less loaded. Randomness breaks the synchronisation that caused the herd.
- Same setup: A, B, D at 10 in flight, C at 2. Any given request samples C with probability 1 − (3 choose 2)/(4 choose 2) = 1 − 3/6 = **50%**. When C is sampled it wins, because it is the minimum.
- So C receives about 50% of arriving requests, not 100%. In the same 100-millisecond window C takes about 500 requests instead of 1,000, and the other 500 spread over A, B, and D.
- 50% is still twice C's fair share of 25% — which is correct behaviour, because C genuinely is the idle one and should get extra. The point is that the overshoot is *bounded by the sampling*, not by the refresh interval.
- And the correction is immediate rather than delayed: as soon as C's counter rises above its peers, C starts losing comparisons. The in-flight counter is incremented at selection time, so the very next request already sees the load the previous one created. There is no refresh interval to wait for.
- Summary of the three: round robin ignores load, so a slow backend is never protected. Least-loaded reads load but synchronises every decision-maker onto one stale answer, producing herds and oscillation. Power-of-two reads load *and* randomises, so it corrects skew without any decision-maker being able to lead a stampede.

---

> - Compare normalized in-flight requests, not just TCP connections.
> - HTTP/2 multiplexing makes connection count a poor proxy for active work.

**What this says in plain words**
Two ideas:

1. **Normalize.** Do not compare raw in-flight counts. Divide by the backend's capacity weight, so 20 in flight on a backend of weight 2 scores the same as 10 on a backend of weight 1.
2. **Count requests, not connections.** In HTTP/1.1 one connection carries one request at a time, so connection count approximated request count. In HTTP/2 one connection carries many concurrent *streams*.

**Worked example of why connection counting fails under HTTP/2**

- Backend A: 100 open connections, 1 active stream each = 100 requests in flight.
- Backend B: 4 open connections, 200 active streams each = 800 requests in flight.
- A "least connections" rule prefers B, because 4 is less than 100. B is carrying eight times the work. The rule is not merely imprecise here; it is inverted.
- This is why the source says "normalized in-flight requests". The unit being counted must be the unit that consumes backend capacity.

**Worked example of normalization**

- Backend A: weight 1 (4 CPU cores), 10 in flight. Score = 10 / 1 = 10.
- Backend B: weight 4 (16 CPU cores), 30 in flight. Score = 30 / 4 = 7.5.
- B wins despite having three times the raw count, which is correct: B is at 7.5 units of load per unit of capacity against A's 10. Without normalization, a heterogeneous fleet is permanently unbalanced in favour of the weakest machines.

---

> - A maintained eligible-array snapshot makes sampling constant time.
> - Refiltering all backends on every request would cost `O(N)`.

**What this says in plain words**
Keep a pre-built array of exactly the backends currently allowed to receive traffic. Then picking two at random is two array index lookups — constant time, written `O(1)`. If instead you walked all 500 backends per request checking each one's eligibility, that is `O(N)` work per request.

**The arithmetic that makes this matter**

- 500 backends, 100,000 requests/s. Filtering per request: 500 × 100,000 = 50,000,000 eligibility checks per second, fleet-wide. At even 20 nanoseconds per check that is a full second of CPU per second — one entire core, burned re-deriving a list that changes a few times a day.
- With a maintained array: 2 random indexes per request = 200,000 lookups per second. Effectively free. The array is rebuilt only when membership or health changes, perhaps a few times per minute at worst.
- This is the general shape of the trade: move work from the per-request path (100,000/s) to the per-change path (0.001/s). The cost is that the array must be rebuilt correctly and published safely, which is what the next block is about.

---

> ```text
> PICK(snapshot):
>     if snapshot.eligible_count == 0: return NO_BACKEND
>     if snapshot.eligible_count == 1: choose its only backend
>     otherwise sample two distinct eligible backends
>     choose lower in_flight / capacity_weight
>     reserve local in-flight capacity
>     if reservation loses a drain/capacity race, retry selection within a bound
>     release reservation in a finally/completion handler
> ```

**Line by line**

1. **Zero eligible: return `NO_BACKEND`.** Handled first and explicitly. Every pool reaches zero eligible backends eventually, and the code must have a defined answer — which becomes a `503` or the panic policy, not a crash or an infinite loop.
2. **Exactly one eligible: take it.** Also explicit, because "sample two distinct backends" is impossible when only one exists. Without this branch the sampling loop spins forever looking for a second distinct choice. This is the classic bug in hand-written power-of-two code.
3. **Sample two distinct.** *Distinct* matters. If the same backend can be drawn twice, you have silently degraded to random choice for that request, since comparing a backend with itself always returns it.
4. **Compare normalized score.** As above.
5. **Reserve immediately.** Increment the in-flight counter at selection, not at dispatch. The gap between choosing and sending is where concurrent requests would otherwise make the same choice.
6. **Retry within a bound on a lost race.** Between the snapshot read and the reservation, the chosen backend may have entered `DRAINING` or hit its cap. Re-select — but with a retry limit, so a pool that is entirely full fails fast instead of spinning.
7. **Release in a `finally` block.** Not "release after the response". A `finally` or completion handler runs on the exception path, the timeout path, and the client-disconnect path too.

---

> - The score is local to a proxy.
>   - A fleet-wide exact minimum would add coordination to every request.
>   - Local randomized choice trades perfect balance for cheap independent decisions.

**What this says in plain words**
Each proxy compares only its *own* in-flight counts. It does not know how much work the other nine proxies have sent to the same backend. Getting the true fleet-wide number would mean asking a shared service on every request.

**Why local is the right call, with numbers**

- Coordinated: every one of 100,000 requests per second consults a shared counter store. Even at 0.5 milliseconds round trip, that adds 0.5 ms to every request — a 5% latency increase on a 10 ms request — and creates a component whose outage stops all traffic. At 100,000 operations per second it is also a substantial system in its own right, built solely to improve balance.
- Local: zero added latency, zero added dependency, and the balance achieved is provably close to optimal. The known result behind power-of-two choices is that the busiest backend's load grows roughly like log log N rather than log N compared with pure random assignment, which is the difference between an imbalance you can ignore and one you cannot.
- The honest cost, stated in the source as the weakest assumption later: a backend that is slow for reasons the proxy cannot see looks identically loaded from all ten proxies, and no amount of local counting reveals it.

---

> - Consistency boundary
>   - The snapshot is immutable for the request
>   - A swap publishes a new array atomically.
>   - In-flight counters are per proxy.
>   - Ten proxies see ten partial views of one backend.
>   - A fleet-wide backend concurrency ceiling requires backend admission or divided permits
>   - Local counters alone cannot enforce it.

**What this says in plain words**
A *consistency boundary* is the edge of the region where a guarantee holds. Six statements defining it here:

1. **The request's snapshot never changes under it.** Read once, use throughout.
2. **Publication is an atomic pointer swap.** Building a new array and then swapping one reference means no reader ever observes a half-built array, and no locking is needed on the read path.
3. **Counters are per proxy.** Nothing is shared.
4. **So one backend has ten partial views of itself.** No proxy holds the truth.
5. **Therefore a fleet-wide concurrency ceiling is not enforceable here.** If the requirement is "never more than 100 concurrent requests at backend b7", local counters cannot deliver it.
6. **Two mechanisms can:** the backend rejecting beyond its own limit (*backend admission*), or dividing the ceiling into per-proxy permits in advance.

**Worked example of the ceiling problem and both fixes**

- Requirement: at most 100 concurrent requests at b7. Ten proxies. Each proxy caps itself at 100, because 100 is the number it was told. Actual worst case: 1,000 concurrent. The requirement is violated by 10×, and every proxy is individually compliant.
- **Fix A, divided permits.** Give each proxy 10 permits. Worst case is now exactly 100 and the requirement holds. Cost: if 9 proxies are idle and 1 is busy, the busy one is capped at 10 while 90 permits sit unused. You bought correctness with stranded capacity.
- **Fix B, backend admission.** b7 accepts 100 and rejects the 101st immediately with a fast `503`. The ceiling is exact and no capacity is stranded, because b7 sees the true total. Cost: rejections are visible to the proxy, which must not mistake a shed request for a sick backend — this is the same distinction as "connection failures versus application `5xx`" in the health section.
- The conclusion the source draws is simply honest: the proxy's local bound is a *protection*, not a *guarantee*. If you need a guarantee, it has to be enforced where the true count exists.

---

> - Concrete example
>   - Backends `A` and `B` with weight `1`, `A` at `40` in flight and `B` at `10`.
>   - Sampling both picks `B`
>   - Sampling `A` twice is excluded by distinct sampling.
>   - Exact failure: a counter leak on cancellation makes a healthy backend look busy forever.

**What this says in plain words**
With only two backends, sampling two distinct ones means sampling both, so power-of-two degenerates into exact least-loaded — and correctly picks B at 10 over A at 40. The example exists mainly to set up its last line.

**The counter leak, walked through — this is the failure to be able to name**

- Every reservation must be released. Suppose the release is written on the success path and the error path, but the client-disconnect path returns early without releasing.
- At 10:00:00 a mobile client sends a request to backend b7, the proxy reserves 1, and the client's network drops. The counter is never decremented. b7's apparent in-flight count is permanently 1 higher than reality.
- The leak is proportional to traffic, not to failures. If 0.1% of 100,000 requests per second are client disconnects, the proxy leaks 100 counts per second.
- After 60 seconds b7 appears to have 6,000 requests in flight while actually serving zero.
- Now the algorithm works perfectly against corrupt input: b7 loses every comparison, so it receives no traffic, so its counter never has a reason to be corrected. A completely healthy machine is permanently removed from service by an accounting bug.
- The symptoms are deliberately confusing: health probes on b7 pass, b7's own metrics show it idle, the pool's error rate is normal, and the only evidence is that the *proxy's* view of b7's in-flight count is absurd and monotonically rising. Note the leak is per proxy, so b7 may be excluded by three proxies and used normally by seven.
- The defences are structural, not vigilant: release in a `finally` or completion handler so no exit path can skip it; and alarm on in-flight counts that exceed any plausible value or never return to zero during a traffic trough.

---

> - Session affinity
>   - Use an authenticated stable session key only when required.
>   - A hot session can overload one backend.
>   - Affinity does not preserve a failed backend's in-memory state.

**What this says in plain words**
*Session affinity* (also called sticky sessions) means always sending the same client to the same backend. Three cautions:

1. **Only when required, and only on an authenticated key.** If affinity keys off something the client controls and you do not verify — a cookie, a header — a client can choose its backend, and then choose everyone's favourite backend.
2. **A hot session concentrates load.** Affinity deliberately disables load balancing for that key.
3. **It does not do the thing people expect it to do.** Affinity does not preserve state; it only preserves *placement*. When the backend dies, the in-memory session is gone. Affinity bought you nothing at the exact moment you needed it.

**Worked example of the hot session**

- 4 backends, 10,000 requests/s, affinity on tenant ID. 999 small tenants average 4 requests/s each; one large tenant sends 6,000 requests/s.
- The large tenant's key hashes to backend C. C now receives 6,000 requests/s plus its share of the rest, roughly 7,000 — against a tested ceiling of 3,000. C fails.
- The proxy cannot fix this, because the whole point of affinity was to override load-based selection. The only fixes are to shard that tenant across several keys or to exempt it from affinity — both of which mean the affinity contract was wrong for this workload.

**Worked example of affinity not preserving state**

- A shopping cart is held in backend C's memory, and affinity keeps the user on C so the cart is found.
- C is restarted during a deploy. The cart is gone. Affinity routes the user to D, which has no cart either.
- The user's experience is identical to having had no affinity at all. The correct fix was always to put the cart in shared storage, at which point affinity is an optimisation for cache warmth, not a correctness mechanism. Treating it as correctness is how a routine deploy becomes a data-loss incident.

---

**Consistent hashing versus plain modulo hashing — the mechanism behind affinity**

When affinity *is* required, the question becomes how to map a key to a backend. There are two schemes, and the difference between them is large enough to be worth working out.

**Plain modulo hashing.** Compute `hash(key) mod N`, where N is the number of backends.

- 4 backends, 1,000,000 distinct session keys. Each backend gets about 250,000 keys. Simple, uniform, and cheap.
- Now add a fifth backend. The rule becomes `hash(key) mod 5`.
- A key keeps its backend only if `hash mod 4 == hash mod 5`. Over any block of 20 consecutive hash values, that is true for exactly 4 of them (hash values 0, 1, 2, and 3). So **20% of keys stay put and 80% move**.
- In numbers: **800,000 of 1,000,000 keys are reassigned** to a different backend, in order to add one machine's worth of capacity.
- Worse, they do not move to the new node. They shuffle among all five. Every backend's cache is invalidated simultaneously, and every affinity binding in the system breaks at once. If those backends held session state, 80% of sessions are lost. If they held caches, all five backends suffer a cold-cache thundering herd against the database at the same moment.
- Removing a node is equally violent: going from 5 back to 4 moves about 80% again, so a single backend *failure* triggers a fleet-wide reshuffle. That is the fatal property: modulo hashing turns one machine's crash into an event affecting nearly every key.

**Consistent hashing.** Place backends and keys on the same circular hash space — imagine a clock face numbered 0 to 2^32. Each backend is hashed to a point on the circle (in practice to many points, called *virtual nodes*). A key is hashed to a point and assigned to the first backend found going clockwise.

- 4 backends, 1,000,000 keys: each still owns roughly a quarter of the circle and roughly 250,000 keys.
- Add a fifth backend. It lands at one point on the circle and claims only the arc between itself and the previous backend. Every other arc is untouched.
- Keys moved: about 1/5 of the total, so roughly **200,000 of 1,000,000**, and all of them move *to the new node*. No existing pair of backends exchanges any key.
- **The comparison, same event: 800,000 keys move under modulo, 200,000 under consistent hashing — a 4× difference — and the 200,000 all move in one direction instead of shuffling.**
- The general rule: consistent hashing moves about K/N keys when going from N to N+1, where K is the key count. Modulo moves about K × (1 − 1/lcm-related fraction), which for small N changes is 75–85%. As N grows, consistent hashing gets *better* (K/N shrinks) while modulo stays catastrophic.
- Removing a node: under consistent hashing, only that node's arc is reassigned, to its clockwise neighbour. One backend fails, 1/5 of keys move, and 4/5 of the system does not notice. This is the property that makes it usable in a fleet where machines fail routinely.
- The cost is real and is named in the source's alternatives table: with few points per backend, the arcs come out uneven, so one backend can own 40% of the circle by luck. The fix is virtual nodes — hash each backend to 100 or 200 points instead of 1 — which averages the arcs out. And consistent hashing does nothing about hot keys: if one key carries 60% of traffic, it lands on exactly one backend under either scheme.

## Health, retries, and draining — IC3 baseline

Three mechanisms that all share one property: each of them is a safety device that, misconfigured, becomes the cause of the outage it was built to prevent. That is why the source attaches a bound to every one of them.

---

> - Active probes
>   - Example settings: interval `2 s`, timeout `0.5 s`, three failures to eject.
>   - Detection is roughly `4.5–6.5 s` from failure under regular scheduling.

**What this says in plain words**
*Active probing* means the proxy sends a request to each backend every 2 seconds purely to ask "are you there". If a probe gets no answer within 0.5 seconds it counts as a failure. After three consecutive failures the backend is *ejected* — removed from the eligible array.

**Where 4.5 to 6.5 seconds comes from**

- The backend dies at some point within a 2-second probe interval. Best case it dies just before a probe; worst case just after one, so 0 to 2 seconds pass before the first probe that can fail.
- Probe 1 fails after its 0.5-second timeout. Then 1.5 seconds until probe 2 (2-second interval, 0.5 of which was the timeout), which fails after 0.5 s. Then the same again for probe 3.
- Best case: 0 + 0.5 + 2 + 2 = 4.5 seconds. Worst case: add the full 2-second wait before the first probe = 6.5 seconds.
- So for up to 6.5 seconds after a backend dies, this proxy keeps sending it requests. At 10,000 requests/s per proxy and 4 backends, that is up to 16,000 requests sent into a dead machine by this proxy alone. Those are not silent — they fail, and passive observation (below) is what catches them much faster than active probing does.

**Why the three-failure threshold exists at all, given that cost**

- One failure is not evidence. A single dropped packet, a 600-millisecond garbage-collection pause, or a momentarily full accept queue produces one failed probe on a perfectly healthy machine.
- If one failure ejected a backend, then with 500 backends and 2,500 probes/s, even a 0.1% spurious failure rate ejects roughly 2 or 3 backends per second continuously. The pool would flap permanently.
- Three consecutive failures at 0.1% each is a one-in-a-billion coincidence, so the ejections you get are real. The price of that confidence is measured in seconds of detection delay, and the source states both halves.

---

> - Scheduling stalls and network saturation extend that time.

**What this says in plain words**
The 4.5–6.5 second figure assumes the probe timer actually fires on time. It may not.

**Why it matters**
The health checker runs on the same machine, in the same process, as the traffic. If the proxy is CPU-saturated, the probe task waits in a queue behind real work. If the network is saturated, the probe packet queues behind real packets.

- Worked example: the proxy is at 100% CPU because of the reconnect storm from the capacity section. The probe scheduler, which wanted to fire at t = 2.000 s, actually fires at t = 3.400 s. Detection stretches from 6.5 seconds to 10 or more.
- The nastier version: the probe *times out* because the proxy could not process the reply in time, not because the backend was slow. The proxy now ejects healthy backends because it is itself overloaded — reducing the pool, concentrating traffic, and making itself more overloaded. This is the first of the two self-inflicted outages in this section.

---

> - Require two successes and slow-start traffic on recovery.

**What this says in plain words**
Ejecting is quick and readmitting is slow. A backend must pass two consecutive probes before it is eligible again, and even then it receives a gradually increasing share rather than its full share immediately.

**Why the asymmetry is deliberate, with numbers**

- A backend that just restarted has an empty cache, an unwarmed connection pool, and a cold just-in-time compiler. It can answer a health probe in 2 milliseconds and still be five times slower than its peers on real work.
- Without slow start: backend C rejoins a 4-backend pool and power-of-two immediately sees it at 0 in flight. C wins nearly every comparison and absorbs a burst — the same herd shape as before, aimed at the machine least able to absorb it. C's cold caches turn that burst into timeouts, three probes fail, and C is ejected again. It now loops: restart, flood, fail, eject, restart.
- With slow start: C is given 10% of its normal weight for the first few seconds, then 30%, then 60%, then full. Its caches warm on a survivable trickle. The ramp is the only thing that makes a restart a non-event.

---

> - Passive observations
>   - Separate connection failures from application `5xx` responses.

**What this says in plain words**
*Passive* health means judging a backend by the outcomes of the real user requests already being sent to it, rather than by extra probes. And two kinds of bad outcome must be counted separately.

1. **A connection failure** — refused, reset, or timed out — is evidence about *the backend or the path to it*. It is strong evidence.
2. **An application `5xx`** — the backend answered, with an HTTP 500-class error — is evidence that the request failed. It is weak evidence that the backend is sick, because the cause is very often somewhere else entirely.

**Why conflating them is dangerous**

- If a shared database is down, every backend returns `500` to every request. Both classes look like failure if you do not distinguish them, so the health logic ejects every backend.
- A connection failure is nearly always local to one backend; a `500` is very often global. Treating them identically means a dependency outage is amplified into a routing outage on top.

---

> - A shared downstream failure can affect every backend simultaneously.
> - Bound ejection percentage to avoid self-inflicted removal of the whole pool.

**What this says in plain words**
Because all backends usually depend on the same database, cache, and identity service, they get sick *together*. So the ejection logic needs a hard cap: never eject more than some percentage of a pool, no matter what the evidence says.

**How a health check causes a fleet-wide outage — the mechanism worth being able to narrate**

- 10:00:00. The shared database becomes slow: queries that took 5 ms now take 4 seconds.
- 10:00:01. All 500 backends start timing out or returning `500` on nearly every request. The database is degraded, but every backend process is alive, correctly configured, and would serve normally the instant the database recovers.
- 10:00:03. Health logic on every proxy, evaluating each backend independently and correctly by its own rules, concludes that backend b1 is failing. And b2. And b3. Each verdict is individually justified by the evidence.
- 10:00:06. Three consecutive failures reached for all 500. Every backend is ejected on every proxy. The eligible array is empty.
- 10:00:07. Every request now returns `503 No healthy upstream` — and crucially, the proxies have stopped sending anything at all, so no traffic reaches the backends.
- 10:00:30. The database recovers. Nothing improves, because no requests are flowing and therefore no passive success can be observed. Active probes may or may not pass depending on whether the probe endpoint touches the database.
- Here is the trap: if the health endpoint queries the database — a very common and well-intentioned design, because "deep" health checks are supposed to be more honest — the probe cannot succeed while the database is slow, and cannot succeed after recovery either, because recovery is invisible until someone sends traffic. **The system has to be restarted by a human to break out of a state where every component is healthy.** The outage outlived its cause by however long it takes to page someone.
- The severity is worth stating plainly: a slow dependency would have caused elevated latency and partial errors. The health checker converted it into a total, self-sustaining outage. The safety device was the amplifier.
- **The bound is the fix.** `max_ejected_pct = 30` in the pool row from the data model means: however bad the evidence is, at most 30% of this pool may be ejected at once. At 10:00:06 only 150 of 500 backends are removed. The other 350 keep receiving traffic and keep failing — which is correct, because failing requests are better than no path at all, and because those failures are the signal that shows recovery the moment the database returns.
- The second fix, from the failure table: a **panic policy**. When the healthy fraction drops below a threshold, the proxy stops believing its health data and reverts to sending traffic to everything. The reasoning is that if 95% of your backends look sick, the most likely explanation is that your health evidence is wrong, not that 95% of your fleet died.
- The third fix is a design rule about the probe itself: a *liveness* probe should test only the backend, never its dependencies. Dependency health is what passive observation and the panic policy handle. A probe that checks the database makes every backend's health a copy of the database's health, which is exactly how 500 independent verdicts become one correlated verdict.

**Why a passing health check does not prove a backend is healthy — the other half of the same coin**

- The probe is `GET /healthz`, which returns `200 OK` after touching nothing. It proves the process is running and the network path works. That is all it proves.
- All of these backends pass that probe while being unable to serve users:
  - A backend whose thread pool is exhausted by 200 stuck requests, but whose health handler runs on a separate reserved thread. Probe: 1 millisecond, `200 OK`. Real requests: timing out.
  - A backend deployed with a bad configuration that makes every real request return `500`, while `/healthz` is a static handler with no such dependency.
  - A backend with a full disk. Reads and the probe succeed; every write fails.
  - A backend whose clock has drifted 10 minutes, so every token it validates is rejected. Probe: fine. Every authenticated request: `401`.
  - A backend serving correct responses at the 99th percentile of 8 seconds because its cache was flushed. Probe: 2 ms. Users: timing out.
  - A backend with a stale routing snapshot, sending its own downstream calls to decommissioned addresses.
- The general statement: an active probe tests *the probe path*, not the request path. The two share a process and very little else. This asymmetry is exactly why the design uses passive observation of real request outcomes as the primary signal and active probes mainly to detect the crude cases — process dead, host unreachable — and to decide when an ejected backend may return.
- The practical consequence for the interview: never claim "the health check confirms the backend is healthy". Claim "the probe confirms the process is reachable; real request outcomes are what tell me whether it is serving".

---

> - Retry contract
>   - At most one retry inside the caller's remaining deadline for the baseline.
>   - Retry connect failures only when request execution is known not to have begun.
>   - Retry application writes only when the backend enforces idempotency.
>   - Cap retry traffic, for example at `10%` of admitted original requests.
>   - Streaming bodies and partially returned responses may not be replayable.

**What this says in plain words**
Five rules, each closing a specific hole:

1. **One retry, and only within time the caller still has.** If the client's deadline is 5 seconds and 4.6 have elapsed, do not start a retry that cannot finish. Spending a backend's capacity on work nobody will wait for is pure waste.
2. **Connect failures are the safe case.** A connection that was refused means no request bytes were ever delivered, so the backend certainly did nothing. That is the one situation where a retry is provably harmless.
3. **Writes need server-side idempotency.** If the request already reached a backend, a retry may duplicate its effect. Only an idempotency key the backend stores and checks makes that safe.
4. **Cap total retry traffic at about 10% of original requests.** This is the *retry budget*.
5. **Some requests cannot be replayed at all.** If the body was streamed and not buffered, the bytes are gone. If 200 kilobytes of response were already sent to the client, you cannot un-send them and start over.

**The retry storm — how a brief blip becomes an outage**

Take 4 backends, each comfortably serving 2,500 requests/s, total 10,000 requests/s. No retry budget, and each client retries up to 3 times.

- t = 0 s. Backend D pauses for 800 milliseconds — a garbage-collection pause, or a brief disk stall. Nothing has failed permanently.
- t = 0.0 to 0.8 s. D's 2,500 requests/s time out. That is 2,000 failed requests.
- t = 0.8 s. Those 2,000 clients retry. The retries land on A, B, and C. Original load on those three was 7,500/s; they now also absorb 2,000 retries, so roughly 9,500 requests/s across three backends with a combined comfortable capacity of 7,500.
- t = 1.0 s. A, B, and C are now over capacity. Their latency climbs from 10 ms to past the client's timeout. They begin failing too — not because anything is wrong with them, but because they were handed 127% of their capacity.
- t = 1.5 s. Failures from A, B, and C generate their own retries. Each failed request produces up to 3 attempts, so offered load is now up to 4× the original: 40,000 requests/s against a fleet capacity of 10,000.
- t = 2.0 s. Health checks begin failing on all four backends, because the probes queue behind 30,000 requests of backlog. Ejection starts. The eligible array shrinks, concentrating the already-quadrupled load onto fewer backends.
- t = 3.0 s. D finished its garbage collection at t = 0.8 s and has been healthy for over two seconds. It is now failing anyway, because it is receiving four times the traffic it can serve.
- **The system is now in a stable failure state.** Offered load is 4× capacity. Every attempt times out. Every timeout creates more attempts. There is no path back, because the thing sustaining the outage is the retries, and the retries are caused by the failures the retries cause. An 800-millisecond blip has become an outage that persists until traffic is cut off at the edge.
- The multiplier is the whole story. With 3 attempts per request, the worst-case offered load is 4× — 1 original plus 3 retries. A system running at 60% utilisation has no chance against a 4× multiplier. Retries are the only mechanism in the design capable of *increasing* offered load precisely when capacity has decreased.

**How a retry budget plus jitter bounds it**

- **The budget** caps retries at 10% of admitted original requests, measured per proxy over a rolling window — say 10 seconds.
  - Replay the same scenario. At t = 0.8 s, 2,000 retries are requested. The proxy's 10-second window admitted 100,000 originals, so the budget allows 10,000 retries. The 2,000 are permitted; A, B, and C absorb them; the blip is invisible to users. This is the budget working *for* availability, which is its normal job.
  - Now suppose the failure is larger: D dies entirely, and 25% of all requests start failing, which is 2,500 retries per second. The budget allows 1,000 per second. The first 1,000 are retried; the remaining 1,500 fail immediately and return the original error to the client, with a hint that it was not retried.
  - Worst-case offered load is now bounded at 1.10× original, not 4×. The fleet is at 110% of normal load against a capacity that lost 25% — bad, and producing real user-visible errors, but *converging*: the surviving backends are not driven into collapse, health checks keep passing, and the moment D returns the system recovers on its own.
  - The trade is stated honestly: the budget converts a small number of extra failures now into the guarantee that the failure stays proportional to its cause. Without it, failures are hidden right up to the point where everything fails at once.
- **Jitter** spreads the retries in time. Without it, all 2,000 clients that timed out at exactly 800 milliseconds retry at exactly 800 milliseconds, so the retry load is not 2,000 spread over a second — it is a 2,000-request spike inside a few milliseconds. That spike alone can exceed the instantaneous capacity of the surviving backends even when the average would have been fine.
  - With jitter, each client waits a random interval — say uniformly between 100 and 400 milliseconds — before retrying. The same 2,000 retries spread over 300 milliseconds, peaking at roughly 7 per millisecond instead of hundreds.
  - Jitter also breaks the *synchronisation* that makes storms recur. Without it, every subsequent failure round happens in lockstep, so the system pulses: spike, fail, wait, spike. With randomisation the clients permanently decorrelate after the first round.
  - Jitter and the budget solve different halves: the budget bounds *how many* retries exist; jitter bounds *how concentrated in time* they are. Either alone is insufficient. A budget of 10% delivered as a single instantaneous spike still breaks things; perfectly jittered 4× load still breaks things.

---

> - Drain sequence
>
> → Publish `DRAINING` and stop new assignments.
> → Let in-flight requests finish within the drain deadline.
> → Stop admitting new HTTP/2 streams and close idle connections.
> → Terminate remaining connections at the deadline.
> → Confirm zero owned traffic before removing the backend.

**What this says in plain words**
Draining is how a backend is removed from service without breaking the requests it is currently serving. Five ordered steps, and the order is the mechanism.

**Second by second, what a drain actually does during a deploy**

Backend b7 is being replaced. Drain deadline is 30 seconds. b7 normally carries 2,500 requests/s with an average duration of 100 ms, so it has about 250 requests in flight at any moment, plus some long-running requests up to 25 seconds.

- **t = 0.0 s.** The operator calls `POST /backends/b7:drain`. Response: `202`, operation `op-91`. Nothing has happened at any proxy yet.
- **t = 0.0 to 0.4 s.** The control plane validates and distributes the change. Proxies begin applying it at slightly different moments. Some proxies are still selecting b7 during this window — which is fine, and is exactly why the drain deadline follows rather than precedes it.
- **t = 0.4 s.** All 10 proxies have rebuilt their eligible array without b7 and acknowledged. `acked_proxies` is 10 of 10. From this instant, **no new request is assigned to b7 anywhere in the fleet**. b7 still holds roughly 250 in-flight requests plus a handful of long ones.
- **t = 0.5 s.** Requests that were already in flight continue normally. The clients behind them notice nothing at all — no error, no retry, no extra latency. This is the entire purpose of draining.
- **t = 0.6 s.** The ordinary 100-millisecond requests that were in flight at t = 0.4 have completed. b7's in-flight count has fallen from 250 to perhaps 12 — the long-running ones.
- **t = 1 s to 25 s.** The remaining long requests — large uploads, slow reports, server-sent event streams — finish one by one. In-flight count decays: 12, then 8, then 3, then 1. Proxies stop opening new HTTP/2 streams on their connections to b7 and close connections that have gone idle, so nothing new can start over the back door of an existing connection.
- **t = 25 s.** The last in-flight request completes. b7's owned traffic is zero.
- **t = 25 s.** The proxies confirm zero owned traffic for b7. `GET /operations/op-91` now reports `SUCCEEDED`. Only now is it safe to power off b7.
- **t = 30 s (if anything remained).** The deadline fires. Any still-open connection to b7 is terminated. A request that has genuinely run for 30 seconds is killed — a deliberate choice, because a drain that waits forever is not a drain, and one stuck request must not block a deploy indefinitely. The deadline is the bound that makes the mechanism operationally usable.
- The operator's discipline follows from step 5: **never act on the `202`, act on `SUCCEEDED`.** The `202` at t = 0 was a promise to begin. The confirmation at t = 25 s is the fact.

**What happens to in-flight requests with no drain at all**

Same backend, same load, but b7 is simply removed and powered off at t = 0.

- **t = 0.0 s.** b7 is killed. Its roughly 250 in-flight requests die instantly. Each client gets a connection reset with no HTTP response.
- **t = 0.0 s.** Every one of those 250 requests has an unknown outcome. Read requests are merely failures. But any `POST` among them may have been fully processed by b7 with only the response lost. If 5% were writes, that is about 12 operations in an ambiguous state per proxy-visible batch — orders that may or may not exist.
- **t = 0.0 to 6.5 s.** The proxies do not yet know b7 is gone. Active probing takes 4.5–6.5 seconds to eject it. During that window, proxies keep selecting b7 and every such request fails on connect. At 2,500 requests/s that is up to 16,000 additional failed requests — all of them avoidable, all of them caused by removing the backend without announcing it.
- **t = 0.1 s.** Those failures generate retries. Connect failures are the safe class to retry, so the retry logic correctly retries them — which means the surviving 3 backends absorb a sudden 2,500 requests/s of retried load. If capacity was tight, this is the retry storm above, triggered by a routine deploy.
- **t = 6.5 s.** b7 is finally ejected. Failures stop.
- Now multiply by the deploy. A rolling deploy replaces all 500 backends this way. Each replacement costs about 250 killed requests, a few ambiguous writes, and up to 16,000 connect failures. Across 500 backends that is 125,000 killed requests and thousands of ambiguous writes — per deploy. This is why teams that deploy without draining see an error spike on the dashboard every single release and learn to call it normal.
- Stated in one sentence: without draining, every deploy is a small outage and every restart is a data-integrity question. Draining costs 25 seconds per backend and removes both.

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |

| Rolling deploy | Replacing backends a few at a time to keep the service up. | Multiplies every per-backend drain cost by the fleet size. |

## Failure, operations, and security

A nine-row failure table, then five topics: panic behavior, what to observe, retry discipline, tenant isolation, and rollout safety.

---

> | Failure | Blast radius | Detection | Degraded behavior | Recovery |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Proxy dies | Its live connections | Front-door health and connection resets | Clients reconnect; writes remain ambiguous | Remove failed proxy and restore fleet headroom |
> | Backend stalls | Its assigned traffic | In-flight age and timeout rate | Circuit limits stop queue growth | Eject cautiously and ramp recovery |
> | All probes fail | Entire pool | Correlated failures across proxies | Explicit panic policy or `503` | Check probe configuration and shared dependencies |
> | One proxy partitioned from backends | That proxy's share of traffic | Its health view diverges from peers | It ejects locally; peers keep serving | Front door removes it when its own probe fails |
> | Configuration service unavailable | New changes only | Version lag | Forward from last-known valid configuration | Resume ordered version delivery |
> | Bad configuration reaches canary | Canary cohort | Error and skew delta versus the fleet | Halt rollout; canary rolls back to prior snapshot | Fix and re-validate before expanding |
> | Bad certificate rollout | A route cohort | Handshake errors | Retain valid overlapping credential if policy permits | Roll back credential reference |
> | Retry storm during partial outage | Surviving backends | Retry ratio above budget | Budget exhausts; failures return to clients | Raise budget only after capacity returns |
> | Slow client | A bounded connection set | Buffer occupancy and write timeout | Backpressure or close that client | Release its buffers and upstream reservations |

**What this says in plain words**
Nine ways the system breaks, each with how far it spreads, how you notice, what happens meanwhile, and how you get back.

**Why the Blast radius column carries the signal**
Naming the radius is what separates an owner from an author. Compare the rows: a dying proxy costs its own connections, while failing probes cost the entire pool. Those are two different incidents with two different pager responses.

- **Row: Proxy dies.**
  - Radius: only the connections it was holding. Other proxies are untouched.
  - Worked example: 10 proxies each holding 20,000 connections. One dies, so 20,000 clients see a reset and reconnect elsewhere. The other 180,000 never notice.
  - "writes remain ambiguous" is the important half. A client with a `POST` in flight receives a connection reset and cannot tell whether the backend applied it. Only an idempotency contract resolves this.
  - Recovery: "restore fleet headroom" means the 9 survivors are now carrying 11% more each. If the fleet had no spare capacity, the failure cascades.
- **Row: Backend stalls.**
  - A stall is worse than a crash, because a crashed backend fails probes and is removed while a stalled one accepts connections and never answers.
  - Detection is *in-flight age*, not error rate: a stalled backend produces zero errors, because nothing has completed yet to be counted as an error.
  - Worked example: a backend accepting requests but answering none accumulates in-flight requests indefinitely. The oldest in-flight age climbs past 30 seconds while the error rate stays at 0%. Only the age metric reveals it.
- **Row: All probes fail.**
  - Radius: the whole pool, which is the largest in the table.
  - Detection is *correlated* failure across proxies. One proxy seeing everything unhealthy is probably that proxy's problem. Every proxy seeing everything unhealthy is either a real total outage or a broken probe.
  - Worked example of a broken probe: a deploy changes the health endpoint path from `/healthz` to `/health`. Every probe now gets `404`, every backend is marked unhealthy, and the backends are perfectly fine. Ejecting them all turns a configuration typo into a total outage.
- **Row: One proxy partitioned from backends.**
  - Radius: only that proxy's share.
  - Detection: its health view diverges from its peers, which is only visible if you compare health views across proxies rather than trusting each in isolation.
  - Behavior: it ejects locally, which is correct. From its position those backends really are unreachable.
  - Recovery: the front door removes it when its own probe fails. Note the layering: the proxy is judged by the front door, not by its opinion of the backends.
- **Row: Configuration service unavailable.**
  - Radius: new changes only. This is the payoff of holding configuration locally.
- **Row: Bad configuration reaches canary.**
  - Detection: error and *skew* delta against the fleet. Skew means how unevenly traffic is spread across backends. A configuration bug can leave error rates normal while sending 90% of traffic to one backend.
- **Row: Bad certificate rollout.**
  - Detection: handshake errors, which appear before any request is processed, so ordinary request metrics may look quiet.
  - Behavior: keep the previous valid credential if policy allows an overlap.
- **Row: Retry storm during partial outage.**
  - Radius: the *surviving* backends, which is the cruel part. The failure moves load onto exactly the machines still working.
- **Row: Slow client.**
  - A client that reads its response slowly forces the proxy to hold the bytes in memory.
  - Worked example: 5,000 slow clients each holding 1 megabyte of buffered response is 5 gigabytes of proxy memory consumed by clients doing nothing wrong, just reading slowly over a poor mobile link.
  - Behavior: apply backpressure, or close that client. The radius is bounded only because per-connection buffer limits exist.

---

> - Panic behavior is a product decision.
>   - Availability-sensitive traffic may retry a last-known eligible set when health evidence is unreliable.
>   - Known compromised or administratively drained backends remain excluded.
>   - Security-sensitive traffic may return `503` instead.
>   - [Envoy panic thresholds](https://www.envoyproxy.io/docs/envoy/latest/intro/arch_overview/upstream/load_balancing/panic_threshold.html) illustrate configurable availability behavior.

**What this says in plain words**
Panic mode is what a load balancer does when almost everything looks unhealthy. The rule here is that the answer is not an engineering default; it belongs to the product.

1. **The situation.**
   - Worked example: a pool of 20 backends, and 19 are marked unhealthy. Sending all traffic to the 1 survivor will certainly kill it within seconds. Rejecting everything guarantees a total outage.
   - The insight behind panic mode: when the health data says almost everything is dead, the most likely explanation is that the health data is wrong.
2. **Availability-sensitive traffic ignores health and spreads load.**
   - Rather than concentrating on the 1 "healthy" backend, send to all 20 as though health were unknown.
   - If the probes were broken, service is restored. If the outage is real, you have lost nothing, because the alternative was rejecting everything anyway.
3. **Two exclusions are absolute.**
   - A backend known to be compromised stays excluded. Panic is a response to unreliable *health* evidence, not a reason to discard *security* evidence.
   - An administratively drained backend stays excluded, because a human deliberately removed it, which is not a probe result that might be wrong.
4. **Security-sensitive traffic returns `503` instead.**
   - For a route where serving wrongly is worse than not serving, panic means reject.
5. **The Envoy citation** shows this is a real, configurable knob in production proxies, not an invention of this design.

**Why it matters**
Calling it a product decision is the strong move. The engineering answer is "it depends on whether availability or correctness matters more for this route", and only the product owner can settle that. Setting it per route class is what makes both answers available in one system.

---

> - Observe
>   - Proxy-added latency independently of backend latency.
>   - In-flight requests and buffer bytes.
>   - Retry amplification.
>   - Healthy fraction by proxy and pool.
>   - Configuration acknowledgments.

**What this says in plain words**
Five things to measure. Each is chosen to answer a question no other metric can.

1. **Proxy-added latency, separately from backend latency.**
   - The time the proxy itself contributes, not the total the client sees.
   - Worked example of why they must be separate: total latency climbs from 20 to 120 milliseconds. Is the proxy slow or is the backend slow? With one combined number you cannot tell and you wake the wrong team. With both, you see backend latency at 118 milliseconds and proxy-added latency at 2, and the answer is immediate.
2. **In-flight requests and buffer bytes.**
   - In-flight count is the input to selection, so if it is wrong, every routing decision is wrong.
   - Buffer bytes is the slow-client exposure. Worked example: buffer bytes climbing steadily with a flat request rate means clients are reading more slowly, and the proxy is heading for memory exhaustion.
3. **Retry amplification.**
   - Retries as a multiple of original requests. At 1.0 nothing is retrying; at 2.0 every request is being sent twice.
4. **Healthy fraction by proxy *and* by pool.**
   - Both dimensions are required, and they answer different questions.
   - By pool: is this service unhealthy?
   - By proxy: does this one proxy disagree with its peers?
   - Worked example: pool healthy fraction 95% overall, but proxy 7 reports 10%. The pool is fine; proxy 7 is partitioned.
5. **Configuration acknowledgments.**
   - Which proxies have applied which version. This is the difference between "the control plane sent version 12" and "version 12 is in force", which are not the same claim.

---

> - Retry discipline
>   - One retry, connect-phase or idempotent only, inside the remaining deadline.
>   - A per-proxy retry budget stops retries from becoming the second outage.
>   - Clients receive the original failure once the budget is spent, with a retry hint.

**What this says in plain words**
Three rules, and the first one has three conditions that all must hold.

1. **One retry, and only when all three conditions are met.**
   - *One*: never a loop. Worked example of why: 3 retries per request turns 10,000 requests per second into a worst case of 40,000 against a fleet that just lost capacity.
   - *Connect-phase or idempotent only*: a connection that was refused proves the backend did nothing, so retrying is safe. Anything after the request was sent is unsafe unless the operation is idempotent or carries an idempotency key.
   - *Inside the remaining deadline*: worked example: the client's deadline is 2 seconds and the first attempt consumed 1.9 seconds. The retry has 100 milliseconds, so it will fail too, and it costs the backend a full request for a result nobody will receive.
2. **A per-proxy retry budget.**
   - A cap on retries as a fraction of original requests, typically 10%, measured over a rolling window.
   - Worked example: 10,000 requests per second with a 10% budget allows 1,000 retries per second, so worst-case offered load is 1.1× rather than 2×. During a partial outage that difference decides whether the survivors hold.
   - Per proxy matters: a global budget requires coordination that does not exist, and each proxy can enforce its own share locally with no communication.
3. **When the budget is spent, the client gets the original failure plus a hint.**
   - The hint says the request was not retried, so the client knows the error is final rather than a transient it should hammer at.

---

> - Tenant isolation
>   - Routes, pools, and certificates are tenant-owned
>   - A tenant cannot reference another tenant's pool in a snapshot.
>   - Bound per-tenant in-flight and connection counts so one tenant's slow clients cannot exhaust proxy buffers.
>   - Health observations are per pool
>   - One tenant's outage never ejects another tenant's backends.

**What this says in plain words**
Three isolation rules, each closing a different leak.

1. **Configuration ownership, enforced at validation time.**
   - A snapshot that names another tenant's pool is rejected before it is ever distributed.
   - Worked example of the leak this prevents: tenant A writes a route pointing at tenant B's backend pool. Without validation, A's traffic reaches B's servers, and B's capacity and data are both exposed. The check belongs in snapshot validation, not in the request path, because a request-path check runs 100,000 times per second to catch a mistake that can only be made at configuration time.
2. **Bounded per-tenant in-flight and connection counts.**
   - Worked example: the proxy has 8 gigabytes of buffer. One tenant's 5,000 slow clients consume all of it, and every other tenant's requests fail with out-of-memory errors. A per-tenant cap of, say, 1 gigabyte means that tenant's slow clients are throttled and everyone else is unaffected.
   - This is the bulkhead principle applied to memory instead of to machines.
3. **Health observations are per pool.**
   - Worked example: tenant A's backends all return `500` because A deployed a broken build. If health were tracked across pools, the proxy might enter panic mode and change behavior for tenant B, whose backends are perfectly healthy. Scoping health to a pool contains A's outage to A.

---

> - Rollout and rollback safety
>   - Validate routes offline.
>   - Canary a proxy cohort.
>   - Compare error rates and pool skew before expanding.
>   - Keep enough capacity to lose one planned failure domain.
>   - Rollback is a snapshot swap to the prior version
>   - Connections are not touched.

**What this says in plain words**
Five rules for changing the fleet safely.

1. **Validate routes offline.**
   - Check the configuration before any proxy loads it: every route resolves, every pool exists and belongs to this tenant, every certificate reference is valid and unexpired.
   - Worked example of skipping this: a route references a pool that was deleted last week. The proxy loads it, and every request to that route gets `503`. Offline validation would have rejected the snapshot with a clear message.
2. **Canary a proxy cohort.**
   - Give the new snapshot to a few proxies first. Worked example: 1 proxy out of 10 is 10% of traffic exposed while still being enough volume to see a real effect within a minute.
3. **Compare error rates *and* pool skew.**
   - Skew is the second signal, and it catches bugs that errors miss.
   - Worked example: a configuration change accidentally makes all backends hash to the same value. Error rate is unchanged, because the one backend receiving everything is still answering. Skew goes from even to 100% on one backend, and that is the only visible symptom until it saturates.
4. **Keep enough capacity to lose one planned failure domain.**
   - Worked example: 10 proxies across 2 availability zones. During a deploy, 1 proxy is down for restart, leaving 9. If a zone then fails, 4 remain. Those 4 must handle 100% of traffic, so each proxy must be provisioned at no more than 40% of its ceiling in normal operation.
5. **Rollback is a snapshot swap, and connections are untouched.**
   - The previous immutable snapshot is still there, so rollback means pointing at it again.
   - Worked example of why connections must not be touched: a rollback that drops all connections turns a configuration mistake into a connection reset for 200,000 clients, which is worse than the bug being rolled back. In-flight requests finish on the old snapshot; new requests use the restored one.

## Alternatives and IC4 stretch

The option table, the base choice, four stretch items, the weakest assumption, and the over-engineering warning.

---

> | Option | What it is | Strength | Weakness | Choose when |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | L4 forwarding | Connection/packet distribution | No HTTP parsing requirement | Limited application routing | TCP services or TLS pass-through |
> | L7 proxy | HTTP-aware forwarding | Routes and request policies | Parsing, TLS, and connection state | This HTTP API design |
> | Round robin | Cyclic assignment | Simple and low cost | Ignores variable work | Requests and capacities are uniform |
> | Power of two choices | Randomized local load comparison | Handles skew cheaply | Local load estimate can lag | Variable request duration |
> | Consistent hash | Affinity-based placement | Cache locality | Hot keys and rebalancing effects | Session or cache affinity is required |

**What this says in plain words**
Five options across two different decisions. The first two are about which layer to operate at; the last three are about how to pick a backend.

- **Row: L4 forwarding.**
  - Moves connections and packets without understanding what is inside them.
  - Strength: no parsing, so it is fast and it works for any protocol, including encrypted traffic it cannot read.
  - Weakness: it cannot route on a URL path, because it never sees one. One connection goes to one backend, whatever the client sends over it.
  - Right when: the service is not HTTP, or the client must terminate TLS at the backend itself.
- **Row: L7 proxy.**
  - Parses HTTP, so it can route per request rather than per connection.
  - Weakness: it must parse, it must hold TLS state, and it must track connections on both sides. That is memory and CPU per connection.
  - Worked example of the difference that decides it: two requests arrive on one HTTP/2 connection, one for `/v1/search` and one for `/v1/upload`, which are served by different pools. An L4 balancer must send both to the same place, because it only sees one connection. An L7 proxy routes each to its own pool.
- **Row: Round robin.**
  - Backend 1, 2, 3, 4, 1, 2, 3, 4.
  - Strength: nothing to measure and nothing to store.
  - Weakness, worked in full: requests take 5 milliseconds or 5 seconds depending on what they ask for. Round robin gives every backend the same *count* of requests, so a backend that happens to receive four 5-second requests in a row accumulates 20 seconds of work while its neighbour with four 5-millisecond requests is idle. Equal counts are not equal load.
- **Row: Power of two choices.**
  - Pick two backends at random, compare their in-flight counts, send to the lighter one.
  - Strength: it removes most of the skew of round robin without any coordination, because each proxy uses only its own local counts.
  - Weakness: the local estimate lags, which is the design's weakest assumption and is expanded below.
- **Row: Consistent hash.**
  - The same key always maps to the same backend, and adding or removing a backend moves only a small fraction of keys.
  - Strength: cache locality. Worked example: a backend that always receives requests for user 12345 keeps that user's data in its local cache, so the hit rate is high.
  - Weakness: a hot key gets no help, because it maps to exactly one backend. And any membership change moves some keys, which invalidates the caches they were relying on.

---

> - Base choice: L7 proxy with power of two choices.
>   - The deciding variable is whether request duration varies enough to make round robin skew visible.

**What this says in plain words**
Use an L7 proxy, and select with power of two choices. The single question that decides the selection algorithm is whether requests take meaningfully different amounts of time.

**Why it matters**
The comparison must end in a recommendation with a named deciding variable rather than a survey.

- Worked example of the decision flipping: every request is a fixed-size key lookup taking 3 milliseconds, on identical machines. Duration does not vary, so round robin produces no visible skew, and the simpler algorithm with no counters to maintain is the correct choice.

---

> - IC4 stretch — safety before availability
>   - Rank safety before availability for security failures.
>   - Choose panic behavior per route class.
>   - A health-check error cannot authorize bypassing backend authentication.

**What this says in plain words**
Three related rules about what degradation may and may not do.

1. **For security failures, safety outranks availability.**
   - Worked example: a backend's certificate has expired. The available choice is to keep sending traffic anyway. The safe choice is to stop. For a security failure, stop.
2. **Panic behavior is per route class, not per fleet.**
   - The same proxy can spread load under panic for a public read endpoint and reject for an administrative write endpoint.
3. **A health-check error cannot authorize bypassing backend authentication.**
   - This is the sharpest line in the section. Degradation may reduce *service*; it may never reduce *authorization*.
   - Worked example of the anti-pattern: the service that validates tokens is unreachable, so the proxy is configured to forward requests unvalidated "so the site stays up". The outage has now become an authentication bypass reachable by anyone who can cause that service to look unhealthy, which means an attacker can create the condition that disables the check.

**Why it matters**
It names a failure mode that looks like good engineering from inside. Every step toward "keep serving" is defensible on its own, and the last one silently removes an access control.

---

> - IC4 stretch — 10× load
>   - Measure the limiting resource.
>   - Packet rate may dominate small requests.
>   - Encryption may dominate new connections.
>   - Memory may dominate slow streams.
>   - Scale based on that resource rather than one universal requests/s threshold.

**What this says in plain words**
At ten times the load, the thing that runs out first depends entirely on the shape of the traffic. Three different shapes, three different limits.

1. **Packet rate dominates small requests.**
   - Worked example: 200-byte requests at 500,000 per second. Bandwidth is only about 100 megabytes per second, which is nothing. But that is 1,000,000 packets per second counting both directions, and the network interface and interrupt handling run out long before bandwidth does.
2. **Encryption dominates new connections.**
   - The expensive part of TLS is the handshake at connection setup, not the bulk encryption afterwards.
   - Worked example: 10,000 new connections per second, each costing a handshake with a private-key operation, versus 10,000 requests per second reusing 100 existing connections. The same request rate, and the first costs orders of magnitude more CPU. A client library that disables connection reuse changes your capacity by an order of magnitude without changing the request rate at all.
3. **Memory dominates slow streams.**
   - Worked example: 50,000 concurrent streaming downloads, each buffering 200 kilobytes, is 10 gigabytes of proxy memory at a request rate of almost nothing.
4. **So scale on the measured resource.**
   - "Our proxies handle 50,000 requests per second" is meaningless without saying which traffic shape produced that number.

---

> - IC4 stretch — cost and blast radius
>   - Separate tenant traffic into cells when shared ejection or TLS load becomes a large blast radius.
>   - Limit each cell's backend inventory.
>   - Route new traffic away from an overloaded cell.
>   - Existing connections still require a reconnect contract.
>   - Cost: cells strand capacity
>     - A cell sized for its peak idles at its trough.

**What this says in plain words**
Celling means splitting the fleet into independent groups that share nothing, each serving a fixed set of tenants.

1. **When to reach for it.**
   - When a shared mechanism has fleet-wide reach. Ejection and TLS load are the two named.
   - Worked example: a bug in outlier detection ejects backends too aggressively. In one shared fleet, every tenant is affected. In 10 cells, one tenant cohort is affected and nine are untouched.
2. **Limit each cell's backend inventory.**
   - A bounded cell has a bounded worst case, which is the whole point.
3. **Route new traffic away from an overloaded cell.**
   - New connections go elsewhere.
4. **Existing connections still need a reconnect contract.**
   - This is the honest caveat. Routing *new* traffic away does nothing for the connections already established against the overloaded cell.
   - Worked example: a cell is overloaded and new connections are steered away. 50,000 existing clients with open connections keep using it, because nothing moves a live connection. They only leave when they reconnect, so the drain rate is set by the client's connection lifetime, not by your routing change.
5. **The cost: stranded capacity.**
   - Worked example: 10 cells, each sized for its own peak of 10,000 requests per second, so 100,000 total provisioned. Peaks do not align, so actual aggregate peak is 60,000. You are paying for 40% more capacity than a shared fleet would need, and buying containment with it.

---

> - IC4 stretch — configuration rollout
>   - Distribute immutable configuration generations and swap the active snapshot atomically per proxy.
>   - Expose desired versus applied version so operators see convergence, not a fleet-wide claim.

**What this says in plain words**

1. **Immutable generations, swapped atomically.**
   - A configuration version is never edited in place. A new complete version is distributed, and the proxy switches its active pointer in one step.
   - Worked example of in-place editing: the proxy is applying 40 route changes one at a time. A request arriving midway sees 20 new routes and 20 old ones, a combination that was never valid and was never tested.
2. **Expose desired versus applied version.**
   - Desired is what the control plane wants everyone on. Applied is what each proxy actually loaded.
   - Worked example: the dashboard says "version 12 rolled out". In reality 8 proxies applied 12 and 2 are stuck on 10 because they cannot reach the control plane. A single fleet-wide claim hides this; a desired-versus-applied view shows 8 of 10 converged and names the stragglers.

---

> - Weakest assumption
>   - Local in-flight counts approximate backend load
>   - A backend slowed by a shared dependency looks equally loaded from every proxy until latency-based outlier detection catches it.
>   - Mitigation: add bounded passive ejection on latency percentiles and cap the ejected fraction.

**What this says in plain words**
The design's foundation is that counting your own outstanding requests to a backend tells you how loaded that backend is. Here is where that is false.

1. **The assumption.**
   - Each proxy counts only its own in-flight requests to each backend, with no coordination.
2. **Where it breaks.**
   - Worked example: backend 3 shares a database connection pool with a batch job that just started. Its responses now take 800 milliseconds instead of 20. Every proxy has, say, 4 requests in flight to it, the same as to every other backend. In-flight count says backend 3 is normal, while it is in fact 40× slower. The counts only rise once enough slow requests accumulate, which is after users have already felt it.
3. **The mitigation, in two halves.**
   - *Passive ejection on latency percentiles*: watch the latency of real traffic, not just probe results, and eject a backend whose p99 is an outlier against its peers. Passive means it uses production traffic as the signal rather than a synthetic probe, which is what catches a dependency-induced slowdown that a health endpoint does not touch.
   - *Cap the ejected fraction*: never eject more than a set share of the pool, typically 10 to 50%.
   - Worked example of why the cap is required: the shared database slows down, so *all* backends become latency outliers relative to their own history. Uncapped ejection removes the entire pool and turns a slowdown into a total outage. The cap means at most a bounded slice is removed, and the rest keep serving slowly, which is far better than not at all.

---

> - Over-engineering warning
>   - Do not promise transparent connection migration or globally synchronized least-load selection.

**What this says in plain words**
Two specific things not to claim.

1. **Transparent connection migration.**
   - Moving a live TCP connection from one proxy to another without the client noticing.
   - Worked example of the cost: the connection's TCP state, TLS session keys, and any partially-received request would all have to move, atomically, faster than the client's timeout. Systems that do this exist and are enormously complex. A reconnect contract, where the client simply reconnects and retries per the retry rules, delivers nearly the same user outcome for almost nothing.
2. **Globally synchronized least-load selection.**
   - Every proxy knowing every backend's exact current load.
   - Worked example: 10 proxies and 100 backends. Keeping a synchronized view means continuous load broadcasts, and the view is stale by the time it is used anyway. Worse, synchronization creates herding: all proxies see the same "least loaded" backend simultaneously and all send to it, which is precisely the problem power of two choices exists to avoid.

**Why it matters**
Both are sophisticated-sounding answers that make the system worse. Naming them is how you show the simpler choice was deliberate.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| L4 forwarding | Moving connections or packets without inspecting the application protocol. | The alternative to an HTTP-aware proxy. |
| L7 proxy | A proxy that parses HTTP and can route per request. | The base choice for this design. |
| TLS pass-through | Forwarding encrypted traffic without decrypting it, so the backend terminates TLS. | A case where L4 is the only option. |
| Power of two choices | Sampling two backends at random and sending to the one with fewer in-flight requests. | The selection algorithm, chosen for variable request duration. |
| Consistent hash | Mapping keys to backends so a membership change moves only a small fraction of keys. | Right only when affinity is required. |
| Deciding variable | The single question whose answer picks between options. | Here: whether request duration varies enough for round-robin skew to show. |
| Panic behavior | What the proxy does when so many backends look unhealthy that health data is suspect. | Set per route class, not per fleet. |
| Authentication bypass | Skipping identity verification, often introduced by a well-meant degradation path. | The specific thing degradation may never do. |
| Packet rate | Packets per second, independent of how many bytes they carry. | The limiting resource for small requests. |
| TLS handshake cost | The expensive setup at connection establishment, distinct from cheap bulk encryption. | Makes connection reuse worth an order of magnitude of capacity. |
| Connection reuse | Sending many requests over one established connection. | A client library disabling it changes capacity without changing request rate. |
| Cell | An independent slice of the fleet serving a fixed tenant cohort, sharing nothing with other cells. | Contains a fleet-wide mechanism's blast radius. |
| Stranded capacity | Provisioned capacity that cannot be used because it sits in a cell that is idle. | The price paid for containment. |

## Level signal — IC3 baseline vs IC4 stretch

One row per interview phase: what must land, what raises it, and the observable that separates the two.

---

> | Phase | IC3 must land | IC4 adds | Separating signal |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Requirements | L7 choice, TLS termination, retry-safety of writes, drain deadline | Per-route panic policy and tenant-scoped configuration | Negotiates the retry contract instead of assuming it |
> | Estimates | In-flight count, buffer memory, probe rate | Names packet rate or handshakes as the real limit for the given payload | A number changes the sizing resource |
> | Architecture | Front door, proxy fleet, config plane with one trace | Front door balances connections not requests; `202` means accepted not applied | Every component has a mechanism and a price |
> | Deep dive | Power of two on in-flight, hysteresis, one bounded retry, drain | Local-view limits, counter-leak failure, correlated-outage ejection cap | Reasons about stale and partial evidence |
> | Operations | Failure table, canary, last-known configuration | Cells, retry budget as an outage control, safety-first panic | Volunteers control-plane outage behavior |
> | Wrap | Recap and local-versus-global tradeoff | Names local load estimation as the weakest assumption | Identifies the flaw before the interviewer does |

**What this says in plain words**
A self-assessment grid. The middle column is a checklist; the fourth column is what actually distinguishes a stronger answer.

**Why the fourth column is the one to score**
The difference is rarely more content. It is a different *kind* of statement about the same content.

- **Row: Requirements.**
  - Must land: layer 7 rather than layer 4, TLS terminates at the proxy, which writes may be retried, and how long a drain waits.
  - Adds: panic policy per route class, and configuration scoped per tenant.
  - Separating signal: you *negotiate* the retry contract rather than assuming one.
  - Worked example of the difference: "I will retry failed requests" assumes retries are safe. "Can I assume writes carry an idempotency key, or should the proxy only retry connect-phase failures?" establishes the contract with the interviewer and makes everything downstream defensible.
- **Row: Estimates.**
  - Must land: concurrent in-flight requests, buffer memory, and probe traffic.
  - Adds: identifying which resource actually runs out for this traffic shape.
  - Separating signal: "a number changes the sizing resource".
  - Worked example of failing it: reciting "50,000 requests per second, 200 kilobytes buffered each" and moving on. Nothing was decided. Passing it: "at 200-byte payloads this is a packet-rate problem, not a bandwidth problem, so I size on network interface capacity".
- **Row: Architecture.**
  - Must land: front door, proxy fleet, configuration plane, and one request traced through them.
  - Adds: two specific precisions. The front door balances *connections*, not requests, so a client that opens one connection and sends 10,000 requests through it lands entirely on one proxy. And `202` means the configuration was accepted, not that it is in force anywhere.
  - Separating signal: every component states both its mechanism and its price.
- **Row: Deep dive.**
  - Must land: power of two choices on in-flight counts, hysteresis to stop flapping, one bounded retry, and the drain sequence.
  - Adds: the limits of a purely local view, what happens if in-flight counters leak, and capping ejection during a correlated outage.
  - Separating signal: reasoning about evidence that is stale or partial.
  - Worked example of the counter leak, since it is the least obvious item: a request is cancelled by the client, and the code path that decrements the in-flight counter is skipped. That backend's count is permanently one too high. After a few thousand such events, the proxy believes the backend is heavily loaded and stops sending it work, while the backend sits idle. Nothing errors, and the symptom is quiet capacity loss.
- **Row: Operations.**
  - Must land: the failure table, canary rollout, and forwarding from the last-known configuration.
  - Adds: cells, the retry budget framed as an outage control rather than a client-experience tweak, and safety-first panic.
  - Separating signal: volunteering the control-plane outage behavior rather than being asked.
- **Row: Wrap.**
  - Must land: the recap and the local-versus-global tradeoff.
  - Adds: naming local load estimation as the weakest assumption.
  - Separating signal: finding the flaw before the interviewer does.

**How to use this table in preparation**
Rehearse the whole answer, then score only the fourth column. Each row is a yes-or-no observable rather than an impression.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Retry contract | The explicit agreement about which failures may be retried and under what conditions. | Negotiating it, rather than assuming it, is the requirements-phase signal. |
| Drain deadline | The maximum time a drain waits before terminating what remains. | A required requirements-phase item. |
| Hysteresis | Requiring several consecutive observations before changing state, so a component does not flip on one reading. | Stops a backend flapping between healthy and unhealthy. |
| Counter leak | An in-flight counter that is incremented but not decremented on some path, permanently overstating load. | Quiet capacity loss with no error to alarm on. |
| Correlated outage | Many backends failing together, usually from a shared dependency. | The case where uncapped ejection removes the whole pool. |
| Connection-level balancing | The front door distributing connections, not individual requests. | One client's connection sends all its requests to one proxy. |
| `202` | The HTTP status code meaning accepted for processing, not completed. | A configuration write is stored, not yet in force fleet-wide. |
| Local view | Each proxy deciding from only its own observations, with no coordination. | Cheap and coordination-free, but lagging under skew. |
| Separating signal | A specific observable that distinguishes a stronger answer from a merely complete one. | The fourth column, and the only one worth scoring. |

## Interview pushback and validation

Eight challenges, each with a baseline and a stretch answer, then seven things to test.

---

> - Q1: Are the proxies stateless?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Their configuration is reconstructible, but connections and TLS state are local.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Decide whether reconnects meet the service contract before adding state replication.

**What is really being asked**
"Stateless" is a word people use loosely. The interviewer is checking whether you distinguish configuration state from connection state.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Two kinds of state, with different properties.

1. **Configuration state is reconstructible.** A proxy that restarts pulls the current snapshot and is identical to its peers. Nothing unique was lost.
2. **Connection and TLS state is local and not reconstructible.** The TCP sequence numbers, the negotiated session keys, and any partially-received request exist only on that machine.
   - Worked example: a proxy holding 20,000 connections restarts. All 20,000 are gone, and no other proxy can take them over, because it does not have the keys. The clients must reconnect.
   - So the accurate statement is "stateless with respect to configuration, stateful with respect to connections", and answering a flat "yes" is wrong.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Before building connection-state replication, ask whether you need it.

- Worked example: clients reconnect in under 100 milliseconds and retry idempotent requests automatically. A proxy loss costs those clients a brief blip. Replicating TLS session state across the fleet to avoid that blip costs continuous cross-proxy traffic and a large amount of complexity, permanently, to improve a rare event.
- The ordering is the signal: contract first, mechanism second.

---

> - Q2: Why not least connections?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Requests multiplex over connections and have different durations.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Include observed work cost or latency only with stability controls against feedback oscillation.

**What is really being asked**
Least connections is a standard algorithm. Why is this design counting in-flight *requests* instead?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Two reasons, and both are needed.

1. **Multiplexing breaks the connection-to-work relationship.**
   - Worked example: with HTTP/2, backend A has 1 connection carrying 100 concurrent requests, and backend B has 50 connections each idle. Least connections picks A, which is carrying 100 times more work.
2. **Duration varies.**
   - Even counting requests, a count is not load. Four 5-second requests are far more load than four 5-millisecond ones.
   - In-flight request count is closer to real load than connection count, which is why it is the chosen metric, but it is still an approximation, which is the acknowledged weakest assumption.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
You could weight by observed latency or cost, but only with damping.

- Worked example of the oscillation without it: backend 3's latency rises, so it is weighted down and receives almost no traffic. With no traffic it looks fast again, so it is weighted up and receives a flood. The flood makes it slow. It oscillates between starved and hammered, and the oscillation is caused entirely by the control loop, not by anything the backend is doing.
- Stability controls means smoothing over a window, limiting how fast weights may change, and requiring a minimum share so a backend never goes fully dark.

---

> - Q3: Every backend returns `500`. Should we eject all of them?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Diagnose a shared dependency and apply bounded ejection.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Distinguish application failure from security failure in panic policy.

**What is really being asked**
Whether you follow the ejection rule off a cliff.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
No. When *everything* fails identically, the common cause is almost never every backend independently breaking.

- Worked example: all 20 backends return `500` because the database they share is down. Ejecting all 20 changes the client's error from `500` to `503` and guarantees that even requests which do not touch the database now fail. You removed the only capacity that might have served something.
- Bounded ejection means a cap on the ejected fraction, so a shared-dependency failure can never remove the whole pool.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
The reason for the `500` matters.

- An application failure is a good reason to keep serving degraded: some requests may still succeed.
- A security failure is not. Worked example: every backend returns `500` because it cannot reach the token validation service. Continuing to send traffic means requests reach backends that cannot verify who is calling. That is not degraded service, it is unauthenticated service, and the correct behavior is `503`.

---

> - Q4: The interviewer now requires raw TCP with end-to-end TLS.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Move to L4 connection routing and remove HTTP path inspection.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Relocate tenant-aware request limiting to the application boundary.

**What is really being asked**
Can you recognize that a requirement change invalidates the layer choice, instead of defending the design you built?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
End-to-end TLS means the client and backend hold the keys and the proxy cannot read the traffic. So every layer 7 feature disappears at once.

- Worked example of what is lost: path-based routing, per-request retries, request-level policy, and HTTP header inspection all require reading the request. None of them are possible.
- What remains is layer 4: distribute connections, watch connection health, and forward bytes.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
The capabilities do not vanish, they move.

- Per-tenant request limiting has to happen where the traffic is readable, which is now the application.
- The strong version names the cost of the move: the limit is now enforced by every service independently rather than once at a shared boundary, so it must be implemented consistently in each of them, and a service that forgets is an unprotected entry point.

---

> - Q5: How do you retry a timed-out POST?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Only through a server-enforced idempotency contract.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Account for streaming body replay and exhausted client deadlines.

**What is really being asked**
The direct test of whether you will retry an ambiguous write.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
You do not, unless the server guarantees a repeat is safe.

- A timeout after the request was sent means the backend may have applied it. There is no way to tell from the proxy.
- Worked example: a `POST /payments` times out. The payment may have been taken. Retrying may take it twice.
- The only safe mechanism is server-enforced: the client sends an idempotency key, the backend records it with the result, and a repeat with the same key returns the stored result instead of acting again. "Server-enforced" is the operative phrase; a client-side convention provides no guarantee.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Two further obstacles, even with a contract.

1. **Streaming body replay.**
   - Worked example: the client is uploading a 2 gigabyte file as a stream. To retry, the proxy would have to replay 2 gigabytes it did not keep. Buffering every request body to make retries possible would consume proxy memory proportional to request size, which is exactly the slow-client memory problem from the failure table.
2. **Exhausted deadlines.**
   - Worked example: the client's deadline was 5 seconds and the timeout fired at 4.9. The retry has 100 milliseconds. It will fail, and it costs the backend a full duplicate request whose result nobody will see.

---

> - Q6: What happens to a long stream during deployment?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Drain with a documented deadline and reconnect behavior.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Use application checkpoints if resumability is required.

**What is really being asked**
Draining is easy for 50-millisecond requests. What about a connection that is open for an hour?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
The drain deadline is an upper bound, so a long stream is cut when it expires.

- Worked example: the drain deadline is 30 seconds and a client is 10 minutes into a 1-hour server-sent-events stream. At 30 seconds it is terminated. The client must reconnect.
- The honest framing is that this is not a bug, it is the documented contract. What matters is that the deadline and the reconnect expectation are written down, so client authors build for it.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
If losing stream position is unacceptable, the fix belongs in the application, not the proxy.

- Worked example: the stream carries a sequence number, and the client stores the last one it received. On reconnect it sends `Last-Event-ID: 4821` and the server resumes from there.
- This is the general shape: the proxy cannot make a stream resumable, because it does not know what the bytes mean. Only the application knows where a safe resume point is.

---

> - Q7 (premise reversal): Why a proxy fleet at all? Give clients the backend list and balance client-side.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Clients then own health, retries, and TLS to every backend, and a bad client library becomes a fleet-wide incident.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Client-side balancing fits internal, trusted, homogeneous callers with a lookaside control plane
>     - Public and multi-tenant traffic still needs the proxy boundary.

**What is really being asked**
The premise reversal, and the most valuable question in the set. Maybe the entire proxy fleet is unnecessary.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Every responsibility the proxy holds moves into every client.

1. **Clients own health checking.** Worked example: 5,000 client instances each probing 100 backends every 5 seconds is 100,000 probes per second of pure overhead, and every client reaches a slightly different conclusion.
2. **Clients own retry policy.** There is no shared retry budget, so nothing bounds amplification. Worked example: a partial outage, and 5,000 clients each independently decide to retry three times. The survivors receive 4× load with no mechanism able to stop it.
3. **Clients own TLS to every backend.** Every client needs the trust material and must handle rotation, so certificate rotation becomes a coordinated change across every client fleet instead of a proxy-side operation.
4. **A bad client library is a fleet-wide incident.** Worked example: a client library ships with a retry bug. It is deployed by 200 teams on their own schedules. There is no single place to fix it and no way to roll it back centrally. The proxy, by contrast, is one deploy you control.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Concede that the idea is right in its proper context rather than dismissing it.

- It works when callers are internal, trusted, and homogeneous, with a lookaside control plane. Lookaside means clients ask a central service which backends exist and are healthy, so the health decision stays centralized while the connection is direct. That removes a network hop and its latency.
- It does not work for public or multi-tenant traffic, for a specific reason: you cannot enforce policy inside code you do not control. Handing external callers the backend list gives up TLS termination, per-tenant limiting, and the ability to change routing at all.
- The closing line: client-side balancing moves the *decision* to the client, which is fine, but it also moves the *enforcement*, which is not.

---

> - Q8: The configuration service is down for an hour. What breaks?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Nothing on the request path
>     - Proxies forward from the last-known snapshot and edits return `503`.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Certificate expiry and revocation are the exceptions, so alarm on credential age and keep an emergency credential path.

**What is really being asked**
Does a control-plane outage become a data-plane outage?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Nothing on the request path. Proxies hold their snapshots and keep forwarding. Only configuration edits fail, with `503`.

- Worked example: one hour at 50,000 requests per second is 180,000,000 requests served normally while the control plane is entirely down.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
There is a category that does *not* survive the outage, and naming it is the whole value of the answer.

- Configuration is static and keeps working. Certificates are not static: they expire on a schedule regardless of whether the control plane is available.
- Worked example: a certificate expires 40 minutes into the outage. The proxy cannot fetch a replacement. Every TLS handshake for that route now fails, and the outage that "did not affect the request path" just did.
- Two mitigations, and both are needed:
  - Alarm on credential age, so you know which certificates are close to expiry before an outage rather than during one.
  - Keep an emergency credential path independent of the normal control plane, so a certificate can be delivered when the usual route is unavailable.

> - Validation scenarios
>   - Zero and one eligible backend.
>   - Drain racing with selection.
>   - Cancellation releasing in-flight counters.
>   - Correlated probe failure.
>   - Malformed snapshot rollback.
>   - Proxy loss with an in-flight non-idempotent write.
>   - Configuration service outage with a certificate near expiry.

**What this says in plain words**
Seven tests, each mapping to a specific mechanism discussed earlier.

1. **Zero and one eligible backend.**
   - The boundary cases of power of two choices, which is defined as "pick two". With one backend there is nothing to compare; with zero there is nothing to pick.
   - Worked example of the bug this finds: an implementation that samples two indices from an empty list and crashes, or one that loops forever trying to find two distinct backends when only one exists.
2. **Drain racing with selection.**
   - Worked example: a backend is selected at the same instant it enters draining. Assert the request is either sent before the state change or routed elsewhere, and never sent to a backend already marked drained.
3. **Cancellation releasing in-flight counters.**
   - The direct test for the counter leak from the level-signal section. Cancel requests mid-flight and assert every counter returns to zero.
4. **Correlated probe failure.**
   - Make every probe fail at once and assert the ejection cap holds and panic policy applies, rather than the pool being emptied.
5. **Malformed snapshot rollback.**
   - Send an invalid configuration and assert the proxy keeps the previous valid one rather than adopting a broken state or crashing.
6. **Proxy loss with an in-flight non-idempotent write.**
   - Assert the client sees a connection failure and that no automatic retry occurs, since that write is ambiguous.
7. **Configuration service outage with a certificate near expiry.**
   - The direct test of the Q8 stretch answer. Assert the credential-age alarm fires and the emergency path delivers.

**Why it matters**
Each item names a mechanism that could be wrong and the observation that would reveal it. That is what turns a design into something testable.

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Reconstructible state | State a restarted instance can fetch again and be identical to its peers. | Configuration is; connections are not. |

## Recall card

Four one-liners and a four-sentence closing script, memorized rather than improvised.

---

> - “I choose L7 for HTTP routing and keep connection ownership explicit.”

**What this says in plain words**
Two decisions in one sentence: operate at layer 7, and be explicit about which component owns which connection.

**Why it matters**
"Connection ownership explicit" is the part that carries weight.

- The client owns its connection to the proxy. The proxy owns its connection to the backend. They are separate, and neither is transferable.
- Worked example of why saying it matters: a proxy dies. Its client connections die with it, and nothing else can take them over, because the TLS keys were local. Having stated ownership up front, this is a consequence you predicted rather than a hole discovered later.

---

> - “Selection uses a local eligible snapshot and bounded in-flight work.”

**What this says in plain words**
Two properties of the selection algorithm: the candidate set comes from a locally-held, consistent snapshot, and the comparison is over a bounded count of outstanding work.

**Why it matters**
Both adjectives are defences.

- *Local* means no coordination, so selection costs microseconds and cannot be blocked by another component.
- *Bounded* means the in-flight count has a ceiling, so a stalled backend cannot accumulate work indefinitely.
- Worked example: a backend stalls. Without a bound, in-flight climbs to thousands and the proxy's memory grows with it. With a ceiling, new work stops at the limit and the stall is contained.

---

> - “Health checks, retry budgets, and draining prevent amplification.”

**What this says in plain words**
Three separate mechanisms, all aimed at the same failure: a small problem growing into a large one.

1. Health checks stop new work reaching a failing backend.
2. Retry budgets stop retries multiplying load during a partial outage.
3. Draining stops a planned removal producing errors.

**Why it matters**
Naming them as a set is the point. Amplification is the characteristic way a load balancer turns a minor incident into an outage, and each mechanism blocks a different amplification path.

---

> - “Configuration survives outages locally; failed connections still reconnect.”

**What this says in plain words**
One claim and one honest limit, deliberately in the same sentence.

- Configuration is held locally, so a control-plane outage does not touch the request path.
- Connections are not recoverable, so clients still reconnect when a proxy is lost.

**Why it matters**
Pairing the strength with the limit is what stops the claim being an overclaim.

- Worked example: 180,000,000 requests served during a one-hour control-plane outage, and 20,000 clients reconnecting when a single proxy dies. Both are true, and stating only the first would be misleading.

---

> - Wrap-up script
>   - “The design is an L7 proxy fleet behind a redundant front door, with per-request immutable configuration snapshots and local power-of-two selection.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence one: the architecture. Layer, topology, configuration model, and selection algorithm.

**Why it matters**
"Per-request immutable snapshots" is the precise phrase. Each request is evaluated against one complete, unchanging configuration version, so no request ever sees a half-applied change.

---

> -   - “The key invariant is that a request sees one consistent snapshot, drained backends receive no new work, and a retry never assumes the backend did nothing.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence two: three invariants in one breath.

1. One snapshot per request, never a mixture.
2. A drained backend receives no new work, and no probe result reverses that.
3. A retry never assumes the backend was untouched, unless a connect-phase failure proves it.

**Why it matters**
The third is the one interviewers press on, so stating it unprompted removes the question.

---

> -   - “The main tradeoff is local load estimation, which keeps selection coordination-free at the cost of a lagging view under skew.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence three: what you gave up and what you bought.

- Given up: an accurate, shared view of backend load.
- Bought: selection with no coordination, so it costs microseconds and cannot be blocked.
- Worked example of the cost: a backend slowed by a shared dependency looks normal by in-flight count until enough slow requests accumulate.

---

> -   - “My weakest assumption is that in-flight counts track real backend load; I would validate latency-based outlier detection under a shared-dependency slowdown first.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence four: the flaw, and the first thing you would test.

**Why it matters**
"Under a shared-dependency slowdown" is what makes it a real plan rather than a gesture. It names the exact condition where the assumption fails, which is the condition the test has to reproduce.

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Connection ownership | Which component holds a given connection, and the fact that it cannot be transferred. | Explains why a proxy loss forces client reconnects. |
| Eligible snapshot | The locally-held, consistent set of backends a request may be sent to. | The candidate set for selection. |
| Bounded in-flight | A ceiling on outstanding requests per backend. | Contains a stalled backend instead of letting work accumulate. |
| Amplification | A small failure growing into a larger one through retries or misrouted load. | The failure the three named mechanisms exist to prevent. |
| Per-request immutable snapshot | Each request evaluated against one complete, unchanging configuration version. | Guarantees no request sees a half-applied change. |
| Local load estimation | Judging backend load from a proxy's own counts alone, with no coordination. | The main tradeoff, and the weakest assumption. |
| Latency-based outlier detection | Identifying a slow backend by comparing its latency against its peers. | The mitigation for the weakest assumption. |
| Shared dependency | A resource several backends rely on, such as one database. | Slows every backend at once, which in-flight counts cannot see. |
