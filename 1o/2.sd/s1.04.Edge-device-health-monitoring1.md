## Interview framing

This section states the level you are aiming at, where the question came from, why the problem is hard, and the exact sentences to open with.

---

> - Target: OCI IC3 ownership of a bounded platform component.
>   - `IC3 baseline` is what must land in every phase.
>   - `IC4 stretch` adds architecture changes under stronger constraints.
>   - These are preparation labels, not Oracle's official rubric.

**What this says in plain words**
You answer as an engineer who owns one clearly-bordered piece of infrastructure, including its failures and its pager.
There are two tiers of answer. `IC3 baseline` is the floor. `IC4 stretch` is extra depth for when the interviewer pushes. Both labels are a study device, not a company grading scale.

**Why it matters**
Three separate points.

1. **Bounded controls scope.** Inside the box: receiving heartbeats, deciding state, alerting. Outside: what the device does with its workload, how it was manufactured, how the network was built.
2. **The baseline is a checklist, not an aspiration.** If you run long on the diagram and skip the coverage watchdog, you dropped a required item.
3. **The labels are yours, not Oracle's.** Saying "this is the IC4 answer" in the room claims knowledge of their rubric you do not have.

>   - It does not claim to reproduce Oracle's implementation or an interviewer's accepted answer.

**What this says in plain words**
Someone reported being asked this in a real Oracle interview, and that report is recorded in the bank file. The design here is this preparation's own answer, not a leaked Oracle design.

**Why it matters**
It separates two kinds of confidence: high that the question gets asked, none at all that this architecture is what the interviewer wanted. Present it as your reasoning, defensible on its merits.

---

> - Why this is hard
>   - Naive: a `last_seen` column per device, a cron scan for stale rows, and a ping to confirm.

**What this says in plain words**
The obvious first design has three parts.

1. A database column per device holding the time it last reported.
2. A scheduled job that scans for rows whose time is old.
3. A network ping to confirm a device is really down.

**Why it matters**
This is what everyone proposes, so the interviewer is waiting to see whether you know why it fails.

- Worked example of it working fine: 500 devices reporting every 60 seconds. That is about 8 updates per second and a 500-row scan every 30 seconds. Correct, cheap, and nothing more is needed.
- Naming the naive design first shows you are choosing complexity deliberately rather than out of habit.

---

> -   - First break: `1 million` devices at `15 s` is `66,667` writes/s plus a full scan every few seconds, and devices behind NAT cannot be pinged.

**What this says in plain words**
Three separate failures in one line.

1. **The write rate.**
   - 1,000,000 ÷ 15 = 66,667 row updates per second.
   - Worked example of the gap: a well-tuned relational database sustains a few thousand row updates per second on one table. You are asking for more than ten times that, continuously, forever, with no natural quiet period.
2. **The scan.**
   - To find stale rows you scan a million-row table every few seconds.
   - Worked example of the cost: a 1,000,000-row scan every 5 seconds is 200,000 rows examined per second purely to find the small number that expired. The scan also competes with the 66,667 writes per second for the same table.
   - And it bounds detection latency: you cannot detect faster than your scan interval, so a 60-second detection target forces a scan at least that often.
3. **NAT makes the ping impossible.**
   - NAT stands for network address translation. Devices behind it have no publicly reachable address; they can start a connection outward, but nothing outside can start one inward.
   - Worked example: a device in a factory behind a firewall on a private address. Your server has no route to it at all. The confirmation ping is not slow or unreliable; it is simply impossible.

---

> -   - Second break: a timer fires while a fresh heartbeat lands, or one collector dies and `100,000` devices go silent at once
> -     - Stale expiry overwrites fresh evidence and monitoring failure looks like device failure.

**What this says in plain words**
Two more failures, and these are the ones the design actually exists to solve.

1. **A timer races a heartbeat.**
   - Worked example, step by step:
     - 14:00:00, the device reports. A timer is set for 14:00:45.
     - 14:00:44, the device reports again. Its deadline should move to 14:01:29.
     - 14:00:45, the old timer fires. It was scheduled when the last-seen time was 14:00:00, and it does not know about the newer heartbeat.
     - The device is marked suspect one second after a perfectly healthy report.
   - At 1,000,000 devices this race is not rare. It happens continuously.
2. **A collector dies and its devices go silent together.**
   - Worked example: 10 collectors, 100,000 devices each. One dies. Its 100,000 devices are still running perfectly and still sending heartbeats into a void.
   - Without a way to tell the difference, the system pages someone about 100,000 dead devices when nothing is wrong with any of them.
   - The second half of the sentence names both consequences: stale expiry overwrites fresh evidence, and monitoring failure is indistinguishable from device failure.

---

> -   - Minimum primitives
> -     - A registry of expected devices
> -     - Heartbeats ordered by incarnation and sequence
> -     - A per-shard deadline index with version-checked expiry
> -     - Ownership epochs
> -     - A separate coverage watchdog that can say `UNKNOWN`.

**What this says in plain words**
Five parts you cannot drop.

1. **A registry of expected devices.**
   - You cannot notice that something is missing unless you knew it was supposed to be there. Silence from a device you never heard of is not an incident.
2. **Heartbeats ordered by incarnation and sequence.**
   - Incarnation identifies one boot session of the device. Sequence numbers the heartbeats within that session.
   - Worked example of needing both: a device reboots and starts its sequence at 1 again. Without an incarnation, the new heartbeat number 1 looks older than the pre-reboot number 4,000 and is discarded, so the freshly-rebooted device looks dead.
3. **A per-shard deadline index with version-checked expiry.**
   - An ordered structure listing which device expires next, plus a version stamp so a timer can tell whether its information is stale.
4. **Ownership epochs.**
   - A number that increases each time a shard changes owner, so writes from an old owner can be rejected.
5. **A separate coverage watchdog that can say `UNKNOWN`.**
   - Something outside the collectors that checks whether the collectors are working, and a state meaning "I do not know", distinct from both up and down.

**Why it matters**
These five give you a recovery path. If you lose your place in the interview, walk the five primitives, and each leads to a concrete part of the design.

---

> - 🗣 “Silence is missing evidence, not proof; the design makes suspicion versioned and monitoring blindness visible.”

**What this says in plain words**
A quotable one-liner. The 🗣 marker means say it roughly verbatim. Three claims compressed:

1. Silence does not prove anything, it is only the absence of evidence.
2. Suspicion carries a version, so it can be checked for staleness.
3. When monitoring itself fails, that fact is visible rather than disguised as device failure.

**Why it matters**
It reframes the question within ten seconds.

- Most candidates treat this as "detect when a device is down", which is a problem with no correct solution, because you cannot distinguish a crashed device from an unreachable one.
- This sentence says you already know that, which lets you spend your deep-dive minutes on the parts that *are* solvable: making suspicion ordered and making blindness visible.

---

> - Opening script
>   - “I will treat this as a registry-driven heartbeat system with a versioned deadline index and an explicit distinction between device suspicion and monitoring failure.”

**What this says in plain words**
The first sentence. Four decisions at once: driven by a registry, based on heartbeats, deadlines carry versions, and device failure is kept distinct from monitoring failure.

**Why it matters**
The interviewer now has a map before you draw anything. A disagreement surfaces in the first 30 seconds rather than at minute 35.

---

> -   - “First I confirm fleet size, heartbeat cadence, NAT constraints, and how fast a silent device must be reported.”

**What this says in plain words**
Four questions to ask before designing, each of which changes the design.

1. **Fleet size** sets the ingest rate and therefore the number of shards.
2. **Cadence** interacts with fleet size to give writes per second, and sets how quickly silence becomes meaningful.
3. **NAT constraints** decide whether pull verification is possible at all.
4. **Detection target** sets the timeout, the tick size, and how much expiry work per second you must sustain.

**Why it matters**
Worked example of the fourth changing everything: a 60-second target with a 15-second cadence means a device may miss three heartbeats before you act. A 5-second target would mean acting on a single missed heartbeat, which makes every transient network blip a page.

---

> -   - “Then I size ingest, hot state, and history, which decides sharding and why transitions rather than raw heartbeats are persisted.”

**What this says in plain words**
Second phase: three estimates, and the two decisions they force.

**Why it matters**
It pre-announces the conclusion the numbers will produce, which is the discipline the whole file follows: an estimate exists to force a decision.

- Worked example of the history estimate: 66,667 heartbeats per second is about 5.76 billion per day. Storing each as a row is not a storage problem you tune your way out of; it is a reason to store something else entirely.

---

> -   - “The deep dive is the deadline algorithm: how a stale timer or an old collector owner is prevented from overwriting fresh evidence.”

**What this says in plain words**
Names the deep dive in advance, and names both races: a stale timer, and an old owner.

**Why it matters**
Interviewers often ask what you would like to go deeper on, and a candidate who has not pre-chosen wanders. Pre-announcing also lets the interviewer redirect you at minute 17 rather than minute 40.

---

> -   - “I will close with what happens when the monitoring itself fails, alert grouping, and where the design changes at ten times the fleet.”

**What this says in plain words**
Announces the ending: monitoring self-failure, alert grouping, and behavior at 10 million devices.

**Why it matters**
"What happens when the monitoring itself fails" is the highest-value item to volunteer.

- It is the question most candidates never reach, and the one an experienced operator asks immediately.
- Worked example of why it is not academic: during a real incident, the first question is always "is the service down, or is the dashboard broken?" A design that cannot answer that is not operable.

## Requirements — IC3 baseline

Six parts: the numbers warning, the clarifying table, functional scope, the honesty paragraph about what health means, the numeric target table, and the invariants plus non-goals.

---

> - Numerical targets are interview assumptions unless an adjacent primary source states otherwise.

**What this says in plain words**
Every number here was chosen to make the design concrete. None came from Oracle unless a link says so.

**Why it matters**
Say "I will assume 1,000,000 devices; tell me if the real number differs" rather than stating it as fact. The first invites a correction, the second invites a question you cannot answer.

- Worked example: at 1,000,000 devices you need sharding and an in-memory deadline index. At 5,000 devices the naive design is correct and the entire discussion is wasted interview time.

---

> | Question | Assumed answer | Why it changes the design |
> |---|---|---|
> | Fleet size and cadence? | `1 million` devices, `15 s` heartbeat with jitter | Fixes ingest rate, shard count, and history volume |
> | Can the service reach devices? | Many sit behind NAT; only an outbound channel exists | Push is primary; pull is a bounded secondary check |
> | What does "health" mean? | Liveness of an authenticated agent; metrics live elsewhere | Keeps payloads small and this service separate from ingestion |
> | Detection target? | Suspect after `45 s`, published within `60 s` | Sets timeout, tick size, and the expiry-work budget |
> | What happens on detection? | Alert and dashboard only; no automated remediation | Removes the fencing burden from this service; keeps it a non-goal |
> | How is a reboot identified? | Enrolled incarnation plus sequence per incarnation | Ordering cannot rely on device clocks or random boot IDs |
> | Multi-tenant? | Yes, devices belong to tenants and sites | Scopes every read, quota, and alert group |

**What this says in plain words**
Seven questions, the answer assumed if the interviewer leaves it to you, and what each decides.

**Why the third column is the point**
A clarifying question that changes nothing is filler, and interviewers notice filler.

- **Row: Fleet size and cadence.**
  - 1,000,000 devices reporting every 15 seconds, with jitter.
  - Jitter means each device offsets its schedule by a small random amount.
  - Worked example of omitting jitter: all million devices boot after a regional power restoration and align on the same 15-second boundary. Every 15 seconds the service receives 1,000,000 heartbeats in a fraction of a second and nothing for the rest of the interval. The average is 66,667 per second; the peak is orders of magnitude higher, and the peak is what sizes the fleet.
- **Row: Can the service reach devices?**
  - Assumed no for many devices, so only an outbound channel exists.
  - This decides the entire shape: push primary, pull only as a bounded secondary check where a path happens to exist.
- **Row: What does health mean?**
  - Assumed: an authenticated agent is alive and communicating. CPU, memory, and disk metrics belong to a different service.
  - Worked example of why the boundary matters: including metrics turns a 200-byte heartbeat into a 20-kilobyte payload. At 66,667 per second that is 13 megabytes per second versus 1.3 gigabytes per second, a hundredfold change in every capacity number, to carry data another service already handles.
- **Row: Detection target.**
  - Suspect after 45 seconds of silence, published within 60.
  - 45 seconds is three missed heartbeats at a 15-second cadence, which tolerates two transient losses before raising suspicion.
  - The 15-second gap between 45 and 60 is the processing budget: timer granularity plus the time to publish downstream.
- **Row: What happens on detection?**
  - Alerts and dashboards only. No automated action.
  - Worked example of why this is a relief: if detection triggered automatic failover, a false positive would start a replacement while the original is still running, and you would need a fencing protocol to stop two owners writing to the same external state. Declaring remediation a non-goal removes that entire problem from this component.
- **Row: How is a reboot identified?**
  - An enrolled incarnation plus a per-incarnation sequence.
  - Two rejected alternatives, each for its own reason:
    - Device clocks: unsynchronized across a million field devices, so timestamps from different devices, or from one device before and after a reboot, cannot be ordered.
    - Random boot identifiers: a random value has no order. You can see that it changed but not which is newer, so a delayed heartbeat from the previous boot is indistinguishable from the current one.
- **Row: Multi-tenant?**
  - Yes, and this scopes reads, quotas, and alert grouping.
  - Worked example of the last one: a shared network failure affects devices belonging to three tenants. Grouping them into one incident would expose to each tenant that the others were affected, which is a disclosure. Alert groups stay inside a tenant even when the cause does not.

---

> - Functional scope
>   - Enroll a device and its active incarnation.
>   - Accept authenticated heartbeats and keep the latest observation.
>   - Project a state per device: `UP`, `SUSPECT`, `UNREACHABLE`, `UNKNOWN`.
>   - Suppress paging during maintenance without stopping observation.
>   - List devices by state per tenant
>   - Expose transition history.
>   - Group alerts by shared collector, network, or region failure.

**What this says in plain words**
Seven capabilities.

1. **Enroll a device and its incarnation.** Registration is an authenticated act, so a device cannot declare its own new incarnation to escape a revocation.
2. **Accept authenticated heartbeats and keep the latest observation.** Only the latest matters; older ones are discarded rather than accumulated.
3. **Project four states.** The four are worth separating clearly:
   - `UP`: a recent authenticated heartbeat exists.
   - `SUSPECT`: no heartbeat for longer than the timeout. Silence, nothing more.
   - `UNREACHABLE`: silence *plus* an independent check that also failed. Stronger evidence.
   - `UNKNOWN`: the system cannot currently observe this device, because monitoring itself is impaired.
   - Worked example of collapsing `UNKNOWN` into `SUSPECT`: a collector dies, and 100,000 devices are reported suspect. The operator sees a catastrophic fleet failure. With `UNKNOWN`, the operator sees "monitoring is blind for 100,000 devices", which is a different incident with a different response.
4. **Suppress paging during maintenance without stopping observation.** The two must be separate: a device under maintenance should not page anyone, but its state must still be recorded, or you emerge from a maintenance window with no idea what happened during it.
5. **List devices by state per tenant.** Paged, tenant-scoped.
6. **Expose transition history.** The record of state changes over time, which is what an investigation reads.
7. **Group alerts by shared cause.** Covered in its own section.

---

> - Health is an observation, not certainty about physical failure.
>   - Heartbeat presence indicates recent communication from an authenticated agent.
>   - Silence may mean agent failure, network failure, or monitoring failure.
>   - A failed verification probe does not prove that a device is dead.

**What this says in plain words**
The epistemic honesty paragraph, and it is the intellectual core of the question.

1. **A heartbeat proves one thing only**: an authenticated agent communicated recently. It does not prove the device is healthy, that its application works, or that its disk is intact.
2. **Silence has at least three causes**, and from the server they are indistinguishable:
   - The agent crashed.
   - The network between is broken.
   - Your own monitoring is broken.
3. **A failed probe adds evidence, not proof.** Worked example: the probe travels the same network path the heartbeats used. If that path is broken, the probe fails for the same reason the heartbeats stopped, and you have confirmed the path is broken rather than learning anything new about the device.

**Why it matters**
This is a genuine impossibility result, not a design shortcoming. In an asynchronous network you cannot distinguish a crashed process from a slow or partitioned one, because either way you are waiting for a message that has not arrived.

- The practical consequence is the state vocabulary: `SUSPECT` and `UNREACHABLE` describe *your evidence*, not the device's condition. A design that has a `DEAD` state is claiming knowledge it cannot have.

---

> - Assumed scope
>   - `1 million` registered devices.
>   - Some devices sit behind NAT and cannot receive unsolicited probes.
>   - Device liveness is primary.
>   - Detailed metrics use the separate ingestion service.

**What this says in plain words**
Four assumptions restated compactly, so they can be checked at a glance. Nothing new; this is the summary the interviewer can challenge in one line.

Eight promises, each with a number and the mechanism that keeps it.

**Why it matters**
A requirement without a number cannot be violated, so it cannot be designed against.

- **Row: Heartbeat interval, 15 seconds with jitter.**
  - The server rejects any device reporting faster than every 5 seconds.
  - Worked example of why the server enforces it: a firmware bug makes 10,000 devices report every 100 milliseconds. That is 100,000 heartbeats per second from 1% of the fleet, more than the entire fleet's normal load. The server-side floor contains a client-side bug, which is the general principle: never let a client's misbehavior set your load.
- **Row: Suspect threshold, 45 seconds.**
  - Three missed heartbeats.
- **Row: Suspect publication within 60 seconds.**
  - Decomposed: 45 seconds of silence, plus up to 5 seconds of timer granularity, plus the time to drain the outbox. The budget is 15 seconds for the last two.
  - "under healthy collector scheduling" is the defeater: if the collector is overloaded, this bound does not hold, and the honest statement includes that condition.
- **Row: Grouped operator alert within 120 seconds.**
  - When observation is unavailable, a human hears about it within two minutes.
  - Enforced by the watchdog, and the word *independent* is what makes it meaningful. A watchdog running inside the collectors cannot report that the collectors are down.
- **Row: Heartbeat acceptance, 99.95% regional.**
  - Worked example: 0.05% of 66,667 per second is about 33 heartbeats per second rejected or lost. Acceptable because a lost heartbeat is not a lost device; the next one arrives 15 seconds later and refreshes the deadline. The design tolerates individual loss by construction, which is why the suspect threshold is three intervals rather than one.
- **Row: Ingest capacity, 66,667 per second with 2× headroom.**
  - The headroom exists for reconnect storms. Worked example: a regional network partition heals, and every device that has been retrying reconnects. Momentary load is several times steady state, and without headroom the recovery itself takes the service down.
- **Row: Dashboard read, p99 under 500 milliseconds.**
  - Enforced by cursor paging over a state index, and by forbidding fleet-wide synchronous scans.
  - Worked example of the forbidden thing: "show me all suspect devices" over a million rows with no index is the same full scan the naive design was rejected for, just moved to the read path.
- **Row: Stale expiry accepted, zero by construction.**
  - The strongest row in the table. Not "rare", but zero, and the phrase *by construction* means the mechanism makes it impossible rather than unlikely.
  - The mechanism: the version compare happens under the shard's serialization, so a timer holding version 7 cannot act on a device now at version 8.

---

> - Invariants
>   - Registry defines which devices are expected to report.
>   - An old heartbeat cannot overwrite a newer device incarnation/sequence.
>   - A stale expiry task cannot mark a recently refreshed device suspect.
>   - Loss of collector coverage must be visible as `UNKNOWN` or `MONITORING_DEGRADED`.
>   - A transition is recorded before its projection is replaced, so evidence for an alert is never lost.

**What this says in plain words**
Five statements true at every instant.

1. **The registry defines expectations.** A device not in the registry is not monitored, and its silence is not an incident.
2. **An old heartbeat cannot overwrite a newer one.** Worked example: a heartbeat delayed 30 seconds in the network arrives after a fresher one. Without ordering, it would move the last-seen time *backwards*, shortening the device's deadline and possibly marking a healthy device suspect.
3. **A stale expiry task cannot mark a refreshed device suspect.** The central race, and the reason versions exist.
4. **Lost coverage is visible.** Worked example of the alternative: the collector dies and its devices silently keep their last known state. The dashboard shows 100,000 devices `UP` forever, which is worse than showing them down, because it is confidently wrong.
5. **A transition is recorded before the projection is replaced.**
   - The projection is the current state. The transition is the record that it changed.
   - Worked example of the wrong order: the device goes `UP` to `SUSPECT`. You overwrite the current state first, then crash before writing the transition. The alert was never sent, and the record that anything happened does not exist. Recording the transition first means the worst case is a duplicate, which the deduplication key already handles.

---

> - Non-goals
>   - Metrics and log ingestion.
>   - Automated remediation or failover of the device's workload.
>   - Proving physical failure from silence.
>   - Firmware or configuration distribution.

**What this says in plain words**
Four things this system deliberately does not do.

1. **Metrics and logs.** A different service, with different volume and retention.
2. **Automated remediation.** Removes the need for a fencing protocol, as discussed above.
3. **Proving physical failure.** Explicitly out of scope because it is impossible, not because it is hard.
4. **Firmware or configuration distribution.** A device-management concern; mixing it in would put a large payload on the heartbeat path.

**Why it matters**
Non-goals are defensive. The third one in particular converts a weakness into a stated position: you are not failing to prove death, you declared that unprovable and designed a vocabulary that reflects it.

## Capacity and consequence — IC3 baseline

Five estimates. The rule is that each one ends in the decision it forces; an estimate that changes nothing is deleted.

---

> - Heartbeat load
>   - `1,000,000 / 15 ≈ 66,667 heartbeats/s`.
>   - At `200 B` payload, approximately `13.3 MB/s` before transport overhead.
>   - Ten logical shards each own about `100,000 devices` and `6,667 heartbeats/s`.
>   - Extra instances supply failover and skew headroom.
>   - Decision: shard by stable device identity with one active owner per shard
>     - A single database row update per heartbeat would need `66,667` writes/s and is rejected.

**What this says in plain words**

1. **The arrival rate.** A million devices divided by a 15-second interval is 66,667 arrivals per second.
2. **The bandwidth.** 66,667 × 200 bytes is about 13.3 megabytes per second. "Before transport overhead" matters: TLS records, HTTP headers, and TCP framing can double or triple that for small messages.
   - Worked example of the overhead being the story: a 200-byte payload inside an HTTP request with headers is realistically 600 to 800 bytes on the wire. The useful data is a quarter of the traffic.
3. **Ten shards.** 1,000,000 ÷ 10 = 100,000 devices each, and 66,667 ÷ 10 = 6,667 heartbeats per second each. That is a rate a single process handles comfortably.
4. **Extra instances for failover and skew.** Ten shards does not mean ten machines. Skew means devices are not perfectly evenly distributed, so some shards run hotter.

**Why it matters — the decision**
Two conclusions, and the second is the one to say aloud.

- **Shard by stable device identity, one active owner per shard.** *Stable* means the identity does not change when the device reboots or moves network, because a changing key would move the device to a different shard and lose its deadline.
- **Per-heartbeat database writes are rejected.** 66,667 row updates per second, continuously. Worked example of the gap: a well-tuned relational instance sustains a few thousand updates per second on one table. You are an order of magnitude over, forever, with no quiet period, and every update also competes with the expiry scan. This is not a tuning problem.

---

> - Hot state
>   - At an assumed `128 B/device`, logical state is about `128 MB` fleet-wide.
>   - Real indexes and runtime objects increase memory.
>   - Deadline bookkeeping is manageable if old timer entries do not grow without bound.
>   - Decision: keep latest state and the deadline index in memory per shard owner with a replicated checkpoint
>     - The store is for recovery, not the hot path.

**What this says in plain words**

1. **The size.** 128 bytes per device covers the incarnation, sequence, receive time, deadline, state, and version. A million of those is 128 megabytes.
2. **The real figure is larger.** Object headers, hash table overhead, and the ordered index itself all add. Even at 4× it is around 512 megabytes, which is ordinary.
3. **The caveat about old timer entries.** Worked example: every heartbeat inserts a new deadline entry without removing the old one. At 6,667 per second per shard, that is 24,000,000 stale entries per hour. Memory grows without bound and the index slows down. The design must either update entries in place or compact obsolete ones.

**Why it matters — the decision**
Keep the hot state in memory, and treat the database as a recovery checkpoint rather than the serving path.

- Worked example of the difference: a heartbeat updates an in-memory map and an in-memory ordered index, which is a few microseconds. Writing through to a database would be a few milliseconds, a thousandfold increase, on a path that runs 66,667 times per second.
- The word *replicated* on the checkpoint matters: a checkpoint on one machine's disk is lost with that machine.
- The cost paid, stated honestly: memory is not durable, so a restart loses the live state and must rebuild it, which is what the warm-up interval in the deep dive is for.

---

> - Deadline work
>   - `6,667` renewals/s per shard against a `100,000`-entry ordered index is about `6,667 × log2(100,000) ≈ 113,000` comparisons/s, which is trivial for one core.
>   - Decision: an indexed heap or sorted set is enough at this scale
>     - A timing wheel is a `10×` follow-up, not a baseline need.

**What this says in plain words**
The arithmetic that rules out premature optimization.

1. Each heartbeat moves one device's deadline, which costs about log₂(N) comparisons in an ordered structure.
2. log₂(100,000) is about 17.
3. 6,667 × 17 ≈ 113,000 comparisons per second.
4. A single core performs hundreds of millions of simple comparisons per second, so this uses a fraction of a percent of one core.

**Why it matters — the decision**
A heap or sorted set is sufficient, and a timing wheel is not needed until roughly ten times the load.

- A timing wheel is an array of buckets indexed by time, giving constant-time insertion instead of logarithmic. It is faster and meaningfully harder to get right.
- Worked example of the value of this estimate: without it, a candidate reaches for a timing wheel because it sounds impressive, spends deep-dive minutes explaining bucket rotation, and has demonstrated that they optimize without measuring. The 113,000 figure is what converts "we should use a timing wheel" into "a heap costs 0.1% of a core, so a wheel is a 10× follow-up".
- This is the clearest example in the file of an estimate whose purpose is to *prevent* work.

---

> - History
>   - Persisting every heartbeat creates `5.76 billion rows/day`.
>   - Persist status transitions and sampled evidence instead.
>   - Keep raw heartbeat diagnostics briefly only when needed for investigation.
>   - Decision: transitions are the durable record
>     - Raw heartbeats get a bounded retention window measured in hours.

**What this says in plain words**

1. **The row count.** 66,667 per second × 86,400 seconds is about 5.76 billion rows per day.
   - Worked example of the storage: at 100 bytes per row that is 576 gigabytes per day, or 210 terabytes per year, to record that healthy devices were healthy.
2. **Store transitions instead.**
   - Worked example of the ratio: a healthy device might change state twice a month. A million devices produces roughly 2,000,000 transitions per month, versus 172 billion heartbeats. That is a reduction of about five orders of magnitude, and the transitions contain all the information anyone actually queries.
3. **Keep raw heartbeats briefly.** Hours, not days, and only for investigating a live problem.

**Why it matters — the decision**
The insight is that the interesting information is in the *changes*, not the samples.

- Nobody asks "was device X up at 14:32:07 yesterday?". They ask "when did device X go down, and what else went down with it?", which is exactly a transition query.
- The general principle worth stating: store the events, not the state samples, when the state is stable most of the time.

---

> - Push and pull both create total work proportional to fleet size.
>   - Push helps NAT traversal and distributes sending schedules.
>   - It does not make central ingestion constant time for the whole fleet.
>   - Decision: push is the primary channel
>     - Pull is a rate-limited verifier for the suspect cohort only.

**What this says in plain words**
A correction of a common misconception.

1. **Both are O(N) at the centre.** Whether the device sends or the server asks, the server handles one message per device per interval.
2. **Push's real advantages are different.** It works through NAT, and the sending schedule is spread across a million independent devices rather than driven by one scheduler.
   - Worked example of the scheduling difference: a pull design needs a scheduler that initiates 66,667 probes per second, tracks 66,667 outstanding responses, and handles their timeouts. Push distributes that work to the devices, and the server only receives.
3. **But the centre still does O(N) work.** Saying "push scales better" without this qualifier is the mistake being corrected.

**Why it matters — the decision**
Push is primary. Pull survives only as a rate-limited verifier applied to the small suspect cohort.

- Worked example of the sizing difference: pulling the whole fleet is 66,667 probes per second. Pulling only the 1% that are suspect is 667, which is a hundredfold reduction and makes pull affordable precisely where it adds information.

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Transport overhead | The bytes added by TLS, HTTP, and TCP around the payload. | Can be three times the useful data for small messages. |
| Logical shard | One subset of the fleet with a single active owner, independent of how many machines exist. | Ten of them, not necessarily ten machines. |
| Skew | Uneven distribution of devices or traffic across shards. | The reason for headroom beyond the arithmetic minimum. |

## Architecture and flow — IC3 baseline

Diagram first, then who owns what with the price each ownership costs, then one heartbeat traced end to end.

---

> ```text
> Control plane: registry + enrollment + maintenance + shard assignments
>         |
>         v (assignments cached by collectors; static under registry outage)
> Device registry -> expected-device assignments -> regional collector shards
>                                                    ^
> Agent -> authenticated heartbeat ingress -----------+
>                                                    |
>                                      latest state + deadline index
>                                                    |
>                                      SUSPECT -> reachable verifier
>                                                    |
>                                 transition outbox -> history + alert grouping
>
> Coverage watchdog (independent) -> collector liveness -> UNKNOWN / MONITORING_DEGRADED
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
Three separate paths: configuration flowing down, heartbeats flowing in, and an independent watcher off to the side.

**Reading the control plane, top to bottom**

1. **Registry, enrollment, maintenance, shard assignments** are grouped as one plane because they share a property: they change rarely and are not on the request path.
2. **The arrow's annotation is the design statement.** "assignments cached by collectors; static under registry outage" means a collector keeps its assignment list locally, so the registry can be entirely down while heartbeat processing continues.
   - Worked example: the registry is offline for an hour. Collectors keep accepting and evaluating 66,667 heartbeats per second from their cached assignments. What stops is enrolling new devices and changing maintenance windows, which is the correct thing to have stopped.

**Reading the data plane**

1. **Agent → authenticated heartbeat ingress.** The device initiates, outward, which is what NAT permits.
2. **Ingress → collector shard.** Routed by stable device identity so a device always reaches the same shard.
3. **Shard → latest state + deadline index.** Both in memory, as decided in the capacity section.
4. **`SUSPECT` → reachable verifier.** Only devices that reached suspect enter the verifier, which is what keeps pull affordable.
5. **→ transition outbox → history + alert grouping.** Transitions are written to a durable outbox first, and history and alerting read from it.

**Reading the watchdog**
It hangs off the side, connected to nothing else, which is the point. It checks collector liveness and produces `UNKNOWN` or `MONITORING_DEGRADED`.

**Why it matters**
The diagram encodes two independence claims: the data plane is independent of the control plane, and the watchdog is independent of the collectors. Both are what make the failure story work, and both are visible in the shape before a word is spoken.

---

> - Registry is the control plane.
>   - Mechanism bought: one source of expected devices, active incarnations, and maintenance windows.
>   - Cost paid: collectors must cache assignments so heartbeat processing survives a registry outage
>     - Enrollment does not.

**What this says in plain words**
The registry is the single authority for three things: which devices are expected, which incarnation of each is current, and which are under maintenance.
The cost is that collectors must hold a cached copy, and the cache is what survives an outage. Enrollment cannot be cached, because it is a write.

**Why it matters**
The "mechanism bought / cost paid" structure repeats for every component, and it is the pattern the source's quality checklist demands: never name a component without saying what it costs.

- Worked example of the cache's limit: during a registry outage, a device enrolling for the first time cannot be added. Its heartbeats arrive for a device the collector has never heard of, and they are rejected. That is correct behavior, and it is a real gap worth stating rather than hiding.

---

> - Heartbeat ingress is the data plane entry.
>   - Mechanism bought: authenticates the device credential and routes by stable device identity to one shard.
>   - Cost paid: stateless fleet must be sized for the full ingest rate plus reconnect bursts after an outage.

**What this says in plain words**
Ingress does two jobs: verify who is calling, and route by device identity to the owning shard.
It holds no state, so any ingress node can handle any device, but the fleet must be sized for peak rather than average.

**Why it matters**
Statelessness buys easy scaling and costs the ability to absorb a burst by buffering.

- Worked example of the burst: a regional partition heals. Every device that was retrying reconnects. If half the fleet reconnects over 10 seconds, that is 50,000 connections per second on top of steady-state traffic, against a normal 66,667 heartbeats per second. Sizing for the average guarantees the recovery fails.
- This is the concrete reason the requirement table asks for 2× headroom.

---

> - Shard owner serializes updates.
>   - Mechanism bought: one active logical owner per shard makes version and deadline updates atomic without a distributed lock per heartbeat.
>   - Cost paid: physical failover needs a new ownership epoch, and downstream must reject an old owner's epoch.

**What this says in plain words**
This is the central architectural claim.

1. **One owner means no distributed locking.** Because only one process handles a given device, the version compare and the deadline update happen inside one process with no coordination at all.
   - Worked example of the alternative: a distributed lock per heartbeat is a network round trip before every update. At 66,667 heartbeats per second, that is 66,667 lock acquisitions per second, each costing more than the work it protects.
2. **The cost is failover complexity.** When ownership moves, the old owner may still be running and still believe it owns the shard.
   - Worked example: collector A becomes unreachable, so ownership moves to B. A was never actually dead, just partitioned, and it continues processing heartbeats it still receives and publishing transitions. Two owners are now writing contradictory state for the same devices.
   - The epoch is what resolves it: every publish carries the epoch it was made under, and downstream rejects anything below the current one. A's writes carry epoch 4 against a current epoch of 5 and are discarded.

---

> - Verifier supplies secondary evidence.
>   - Mechanism bought: an independent reachable path turns some `SUSPECT` cases into `UNREACHABLE` or back to `UP`.
>   - Cost paid: bounded concurrency and queue age
>     - Same-path probes add little independent evidence.

**What this says in plain words**
The verifier tries a second, different route to the device. Success restores `UP`. Failure strengthens `SUSPECT` into `UNREACHABLE`.
It costs a bounded concurrency budget and introduces a queue that can age.

**Why it matters**
The word *independent* carries the whole value.

- Worked example of a useless verifier: the heartbeats arrived over the internet through a particular transit provider, and the probe goes out the same way. The provider is having an outage. The probe fails for exactly the reason the heartbeats stopped. You spent a probe and learned nothing you did not already know.
- Worked example of a useful one: a local gateway at the device's site, reachable over a different network path, is asked whether it can see the device. That answer is genuinely new information.

---

> - Transition outbox and alert grouping own downstream fan-out.
>   - Mechanism bought: transitions are durable and deduplicated by `(device_id, transition_version)` before history and alerting consume them.
>   - Cost paid: alert latency includes outbox drain
>     - The outbox backlog is a monitored quantity.

**What this says in plain words**
An outbox is a durable queue written as part of the same operation that changes state. Consumers read from it afterwards.

1. **Durability and deduplication.** The key `(device_id, transition_version)` means the same transition can be delivered many times and applied once.
   - Worked example: the outbox consumer crashes after sending an alert but before recording that it did. On restart it re-reads the same transition. Without the deduplication key, the operator is paged twice for one event. With it, the second delivery is recognized and dropped.
2. **The cost is latency.** The alert is not sent at the moment of the transition; it is sent when the consumer drains the outbox.
   - Worked example: a backlog of 50,000 transitions draining at 5,000 per second means the newest alert is 10 seconds behind reality. That is invisible unless outbox age is measured, which is why the source names it a monitored quantity.

---

> - Coverage watchdog keeps monitoring self-health separate.
>   - Mechanism bought: an independent check of collector coverage
>     - Dashboards expose observation age.
>   - Cost paid: another component to keep alive
>     - Last-known `UP` is not presented as current `UP` during a blind interval.

**What this says in plain words**
Something outside the collectors checks that the collectors are working, and dashboards show how old each observation is.
The cost is one more component that itself needs to be alive, and a discipline about how state is displayed.

**Why it matters**
The second cost line is the important one, and it is easy to skip past.

- Worked example: a collector died 4 minutes ago. Its devices' last known state is `UP`. The dashboard could show `UP`, which is what the stored state says and is confidently wrong.
- The discipline: every state is displayed with its observation age, so the operator sees "UP, as of 4 minutes ago, coverage degraded" rather than "UP".
- The general principle: a cached value presented without its age is indistinguishable from a fresh one, and during an incident that difference is everything.
- The classic objection, worth having ready: who watches the watchdog? The answer is that the watchdog is far simpler than the collectors and can be monitored by an external, third-party checker, so the regress terminates at something outside your system entirely.

---

> → Authenticate the device credential and resolve its registry identity.  
> → Validate its registered incarnation and increasing sequence.  
> → Set last-seen from collector receive time.  
> → Atomically replace its deadline and latest state.  
> → Emit a transition only when the observable state changes.  
> → When a deadline expires, compare the current version before marking suspect.  
> → Verify through an independent reachable path when available.  
> → Group alarms by shared collector, network, or region failure.

**What this says in plain words**
One heartbeat traced end to end. Walked with a concrete device:

Device `edge-4471` at tenant `acme`, site `plant-3`, sends a heartbeat with incarnation `inc-9` and sequence `4,122`.

1. **Authenticate and resolve identity.**
   - The client certificate verifies, and it maps to registry identity `(acme, edge-4471)`.
   - A credential that does not resolve to a registered device is rejected here, before any state is touched.
2. **Validate incarnation and sequence.**
   - The registry says the active incarnation is `inc-9`, so this matches.
   - The last accepted sequence was 4,121, and 4,122 is greater, so it is accepted.
   - Worked example of the rejection: a heartbeat arrives with sequence 4,118, delayed in the network. It is less than 4,121, so it is ignored and returns `202`. Accepting it would move last-seen backwards.
3. **Set last-seen from collector receive time.**
   - The collector's own clock, never the device's `sentAt` field.
4. **Atomically replace deadline and latest state.**
   - Version 883 becomes 884, last-seen becomes now, and the deadline becomes now plus 45 seconds. The deadline index entry moves.
   - *Atomically* means no timer can observe a half-updated device.
5. **Emit a transition only on an observable change.**
   - This device was already `UP` and stays `UP`, so nothing is emitted.
   - Worked example of why this filter matters: without it, 66,667 transitions per second flood the outbox with "UP stayed UP", which is the raw-heartbeat volume the capacity section rejected, reintroduced through the back door.
6. **On expiry, compare the version before marking suspect.**
   - The deep dive covers this; here it establishes where in the flow it sits.
7. **Verify through an independent path when available.**
   - "when available" is the honest qualifier. For many NAT-bound devices there is no such path, and the state stays `SUSPECT` rather than advancing to `UNREACHABLE`.
8. **Group alarms by shared cause.**
   - Worked example: 100,000 devices transition within the same 10 seconds and all belong to shard 7. That is one incident about shard 7, not 100,000 device pages.

| Outbox age | How far behind the consumer is. | The measurement that reveals alert latency. |
| Observation age | How long ago the displayed state was actually observed. | Displayed alongside every state so stale is not shown as fresh. |
| Coverage watchdog | An independent checker of collector liveness. | Produces `UNKNOWN` rather than letting stale state look current. |
| Transition filter | Emitting downstream only when the observable state changes. | Keeps the outbox at transition volume rather than heartbeat volume. |

## API and data model — IC3 baseline

The interface table, the state table, the reference SQL, what the SQL deliberately omits, the companion deviations, and two convention blocks.

---

> | Surface | Contract | Idempotency and conflict behavior |
> |---|---|---|
> | `POST /v1/heartbeats` | Device incarnation, sequence, bounded status payload | Duplicate or older sequence returns `202` and is ignored; collector supplies receive time |
> | `GET /v1/devices/{id}/health` | State, observation time, monitoring coverage | Read-only; tenant scope and freshness are explicit |
> | `GET /v1/devices?state=SUSPECT` | Cursor page over a tenant's current index | Read-only; bounded page size, no fleet-wide synchronous scan |
> | `PUT /v1/devices/{id}/maintenance` | Suppression deadline and reason | `If-Match` version; mismatch returns `409`; audited; observations continue |
> | `POST /v1/devices/{id}:enroll` | Authorize a new device incarnation | Idempotency key; old incarnation cannot reactivate itself |
> | `POST /v1/devices/{id}:probe` | Request a bounded verification | `202` with operation id; duplicate request joins the pending probe |

**What this says in plain words**
Six interfaces. One runs 66,667 times per second; the rest are administrative or read-only.

- **Row: `POST /v1/heartbeats`.**
  - Carries the incarnation, the sequence, and a small bounded status payload. *Bounded* means a size limit, so a misbehaving agent cannot send a megabyte.
  - A duplicate or older sequence returns `202` and is ignored. `202` means accepted for processing, which is the honest code: the message was received and it changed nothing.
  - Worked example of why not `400`: a delayed duplicate is not the agent's fault, and returning an error would make agents retry, which is the opposite of what you want. `202` ends the exchange quietly.
  - "collector supplies receive time" means the device's own clock is never used for the deadline.
- **Row: `GET /v1/devices/{id}/health`.**
  - Returns three things together: the state, when it was observed, and whether monitoring currently covers this device.
  - The third field is what stops a stale `UP` being read as a current `UP`.
- **Row: `GET /v1/devices?state=SUSPECT`.**
  - Cursor-paged and explicitly forbidden from doing a fleet-wide synchronous scan.
  - Worked example of the forbidden query: during a large incident, 100,000 devices are suspect. An unbounded query would try to return all of them in one response, consuming memory on the server and timing out on the client, at the exact moment the dashboard matters most.
- **Row: `PUT /v1/devices/{id}/maintenance`.**
  - `If-Match` carries the version the caller was looking at, and a mismatch returns `409` rather than silently overwriting.
  - Worked example: two operators open the same device. One sets maintenance until 18:00, the other until 15:00. Without the version check, whichever saves last wins silently, and a device may come out of maintenance three hours early during a planned upgrade.
  - "observations continue" repeats the earlier rule: maintenance suppresses paging, not recording.
- **Row: `POST /v1/devices/{id}:enroll`.**
  - Registers a new incarnation, protected by an idempotency key so a retried enrollment does not create two.
  - "old incarnation cannot reactivate itself" is the security property: a revoked incarnation cannot enroll a successor for itself, because that would let a compromised device re-establish trust on its own authority.
- **Row: `POST /v1/devices/{id}:probe`.**
  - Returns `202` with an operation identifier rather than holding the connection open while the probe runs.
  - Worked example of the alternative: a synchronous probe with a 30-second timeout holds a server thread for 30 seconds. A hundred concurrent probe requests hold a hundred threads, and the API stops serving anything else.
  - "duplicate request joins the pending probe" is single-flight applied here: ten operators clicking the same button produce one probe and ten subscribers to its result.

---

> | State | Key | Fields and boundary |
> |---|---|---|
> | Registry | `(tenant_id, device_id)` | Expected flag, region, active incarnation, maintenance, version |
> | Latest observation | `(tenant_id, device_id)` | Incarnation, sequence, receive time, deadline, status, owner epoch |
> | Deadline index | `(shard_id, deadline, device_id)` | Stores observation version for stale-task rejection |
> | Transition | `(device_id, transition_version)` | Old/new state, cause, observation time; deduplication identity |
> | Shard ownership | `shard_id` | Owner instance, epoch; only the current epoch may publish transitions |

**What this says in plain words**
Five kinds of state, with a deliberate split between what is configured and what is observed.

- **Row: Registry.** Desired configuration. What *should* be true. Changes rarely, by human or automated action.
- **Row: Latest observation.** Observed evidence. What the system currently believes, derived from heartbeats. Changes constantly.
  - Keeping these two separate is the design's cleanest idea. Worked example of merging them: one row holding both "this device is expected" and "this device was last seen at 14:03" is written 66,667 times per second while also being the configuration record an operator edits. The hot path and the configuration path now contend on the same row.
- **Row: Deadline index.** Keyed by `(shard_id, deadline, device_id)`, which is exactly the order needed to ask "what expires next in this shard". It also stores the observation version, which is what the expiry compare reads.
- **Row: Transition.** Keyed by `(device_id, transition_version)`, which doubles as the deduplication identity.
- **Row: Shard ownership.** Keyed by shard alone, not by tenant, because one shard holds devices belonging to many tenants.

---

> - Reference DDL
>   - Keys and uniqueness match the state table above and the [companion schema](../../1sde/1.backend/2.rest102.md#infra-4).
>   - The live latest observation and the deadline index live in shard-owner memory; SQL holds the registry, incarnations, transitions, a bounded-rate observation checkpoint, and shard ownership.

**What this says in plain words**
The SQL covers only what is durable. The hot state is in memory, and the second bullet says so explicitly rather than leaving the reader to wonder where the 66,667 writes per second went.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE devices (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, device_id text NOT NULL,
>   expected boolean NOT NULL,                                   -- false: silence is not an incident
>   region text NOT NULL,
>   active_incarnation text,                                     -- NULL until first enrollment
>   maintenance_until timestamptz, maintenance_reason text,      -- suppression only; observations continue
>   credential_ref text NOT NULL,                                -- mTLS identity reference, never key material
>   version bigint NOT NULL CHECK (version > 0),                 -- If-Match validator
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, device_id),
>   CHECK ((maintenance_until IS NULL) = (maintenance_reason IS NULL))
> );
> CREATE INDEX devices_by_region ON devices (tenant_id, region, device_id);
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The registry table: one row per device, holding desired configuration.

- **`expected boolean`.** The flag that decides whether silence is an incident.
  - Worked example: a device is being decommissioned. Setting `expected` to false means it can go silent forever without paging anyone, while its history and identity remain.
- **`active_incarnation text` nullable.** Null until the device enrolls for the first time. A registered-but-never-seen device is a real and expected state.
- **`maintenance_until` and `maintenance_reason`.** Both present or both absent, enforced by the final `CHECK`.
  - Worked example of what the constraint prevents: maintenance set with no reason. Six hours later nobody knows why pages are suppressed, whether it is still needed, or who to ask. Requiring the reason at write time is cheap; reconstructing it afterwards is impossible.
- **`credential_ref text`.** A *reference* to the identity, never the key material itself.
  - mTLS means mutual TLS, where both sides present certificates, so the device proves its identity cryptographically rather than with a shared token.
  - The comment "never key material" is a rule: a database holding a million private keys is a single target whose compromise forges the entire fleet.
- **`version bigint CHECK (version > 0)`.** The `If-Match` validator for maintenance edits.
- **`devices_by_region` index on `(tenant_id, region, device_id)`.**
  - Serves "show me this tenant's devices in this region", which is the query alert grouping and regional investigation need.
  - Column order: tenant narrows first, region next, device last for uniqueness and a pagination cursor.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE device_incarnations (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, device_id text NOT NULL, incarnation text NOT NULL,
>   idempotency_key text NOT NULL, enrolled_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   revoked_at timestamptz,                                      -- a revoked incarnation cannot reactivate itself
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, device_id, incarnation),
>   UNIQUE (tenant_id, device_id, idempotency_key),              -- a repeat enroll returns the first result
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, device_id) REFERENCES devices
> );
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
One row per boot session ever enrolled, kept rather than overwritten.

- **Why history is kept rather than only the current incarnation.**
  - Worked example: a device reboots 40 times in an hour. The incarnation history shows a crash loop. If the table held only the current one, that pattern would be invisible.
- **`revoked_at`.** A timestamp rather than deletion, so the record that an incarnation existed and was revoked survives.
  - The comment states the security rule: a revoked incarnation cannot enroll its own successor.
- **`UNIQUE (tenant_id, device_id, idempotency_key)`.** The database enforces enrollment idempotency.
  - Worked example: an enrollment call times out and the agent retries. Without the constraint, two incarnations exist for one boot, and heartbeats for one look like an obsolete incarnation. With it, the second insert conflicts, and the service returns the first result.
  - The uniqueness is enforced by the database, not by application logic, so there is no window between a check and an insert.
- **`FOREIGN KEY` to `devices`.** An incarnation of an unregistered device is impossible.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE device_transitions (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, device_id text NOT NULL, transition_version bigint NOT NULL,
>   old_state text NOT NULL CHECK (old_state IN ('UP','SUSPECT','UNREACHABLE','UNKNOWN')),
>   new_state text NOT NULL CHECK (new_state IN ('UP','SUSPECT','UNREACHABLE','UNKNOWN')),
>   cause text NOT NULL, observed_at timestamptz NOT NULL,       -- collector receive time, never device sentAt
>   owner_epoch bigint NOT NULL CHECK (owner_epoch > 0),         -- only the current epoch may publish
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, device_id, transition_version),      -- deduplication identity
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, device_id) REFERENCES devices,
>   CHECK (old_state <> new_state)
> );
> CREATE INDEX device_transitions_history ON device_transitions (tenant_id, device_id, observed_at, transition_version);
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The durable history: one row per state change.

- **Both states constrained to the same four values.** The database refuses an invented state, so a bug cannot introduce a fifth one that nothing else understands.
- **`CHECK (old_state <> new_state)`.** A transition must actually change something.
  - Worked example of what it catches: a bug that emits a transition on every heartbeat with old and new both `UP`. Without the constraint, the table grows at heartbeat volume, which is the 5.76 billion rows per day the capacity section rejected. The constraint turns that bug into a loud write failure instead of a silent storage explosion.
- **`cause text NOT NULL`.** Every transition records why. Not optional.
- **`observed_at`** with the comment "collector receive time, never device sentAt", repeating the clock rule where it is enforced.
- **`owner_epoch`.** Stored on the transition itself, so you can tell afterwards which owner published it, and so a stale owner's write is identifiable.
- **`PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, device_id, transition_version)`.** The deduplication identity. A redelivered transition conflicts on insert and is recognized.
- **`device_transitions_history` index on `(tenant_id, device_id, observed_at, transition_version)`.**
  - Serves "show me this device's history in time order", the query an investigation runs.
  - Column order: tenant, then device, then time for ordering without a sort, then version for a unique pagination cursor.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE device_observation_checkpoints (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, device_id text NOT NULL,
>   incarnation text NOT NULL, sequence bigint NOT NULL CHECK (sequence >= 0),
>   received_at timestamptz NOT NULL, deadline_at timestamptz NOT NULL, -- wall clock; the live deadline is monotonic in memory
>   state text NOT NULL CHECK (state IN ('UP','SUSPECT','UNREACHABLE','UNKNOWN')),
>   observation_version bigint NOT NULL CHECK (observation_version > 0), -- stale timers compare against this
>   owner_epoch bigint NOT NULL CHECK (owner_epoch > 0),
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, device_id),
>   FOREIGN KEY (tenant_id, device_id) REFERENCES devices,
>   CHECK (deadline_at > received_at)
> );
> CREATE INDEX device_state_index ON device_observation_checkpoints (tenant_id, state, device_id); -- GET /devices?state=
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The periodic durable snapshot of the in-memory observation, written at a bounded rate rather than per heartbeat.

- **`deadline_at` with the comment about clocks.** This is the most subtle line in the schema.
  - The *live* deadline in memory uses a monotonic clock, which only moves forward and is immune to time corrections.
  - The *stored* deadline must be wall-clock, because a monotonic value is meaningless outside the process that produced it.
  - Worked example: a monotonic reading of 4,829,113 means "4,829,113 units since this machine booted". After a restart, or on another machine, it refers to nothing. Recovery therefore needs a wall-clock value, and the deep dive explains that the rebuild must be conservative because wall clocks may disagree between hosts.
- **`observation_version`** with the comment "stale timers compare against this", naming the field's purpose at the point of definition.
- **`CHECK (deadline_at > received_at)`.** A deadline in the past relative to the observation that set it is nonsense, and the database refuses it.
- **`PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, device_id)`.** Exactly one checkpoint per device; it is a snapshot, not a log.
- **`device_state_index` on `(tenant_id, state, device_id)`.**
  - Named in its comment as serving `GET /devices?state=`.
  - Worked example of the column order mattering: with `state` first, finding one tenant's suspect devices would scan every tenant's suspect devices. With `tenant_id` first, the query starts inside that tenant, which is also the isolation property.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE shard_owners (
>   shard_id integer NOT NULL, owner_instance text NOT NULL,
>   epoch bigint NOT NULL CHECK (epoch > 0),                     -- fence; publish only if epoch = expected
>   lease_until timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (shard_id)
> );
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
Who currently owns each shard, under which epoch, and until when.

- **No tenant column**, and the deviation note explains why: a shard holds devices from many tenants.
- **`epoch`** is the fence. Every publish states its epoch, and only the current one is accepted.
- **`lease_until`.** Ownership expires unless renewed, which is what allows recovery from an owner that stopped without announcing it.
  - Worked example of why a lease rather than a flag: an owner crashes without releasing anything. A boolean "owned" flag stays true forever and the shard is never reassigned. A lease simply expires, and a new owner can claim it with a higher epoch.
  - The safety rule, expanded in the deep dive: a new owner must wait for the old lease to expire plus a margin for clock disagreement, or both may briefly believe they own the shard.

---

> - Not captured by the DDL
>   - The `(shard_id, deadline, device_id)` index is an in-memory ordered structure per shard owner; it is rebuilt from `device_observation_checkpoints` with a warm-up `UNKNOWN` interval and never queried per tick.
>   - `device_observation_checkpoints` is written at a bounded checkpoint rate, not per heartbeat; the `66,667 heartbeats/s` hot path stays in memory.
>   - Raw heartbeats are retained briefly in a log keyed by `(tenant, device)`; only transitions reach SQL.

**What this says in plain words**
Three deliberate absences.

1. **The deadline index is in memory.** "never queried per tick" is the key phrase: the tick reads the head of an in-memory structure, it does not run a database query every few seconds. That is precisely the full scan the naive design was rejected for.
2. **Checkpoints are rate-bounded.** Worked example: checkpointing every device every 30 seconds is about 33,000 writes per second, still a lot, so a real implementation checkpoints on state change plus a slow background sweep. The point stated here is that the number is decoupled from the heartbeat rate.
3. **Raw heartbeats live in a log, not SQL.** Briefly, for investigation only.

---

> - Deviation from the companion
>   - Companion `devices.state` moves to `device_observation_checkpoints.state` so the registry row is desired configuration and the projection is observed evidence with its own version and epoch.
>   - `device_incarnations` and `shard_owners` are added for the enrollment idempotency key and the ownership epoch the deep dive depends on; `device_transitions` is the companion's `device_health_events` under this file's name.
>   - `shard_owners` is not tenant-scoped because a shard owns devices of many tenants.

**What this says in plain words**
Three differences from the shared schema file, each with its reason.

1. **State moves out of the registry row.** The separation of desired configuration from observed evidence, stated as the justification rather than left implicit.
2. **Two tables added and one renamed**, because the deep dive depends on mechanisms the companion did not model.
3. **Shard ownership is not tenant-scoped**, because shards cut across tenants.

**Why it matters**
When two documents disagree, name which one wins and why, rather than leaving a future reader to discover the conflict.

---

> - Incarnation handling
>   - A reboot may reset sequence numbers.
>   - Assign or register a new incarnation through an authenticated enrollment flow.
>   - Do not compare random boot UUIDs as if they were ordered epochs.
>   - Reject delayed heartbeats from a revoked prior incarnation.

**What this says in plain words**
Four rules about reboots.

1. **Sequence numbers reset.** The counter lives in the device's memory, so a reboot sets it back to zero or one.
2. **New incarnations come through authenticated enrollment.** Not self-declared.
3. **Do not treat random identifiers as ordered.**
   - Worked example: boot identifier `a3f9` is replaced by `7c21`. Is `7c21` newer? There is no way to tell from the values. If a delayed heartbeat from `a3f9` arrives after `7c21` is active, comparing the values cannot order them. An enrolled incarnation has a recorded enrollment time and an active flag, which does order them.
4. **Reject delayed heartbeats from a revoked incarnation.**
   - Worked example: a device is compromised and its incarnation revoked. Heartbeats from the old credential keep arriving, delayed or replayed. Accepting them would keep a compromised device showing as healthy.

---

> - Conventions
>   - Receive time is the collector's clock, never the device's `sentAt`.
>   - Every read carries observation age and coverage status so a stale `UP` is never silent.
>   - Long-running probes return an operation id and are polled, not held open.

**What this says in plain words**
Three cross-cutting rules, each stated once here so it does not have to be repeated everywhere.

1. **Collector clock only.**
   - Worked example of trusting the device: a device with a clock one hour fast sends `sentAt` an hour in the future. Using it as the deadline base means the device is never marked suspect, whatever happens to it.
2. **Observation age and coverage travel with every read.**
   - The display discipline from the architecture section, expressed as an API contract rather than a dashboard convention, which is what makes it enforceable.
3. **Long operations are polled.**
   - Returning an operation identifier rather than holding a connection open, for the thread-exhaustion reason given in the interface table.

## Deadline algorithm and ownership — IC3 baseline

The deep dive, and the section that produces the level signal. The pseudocode, then the index choice, the timing-wheel follow-up, restart behavior, the clock boundary, and the exact failure if the version check is skipped.

---

> ```text
> HEARTBEAT(device, incarnation, seq), under shard serialization:
>     require current owner epoch before publishing any observation
>     reject if incarnation is not the registry's active incarnation
>     ignore if seq <= latest accepted seq for that incarnation
>     version += 1
>     last_seen = collector_receive_time
>     deadline = last_seen + timeout
>     replace device's deadline-index entry
>     atomically persist observation, deadline identity, and any transition outbox
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The heartbeat path, line by line. The header phrase "under shard serialization" applies to everything below it: these steps happen one at a time for a given shard, so no other operation interleaves.

1. **`require current owner epoch before publishing any observation`.**
   - Check first, before doing anything. If this process is no longer the owner, it must not write at all.
   - Worked example: collector A was partitioned and ownership moved to B at epoch 5. A is still receiving heartbeats and still thinks it owns the shard at epoch 4. This first line stops A from writing, so its stale view never reaches anyone.
2. **`reject if incarnation is not the registry's active incarnation`.**
   - A heartbeat from a superseded or revoked boot session is discarded.
3. **`ignore if seq <= latest accepted seq for that incarnation`.**
   - Note `<=`, not `<`. Equal is also ignored, which makes an exact duplicate harmless.
   - Worked example: the network duplicates a packet, so sequence 4,122 arrives twice. The second is ignored rather than processed again.
4. **`version += 1`.**
   - Every accepted observation increments a counter. This is the value timers capture and later compare against.
5. **`last_seen = collector_receive_time`.**
   - The collector's clock, never the device's.
6. **`deadline = last_seen + timeout`.**
   - 45 seconds from now.
7. **`replace device's deadline-index entry`.**
   - *Replace*, not add. This is the rule that stops the index growing without bound, as flagged in the capacity section.
8. **`atomically persist observation, deadline identity, and any transition outbox`.**
   - All three together or none. Worked example of splitting them: the observation is updated and the process crashes before writing the transition. The device's state says `UP` and no record exists that it changed from `SUSPECT`, so the recovery alert was never sent and nothing shows it was due.

---

> ```text
> EXPIRE(device, observed_version):
>     under the same serialization / conditional update:
>         require current owner epoch
>         if current.version != observed_version: ignore stale timer
>         if current.deadline > now: ignore early timer
>         if collector coverage unhealthy: mark observation UNKNOWN
>         otherwise persist SUSPECT and verification outbox in one conditional transaction
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The expiry path. Note that the timer carries `observed_version`: the version that was current when the timer was created.

1. **`require current owner epoch`.** Same first check as the heartbeat path.
2. **`if current.version != observed_version: ignore stale timer`.**
   - The central check of the whole design.
   - Worked example: a timer was created when the device was at version 883. A heartbeat since then advanced it to 884. The timer's 883 does not match 884, so the timer is discarded. The device stays `UP`.
3. **`if current.deadline > now: ignore early timer`.**
   - A second, independent guard. Even if versions somehow match, the actual deadline is checked against the real clock.
   - Worked example of it earning its place: a timing wheel rounds a deadline up to a bucket boundary and the bucket fires slightly early. The version may still match because no heartbeat arrived, and only this check prevents an expiry 200 milliseconds too soon. Two independent guards protect against two different mistakes.
4. **`if collector coverage unhealthy: mark observation UNKNOWN`.**
   - Before concluding the device is suspect, ask whether *we* are working.
   - Worked example: this collector has been unable to reach the ingress layer for 30 seconds. Every device it owns has gone silent, from its point of view. Marking them all `SUSPECT` would be a mass false alarm. Marking them `UNKNOWN` states the truth: we cannot currently see them.
   - This single line is what converts the epistemic honesty from the requirements section into running code.
5. **`otherwise persist SUSPECT and verification outbox in one conditional transaction`.**
   - The state change and the request to verify are written together, so a device can never be marked suspect with no verification queued.
   - *Conditional* means the write applies only if the version still matches at write time, closing the gap between checking and writing.

---

> - Ordered index baseline
>   - A heap with an indexed update or a sorted set supports `O(log N)` renewal.
>   - Process only deadlines due at the current time.
>   - Keep one live deadline entry per device where practical.

**What this says in plain words**
Three properties the baseline structure needs.

1. **Logarithmic renewal.**
   - "with an indexed update" is essential: a plain heap can find the minimum cheaply but cannot locate an arbitrary item to change its priority without scanning. An indexed heap keeps a map from device to heap position, so a renewal is a direct lookup plus a sift.
2. **Process only what is due.**
   - Read from the head while the head's deadline is in the past, then stop. This is what makes the tick cost proportional to expiries rather than to fleet size, and it is the difference from the rejected full scan.
   - Worked example: 100,000 devices in the shard, of which 3 expire this tick. The tick examines 3 entries.
3. **One live entry per device.**
   - "where practical" hedges because some implementations find lazy deletion simpler, accepting obsolete entries and skipping them when they surface. That is acceptable only if obsolete entries are compacted, or memory grows without bound.

---

> - Timing-wheel follow-up
>   - Round deadlines upward to the next tick, never earlier than the actual deadline.
>   - Check the exact deadline again when a bucket fires.
>   - A `5 s` tick adds at most about `5 s` quantization delay under regular scheduling.
>   - Rescheduling must remove old entries or compact obsolete versions.
>   - Scanning all bucket keys on every tick is not a constant-time wheel.

**What this says in plain words**
A timing wheel is an array of buckets, one per tick, and a deadline is placed in the bucket covering its time. Insertion becomes constant time. Five rules.

1. **Round upward, never downward.**
   - Rounding down would fire early and mark a device suspect before its timeout elapsed. Rounding up only delays.
2. **Re-check the exact deadline when the bucket fires.**
   - The bucket is a coarse approximation, so the precise check still happens, which is the `current.deadline > now` guard in the pseudocode.
3. **A 5-second tick costs at most about 5 seconds of delay.**
   - Worked example of the budget: 45 seconds of silence, plus up to 5 seconds of rounding, is 50 seconds, inside the 60-second publication target with 10 seconds to spare for the outbox.
   - "under regular scheduling" is the defeater: if the tick thread is starved, the delay is unbounded.
4. **Rescheduling must remove old entries or compact them.**
   - Worked example: a device renews every 15 seconds and each renewal adds a bucket entry without removing the old. Over an hour, each device accumulates 240 stale entries. Across 100,000 devices that is 24,000,000 obsolete entries the wheel must skip past.
5. **Scanning all buckets every tick is not a wheel.**
   - The wheel's whole benefit is that a tick touches exactly one bucket. An implementation that iterates every bucket to find due items has the full-scan cost with extra complexity, which is a real and common mistake.

---

> - Collector restart
>   - Restore latest observations from replicated state or a checkpoint.
>   - Rebuild deadlines with an explicit warm-up/unknown interval.
>   - Shared storage alone does not eliminate restart races.
>   - Ownership fencing and freshness checks remain necessary.

**What this says in plain words**
What happens when a collector comes back.

1. **Restore observations from the checkpoint.** The in-memory state is gone; the checkpoint is what it is rebuilt from.
2. **Rebuild deadlines with a warm-up interval.**
   - Worked example of skipping the warm-up: the collector was down for 3 minutes. Every restored deadline is already in the past. On the first tick, all 100,000 devices expire simultaneously and page as suspect, even though most sent heartbeats during the outage that simply were not received.
   - The warm-up says: for the next interval, report `UNKNOWN` and let fresh heartbeats re-establish reality. It is the same honesty rule applied to restart.
3. **Shared storage does not remove restart races.** Putting the state in a shared database does not help, because the question is not where the state lives but who is allowed to act on it.
4. **Fencing and freshness checks remain necessary.** The epoch and the version compare are still required after restart, not replaced by it.

---

> - Clock boundary
>   - Use owner-local monotonic elapsed time for live deadline intervals.
>   - Store wall-clock receive time separately for display and recovery metadata.
>   - Monotonic timestamps cannot be compared across process restarts or hosts.
>   - After failover, reconstruct deadlines conservatively with a stated wall-clock skew allowance.
>   - If that allowance cannot be established, expose `UNKNOWN` until fresh heartbeats restore coverage.

**What this says in plain words**
Five rules about time, and this is the most easily misunderstood part of the design.

1. **Monotonic time for live intervals.**
   - A monotonic clock only moves forward at a steady rate and is unaffected by time synchronization or an administrator changing the date.
   - Worked example of using wall-clock instead: a time daemon steps the clock back 3 seconds. Every deadline is now 3 seconds further away than intended, and detection is silently delayed. Step it forward 30 seconds and every device expires at once.
2. **Wall-clock stored separately.** For display, and as recovery metadata.
3. **Monotonic values do not survive a restart or move between hosts.**
   - They are counts since an arbitrary origin, usually machine boot. Worked example: a monotonic reading of 4,829,113 written to a checkpoint and read back on another machine refers to that machine's boot, not the original's. The number is not merely wrong, it is meaningless.
4. **After failover, rebuild conservatively with a stated skew allowance.**
   - The new owner only has wall-clock values, and its own wall clock may differ from the old owner's.
   - Worked example: the checkpoint says the deadline is 14:00:45 by the old owner's clock. The new owner's clock reads 200 milliseconds ahead. Rebuilding naively could expire the device 200 milliseconds early. *Conservative* means adding the maximum plausible skew, so you always err toward waiting longer rather than expiring sooner.
5. **If skew cannot be bounded, expose `UNKNOWN`.**
   - The honest fallback when you cannot trust the reconstruction: say you do not know, and let fresh heartbeats rebuild the picture.

**Why it matters**
Monotonic versus wall-clock is one of those distinctions that seems pedantic until it causes an incident that is nearly impossible to diagnose, because the symptom is timers behaving oddly with nothing in the logs.

---

> - Exact failure if the version check is skipped
>   - A timer scheduled at version `7` fires after a heartbeat advanced the device to version `8`.
>   - Without the compare, the device is marked `SUSPECT` `45 s` after a heartbeat that arrived seconds ago.
>   - With an old owner still running, the same write can also arrive from a stale epoch
>   - The epoch check rejects it.

**What this says in plain words**
The precise consequence of removing the check, which is what makes the mechanism's necessity concrete rather than asserted.

- Worked example as a step table:

| Time | Event | Version | Device state |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14:00:00 | Heartbeat arrives | 7 | `UP`, deadline 14:00:45 |
| 14:00:40 | Heartbeat arrives | 8 | `UP`, deadline 14:01:25 |
| 14:00:45 | Timer created at version 7 fires | 8 | without the check: `SUSPECT` |

- The device sent a heartbeat 5 seconds ago and is now marked suspect.
- Worked example of the scale: at 1,000,000 devices reporting every 15 seconds, a timer fires for roughly every device every 45 seconds. Any timer whose device reported shortly before it fires hits this race. Across the fleet that is thousands of false suspicions per minute, each one a page or an alert, and the operator's only recourse is to stop trusting the system.
- The last two lines add the second source: an old owner can produce the same wrong write, and the epoch check is what rejects that one. Two mechanisms, two distinct races, and both are needed.

**Why it matters**
"Exact failure if the rule is broken" is a required element in the source's structure, and this is why: a mechanism defended by a concrete failure is remembered and can be defended under questioning. A mechanism presented as a good practice cannot.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Shard serialization | Forcing a shard's operations to happen strictly one at a time. | Makes the compare and the update atomic without distributed locking. |
| Owner epoch | A number increased on each ownership change, checked before any write. | Stops a superseded owner writing. |
| Observation version | A counter increased on each accepted heartbeat, captured by timers. | The stale-timer check compares against it. |
| Stale timer | A timer created under an older version of a device's state. | The central race; discarded by the version compare. |
| Early timer | A timer firing before the actual deadline, usually from tick rounding. | Caught by the second, independent deadline check. |
| Indexed heap | A heap plus a map from item to position, allowing one item's priority to be updated in log time. | Required for renewals; a plain heap cannot do it. |
| Lazy deletion | Leaving obsolete entries in the index and skipping them when they surface. | Simpler, but requires compaction or memory grows without bound. |
| Timing wheel | An array of time buckets giving constant-time insertion. | The 10× follow-up, with five rules to get right. |
| Quantization delay | Extra delay from rounding a deadline up to a tick boundary. | About 5 seconds, and it must fit inside the publication budget. |
| Warm-up interval | A period after restart during which state is reported `UNKNOWN` rather than guessed. | Prevents a restart causing mass false expiry. |

| Fencing | Rejecting an action from a participant that has been superseded. | The general name for what epochs and versions do. |

## Verification and alerting — IC3 baseline

The second deep dive. Four parts: what pull verification can honestly achieve, its budget, the alerting rules, and the grouping rule.

---

> - Pull verification
>   - Use only a permitted reachable path.
>   - NAT may require an existing outbound control channel or a local gateway.
>   - Reusing the same failed network path adds little independent evidence.

**What this says in plain words**
Three constraints on probing a silent device.

1. **Only a permitted path.**
   - *Permitted* is a security word, not a networking one. Probing a customer's device means reaching into their network, which must be something they have agreed to and that is authorized per device.
   - Worked example of the alternative: a monitoring system that probes arbitrary addresses inside customer networks is a scanning tool. It will trip intrusion detection and it is a genuine trust problem.
2. **NAT requires an existing channel or a local gateway.**
   - Two concrete mechanisms:
     - An outbound control channel the device already opened and keeps open, over which the server can send a request. The device dialed out, so NAT permits the return traffic.
     - A gateway at the customer's site that is reachable and can see the device on the local network.
   - Worked example of neither existing: a device behind carrier-grade NAT with no persistent channel and no local gateway. There is no probe. The state stays `SUSPECT` and never becomes `UNREACHABLE`, and the design must be honest that verification coverage is partial.
3. **The same path adds little.**
   - Worked example: heartbeats stopped because the site's internet link is down. A probe over that link fails. You have confirmed the link is down, which you already suspected, and learned nothing about the device.
   - The general principle: evidence is only worth collecting if it could have come out differently for a reason you care about.

---

> - Verification budget
>   - If `1%` of the fleet is suspect at once, that is `10,000` probes.
>   - At an assumed verifier concurrency of `500` and `1 s` per probe, the cohort drains in about `20 s`.
>   - A regional outage can make `10%` suspect
>   - The verifier must shed load and let grouping speak instead.

**What this says in plain words**
The arithmetic that shows verification does not scale to an incident.

1. **Normal case: 1% of 1,000,000 is 10,000 probes.**
2. **Drain time: 10,000 ÷ 500 concurrent × 1 second each = 20 seconds.**
   - That fits inside the alerting budget, so verification is useful in the normal case.
3. **Incident case: 10% is 100,000 probes.**
   - Worked example of the same arithmetic: 100,000 ÷ 500 = 200 seconds, which is more than three minutes. The 120-second grouped-alert target is already missed, and the newest probes are answering questions about a situation three minutes stale.
   - And it is worse than the arithmetic suggests: a regional outage is exactly when probes are least likely to succeed, so you spend the entire budget confirming failures over a broken path.
4. **So shed load and let grouping speak.**
   - When 100,000 devices go silent together, the useful output is not 100,000 individual verdicts. It is one sentence: "a regional network event affected 100,000 devices in region X".
   - The verifier's job is to add information at the margin, and during a correlated incident there is no margin. Grouping already tells the whole story.

**Why it matters**
This is a mechanism that works in the normal case and must deliberately stand down in the abnormal one. Saying that explicitly is the signal; a design that tries to verify every device during an outage spends its capacity making the outage worse.

---

> - Alerting
>   - Suppress duplicate device pages when monitoring coverage itself fails.
>   - Preserve the affected-device list for diagnosis.
>   - Maintenance suppresses paging, not health recording.
>   - A monitoring uncertainty signal must not trigger destructive automated failover by itself.

**What this says in plain words**
Four rules.

1. **Suppress per-device pages when coverage fails.**
   - Worked example: a collector dies. Without this rule, 100,000 pages fire. The paging system itself may fall over, and the one useful signal, "a collector died", is buried under 100,000 identical device alerts.
2. **Preserve the affected-device list.**
   - Suppressing the *pages* must not discard the *data*. The operator needs the list to check afterwards which devices really did not recover.
   - The distinction: suppress notification, retain information.
3. **Maintenance suppresses paging, not recording.**
   - Repeated from requirements because it is enforced here.
   - Worked example: a device is under maintenance from 02:00 to 04:00 and genuinely fails at 02:30. Nobody is paged, correctly. But the transition is recorded, so at 04:00 the operator can see the device failed during the window rather than discovering it hours later.
4. **Monitoring uncertainty must not trigger destructive automation.**
   - The strongest line in the section. `UNKNOWN` means "I cannot see", and acting destructively on "I cannot see" is how a monitoring failure becomes a data-loss incident.
   - Worked example: an automated system replaces any device that is not healthy. A collector dies, 100,000 devices become `UNKNOWN`, and the automation begins replacing 100,000 perfectly healthy devices. The monitoring outage has become a fleet-wide destructive event.
   - "by itself" is the precise qualifier: uncertainty may contribute to a decision made with other evidence, but it may not be sufficient on its own.

---

> - Grouping rule
>   - Correlate transitions within a short window by collector shard, site, and region.
>   - Above a threshold share of a group, emit one incident with a device count instead of per-device pages.
>   - Keep per-device transitions in history so the incident can be expanded.

**What this says in plain words**
The concrete algorithm for turning many alerts into one.

1. **Correlate by three dimensions within a short window.**
   - Collector shard catches monitoring failures. Site catches a local network or power problem. Region catches a wide network event.
   - Worked example of the dimensions distinguishing causes: 100,000 devices transition, and they all share collector shard 7 but are spread across 400 sites and 3 regions. The common factor is the shard, so this is a monitoring failure. If instead they shared a site, it is that site's link or power.
2. **Above a threshold share, emit one incident with a count.**
   - *Share*, not absolute count, which matters. Worked example: 50 devices failing out of 60 at a small site is 83% and is clearly a site-wide event. 50 out of 100,000 in a region is 0.05% and is 50 independent device failures. The same absolute number means entirely different things.
3. **Keep per-device transitions in history.**
   - The incident is a summary for the pager. The underlying rows remain, so the operator can expand it and ask which specific devices did not come back.
   - This is the same pattern as rule 2 above: reduce notification volume, never reduce recorded information.

**Why it matters**
Alert grouping is where monitoring systems most often fail in practice. The failure mode is well known: an incident produces thousands of pages, humans start ignoring the channel, and the next real alert is missed. A design that produces one incident with a device count is operable; one that produces 100,000 pages is not, regardless of how correct each individual page is.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Pull verification | Actively probing a silent device instead of waiting for it to report. | The secondary evidence path, applied only to the suspect cohort. |
| Permitted path | A route to the device the customer has authorized. | Probing without authorization makes the monitor a scanning tool. |
| Outbound control channel | A long-lived connection the device opened, over which the server can send requests. | One of two ways to reach a device behind NAT. |
| Local gateway | A reachable component at the customer site that can observe the device locally. | The other way, and it gives genuinely independent evidence. |
| Independent evidence | An observation that could fail or succeed for reasons unrelated to the original signal. | A same-path probe is not independent and adds nothing. |
| Verifier concurrency | How many probes may be outstanding at once. | 500 here; with probe duration it fixes the drain rate. |
| Suspect cohort | The set of devices currently silent past the threshold. | The population verification is applied to. |
| Load shedding | Deliberately dropping work when demand exceeds what is useful. | The verifier's required behavior during a correlated outage. |

| Alert grouping | Collapsing many related transitions into one incident with a count. | Keeps the paging channel usable during an incident. |

## Failure, operations, and security

A nine-row failure table, then what to observe, retry discipline, tenant isolation, and rollout safety.

---

> | Failure | Blast radius | Detection | Degraded behavior | Recovery |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Single heartbeat lost | One device | Next sequence arrival | No immediate page | Refresh deadline on a newer heartbeat |
> | Device unreachable | Device or access network | Silence and independent observation | `SUSPECT` or `UNREACHABLE`, not proven dead | Recover on fresh authenticated evidence |
> | Collector shard unavailable | Its assigned fleet | Independent coverage watchdog | Mark observation `UNKNOWN`; group monitoring alarm | Restore ownership and rebuild deadlines |
> | Region network partition | Regional fleet | Correlated missing reports | One regional incident plus affected-device count | Reconcile latest state without replaying stale alarms |
> | Old timer races with heartbeat | One device | Version comparison | Ignore stale expiry | Keep the new deadline |
> | Device reboot resets sequence | One device | New enrollment/incarnation | Ignore obsolete incarnation messages | Register fresh incarnation securely |
> | Verifier queue overloaded | Suspect cohort | Queue age | Delay secondary evidence with visible age | Rate-limit verification and prioritize critical devices |
> | Registry or metadata store unavailable | Enrollment, maintenance, assignment changes | Control API errors | Collectors keep processing heartbeats on cached assignments (static stability) | Restore store; reconcile assignment version |
> | Transition outbox backlog | Alert and history latency | Outbox age | Dashboards show state with a lag flag | Drain; deduplicate by `transition_version` |

**What this says in plain words**
Nine failures, ordered roughly from smallest blast radius to largest, with how you notice and what the system does meanwhile.

- **Row: Single heartbeat lost.**
  - Radius: one device, and nothing visible happens.
  - Detection is simply the next heartbeat arriving with a higher sequence.
  - This row exists to make a point: the system is designed so the most common failure is a non-event. The 45-second threshold at a 15-second cadence tolerates two consecutive losses.
- **Row: Device unreachable.**
  - The degraded behavior column is the honest one: `SUSPECT` or `UNREACHABLE`, explicitly "not proven dead".
  - Recovery is "fresh authenticated evidence", meaning the device returns to `UP` only on a valid heartbeat, never on an assumption or a timeout.
- **Row: Collector shard unavailable.**
  - Radius: its entire assigned fleet, 100,000 devices.
  - Detection is the independent watchdog, and independence is the whole point: a dead collector cannot report itself dead.
  - Behavior: `UNKNOWN` plus a grouped monitoring alarm, not 100,000 device pages.
  - Recovery includes rebuilding deadlines, which carries the warm-up interval from the deep dive.
- **Row: Region network partition.**
  - Detection is correlated missing reports, which is the grouping rule doing its job.
  - Recovery says "without replaying stale alarms". Worked example: the partition heals and 100,000 devices report at once. Every one of them generates an `UNKNOWN` to `UP` transition. Replaying all of those as alerts would page the operator 100,000 times about a recovery, which is as useless as paging about the failure.
- **Row: Old timer races with heartbeat.**
  - Radius: one device. Detection: the version comparison. This is the deep dive's central mechanism appearing as a routine, handled event rather than an incident.
- **Row: Device reboot resets sequence.**
  - Handled by incarnations. The recovery says "securely", pointing back at the rule that a revoked incarnation cannot enroll its own successor.
- **Row: Verifier queue overloaded.**
  - Detection is queue age, not queue length. Worked example of why: a queue of 10,000 draining at 5,000 per second is 2 seconds old and fine. A queue of 500 draining at 5 per second is 100 seconds old and useless. Length alone cannot distinguish them.
  - "visible age" repeats the discipline: a delayed answer is presented with its delay.
- **Row: Registry or metadata store unavailable.**
  - The row that names static stability explicitly.
  - Radius is only control operations. Worked example: one hour of registry downtime, during which 240,000,000 heartbeats are processed normally, and what stops is enrolling and editing maintenance.
- **Row: Transition outbox backlog.**
  - Behavior: dashboards show the state *with a lag flag*, rather than pretending it is current.
  - Recovery deduplicates by `transition_version`, which is why that key exists.

---

> - Observe
>   - Heartbeat age percentiles.
>   - Registered versus currently observed devices.
>   - Collector coverage by shard.
>   - Expiry work backlog.
>   - Verification latency and independent success rate.
>   - Correlated state transitions by network and region.
>   - Outbox age and transition duplicates rejected.

**What this says in plain words**
Seven measurements, each answering a question nothing else answers.

1. **Heartbeat age percentiles.**
   - The distribution of how long ago each device was heard from.
   - Worked example of its value: the p99 heartbeat age climbing from 16 seconds to 40 seconds means the fleet is drifting toward the 45-second threshold. Nothing has failed yet, and every device is about to be marked suspect. This is the leading indicator for a mass false alarm.
2. **Registered versus currently observed.**
   - Worked example: 1,000,000 registered, 940,000 observed. The 60,000 gap is the number of devices not currently reporting, which is the single clearest health number for the fleet.
3. **Collector coverage by shard.**
   - Per shard, not aggregated, for the same reason as everywhere else: an aggregate hides one shard at zero.
4. **Expiry work backlog.**
   - If the tick cannot keep up, detection latency grows silently. Worked example: the tick should complete in milliseconds but is taking 8 seconds because of an unbounded index. Expiries are late and the publication target is being missed with nothing else showing it.
5. **Verification latency and independent success rate.**
   - The second half is the subtle one: what fraction of probes actually used an independent path. Worked example: if that rate is near zero, verification is producing no information at all and the `UNREACHABLE` state is meaningless.
6. **Correlated transitions by network and region.**
   - The input to grouping, and a signal in its own right.
7. **Outbox age and duplicates rejected.**
   - Age reveals alert lag. A rising duplicate-rejection count reveals a consumer that is failing and retrying, which is otherwise invisible because deduplication makes it harmless.

---

> - Retry discipline
>   - Agents retry with exponential backoff and jitter
>   - A failed heartbeat is not retried past the next scheduled one.
>   - After an outage, reconnects spread over a window so ingress does not see the whole fleet at once.
>   - Ingress returns `429` with a retry hint when a shard is over budget
>   - Agents honor it.
>   - Verifier probes retry at most once
>   - A second failure is evidence, not a retry trigger.

**What this says in plain words**
Four rules, and the last one is the interesting one.

1. **Exponential backoff with jitter.**
   - Backoff lengthens the wait after each failure. Jitter randomizes it so clients do not synchronize.
   - Worked example without jitter: a million devices fail at the same instant and all retry after exactly 1 second, then 2, then 4. Every retry wave is a synchronized million-device thundering herd.
2. **A failed heartbeat is not retried past the next scheduled one.**
   - Worked example of why: a heartbeat fails at 14:00:00 and the next is due at 14:00:15. Retrying the old one at 14:00:20 delivers stale information that a newer heartbeat has already superseded, and it costs ingress capacity during an outage.
   - The general rule for periodic data: do not retry a sample when a fresher one is coming.
3. **Spread reconnects over a window.**
   - The capacity control behind the 2× headroom requirement.
4. **`429` with a retry hint, honored by agents.**
   - `429` means too many requests. The server pushes back rather than falling over, and the agents must actually respect it.
5. **Verifier probes retry at most once, and a second failure is evidence.**
   - This reframes what a retry *is*. In most systems a retry is an attempt to overcome a transient problem. Here the probe's purpose is to gather evidence, so a second failure is not a reason to try a third time, it is the answer.
   - Worked example of getting it wrong: probing a silent device 10 times. You have spent 10 units of a scarce budget to learn what the second attempt already told you, during an incident when that budget is most contended.

---

> - Tenant isolation
>   - Device identity and every read are scoped by tenant
>   - Another tenant's device id returns `404`, not `403`.
>   - Per-tenant heartbeat rate quotas stop one tenant's misconfigured fleet from starving a shard.
>   - Alert groups never span tenants even when the underlying network incident does.
>   - Device credentials are per incarnation and revoked at enrollment of the next one.

**What this says in plain words**
Four isolation rules, and the first carries a security subtlety worth dwelling on.

1. **`404`, not `403`, for another tenant's device.**
   - `403` means forbidden, which confirms the resource exists. `404` means not found, which reveals nothing.
   - Worked example of the leak: an attacker enumerates device identifiers. `403` responses tell them which identifiers are real devices belonging to someone else, so they learn the shape and size of another tenant's fleet without ever accessing it. `404` for everything they cannot see removes that channel.
2. **Per-tenant heartbeat quotas.**
   - Worked example: a tenant deploys firmware that reports every 100 milliseconds instead of every 15 seconds, a 150-fold increase. Without a per-tenant quota, that tenant's 50,000 devices generate 500,000 heartbeats per second and saturate every shard they touch, degrading every other tenant.
3. **Alert groups never span tenants.**
   - Worked example: one regional network failure affects devices from three tenants. A single incident naming all three would tell each tenant who else was affected, which is a disclosure. Three separate incidents, one per tenant, describe the same event without leaking.
4. **Credentials are per incarnation, revoked at the next enrollment.**
   - Worked example: a device is stolen. The replacement enrolls a new incarnation, which automatically revokes the old credential. The stolen device's heartbeats are rejected from that moment. Credential lifetime is tied to incarnation lifetime rather than managed separately, so there is no way to forget the revocation step.

---

> - Rollout and rollback safety
>   - Update a small agent cohort.
>   - Jitter both steady heartbeats and reconnect backoff.
>   - Compare gaps and payload rejection before fleet rollout.
>   - Keep the old payload version readable during migration.
>   - Collector deploys hand off shard ownership with a new epoch and a declared warm-up interval
>   - Rollback follows the same handoff.

**What this says in plain words**
Six rules, split between agent rollout and collector rollout.

1. **Update a small agent cohort first.**
   - Worked example: 1,000 devices out of 1,000,000 is 0.1%, enough to see behavior and small enough that a broken agent is not a fleet outage.
   - This matters more than usual here: a bad server deploy is rolled back in minutes, but a bad agent on a field device may be very hard to recover, because the broken agent is also the thing you would use to fix it.
2. **Jitter both the steady schedule and the reconnect backoff.**
   - Two different places, both needed. Steady jitter smooths normal load; reconnect jitter smooths recovery.
3. **Compare gaps and payload rejection before fleet rollout.**
   - Gaps means the distribution of intervals between heartbeats. Worked example: the new agent's median interval is 15 seconds but its p99 is 44 seconds, one second under the threshold. At fleet scale that produces a steady stream of false suspicions, and it is only visible by comparing the distribution rather than the average.
4. **Keep the old payload version readable during migration.**
   - Old and new agents coexist for as long as the rollout takes, which for field devices can be weeks.
   - Worked example of forgetting: the collector is updated to require a new field. Every un-upgraded device's heartbeat is rejected, and the entire un-upgraded fleet goes silent at once.
5. **Collector deploys hand off ownership with a new epoch and a declared warm-up.**
   - A deploy is a deliberate ownership change, using exactly the same mechanism as a failure-driven one. That is the right design: the planned path and the emergency path are the same code, so the emergency path is exercised on every deploy.
6. **Rollback follows the same handoff.**
   - No special reverse procedure, which means the rollback path is also exercised routinely rather than being an untested script.

| Per-tenant quota | A cap on one tenant's share of a shared resource. | Contains a tenant's misconfigured fleet. |
| Credential revocation | Invalidating an identity so it can no longer authenticate. | Tied to incarnation enrollment so it cannot be forgotten. |
| Payload version compatibility | Accepting both old and new message formats during a migration. | Field-device rollouts take weeks, so both coexist. |
| Heartbeat gap distribution | The spread of intervals between consecutive heartbeats. | A bad p99 causes false suspicions that an average hides. |
| Ownership handoff | Deliberately moving shard ownership with a new epoch. | Makes the planned path and the failure path the same code. |

## Alternatives and IC4 stretch

The option table, five stretch items, the weakest assumption, and the over-engineering warning.

---

> | Option | What it is | Strength | Weakness | Choose when |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Push heartbeat | Device-initiated report | NAT-friendly and naturally distributed scheduling | Agent may be alive while application is unhealthy | Primary liveness channel |
> | Central pull | Scheduled remote probe | Observer controls checks | Reachability and probe storms | Small or directly reachable fleets |
> | Push plus selective verification | Heartbeat with secondary observation | Better evidence on suspicious silence | Same-path verification remains correlated | Baseline when a second path exists |
> | Fixed timeout | Deadline from last observation | Easy to explain and operate | False suspicions under variable delay | Stable heartbeat cadence |
> | Adaptive suspicion | Threshold from latency distribution | Threshold from measured behavior | More tuning and less predictable thresholds | Measured jitter makes fixed thresholds noisy |

**What this says in plain words**
Five options across two decisions: how evidence arrives, and how the timeout is chosen.

- **Row: Push heartbeat.**
  - The weakness is worth dwelling on: the agent may be alive while the application is broken.
  - Worked example: the monitoring agent is a separate process that reports happily every 15 seconds while the application it shares a machine with has been crash-looping for an hour. Every dashboard shows `UP`. The heartbeat proves the agent runs, and nothing more, which is exactly what the requirements section said health means.
  - Mitigating it means the agent checks something real about the application before reporting, which turns the heartbeat into a health check rather than a liveness ping.
- **Row: Central pull.**
  - Strength: the observer controls timing and knows exactly when it last checked.
  - Weaknesses: reachability, which NAT kills, and probe storms, where a scheduler initiating 66,667 probes per second is itself a load problem.
  - Right for small or directly reachable fleets, which is the naive design's proper home.
- **Row: Push plus selective verification.**
  - The chosen baseline. Better evidence where a second path exists, with the honest caveat that same-path verification stays correlated.
- **Row: Fixed timeout.**
  - Strength is operational rather than technical: everyone understands "45 seconds of silence", it can be explained to a customer, and its behavior is predictable.
  - Weakness: variable delay produces false suspicions.
- **Row: Adaptive suspicion.**
  - The threshold is derived from each device's measured interarrival distribution rather than set globally.
  - Worked example: a satellite-connected device normally has interarrival times between 20 and 90 seconds. A global 45-second threshold marks it suspect several times an hour, every hour, forever. An adaptive threshold learns its distribution and sets the bound where silence is actually unusual for that device.
  - The weakness is real: thresholds vary per device, are harder to explain, and a device that degrades slowly can teach the detector to accept its degradation.

---

> - IC4 stretch — partition by operational cell
>   - Each cell owns collectors, hot state, and alarm grouping.
>   - Global dashboards read aggregated cell summaries.
>   - One overloaded cell must not delay every region's deadline processing.

**What this says in plain words**
A cell is an independent, complete copy of the system serving a subset of the fleet, sharing nothing with other cells.

- Worked example: 10 cells of 100,000 devices, each with its own collectors, its own deadline processing, and its own alert grouping. A cell that falls behind affects its 100,000 devices and no others.
- Global dashboards read per-cell summaries rather than querying every cell's raw state, so the dashboard does not become the shared component that couples the cells back together.
- The third line names the property being bought: the failure of one cell does not delay deadline processing anywhere else. Without cells, a single overloaded component delays detection for the entire fleet.

---

> - IC4 stretch — separate detection from remediation
>   - Remediation checks authoritative ownership and fencing before replacing a worker.
>   - A false positive must not create two active owners of external state.

**What this says in plain words**
If remediation is ever added, it must do its own authoritative check rather than trusting the detector.

- Worked example of the danger: a device is marked `SUSPECT` because of a network blip. An automated system starts a replacement. The original was never down and is still writing to a shared database. Two workers now believe they own the same job.
- Detection is inherently uncertain, as the requirements section established. Remediation is destructive. Wiring an uncertain signal directly into a destructive action means every false positive becomes a correctness incident.
- The correct structure is that remediation acquires an authoritative, fenced ownership claim, which the old worker will fail to renew if it really is gone, and which blocks it if it is not.

---

> - IC4 stretch — adapt detection targets by device class
>   - A `60 s` target cannot coexist with devices sleeping offline for hours without an explicit expected schedule.
>   - Battery and bandwidth constraints may reverse the always-on-agent premise.

**What this says in plain words**
Not every device can or should report every 15 seconds.

- Worked example: a battery-powered sensor wakes once an hour, reports, and sleeps. Under a 45-second threshold it is `SUSPECT` for 59 minutes out of every 60. The detector is not wrong; the threshold does not describe this device.
- The fix is an expected schedule per device class: this class reports hourly, so silence is only meaningful after, say, 90 minutes.
- The second line is the deeper point: for a battery device, a 15-second heartbeat may be the dominant power cost, so monitoring cadence becomes a product decision about battery life rather than a monitoring decision at all.

---

> - IC4 stretch — 10× load
>   - At `10 million` devices the same interval produces approximately `666,667 heartbeats/s`.
>   - Expand cells and deadline ownership.
>   - Move the deadline index to a timing wheel so renewal is constant time per shard.
>   - Keep global alert aggregation bounded by incidents rather than raw device count.

**What this says in plain words**
Three changes at ten times the fleet.

1. **The rate.** 10,000,000 ÷ 15 ≈ 666,667 heartbeats per second.
2. **Expand cells and ownership.** More cells, more shards. This part is arithmetic.
3. **Move to a timing wheel.**
   - Worked example of why now and not before: at 100,000 devices per shard, renewals cost about 113,000 comparisons per second, which is nothing. At 1,000,000 per shard, log₂ rises to about 20 and the renewal rate rises tenfold, so the cost goes up roughly twelvefold. It is still not catastrophic, but it is now a real fraction of a core, and constant-time insertion begins to pay for its complexity.
   - This is the payoff of having done the arithmetic earlier: the wheel arrives when a number says so.
4. **Bound global aggregation by incidents, not devices.**
   - Worked example: a regional failure affects 1,000,000 devices. The global dashboard must show one incident with a count of 1,000,000, not attempt to render 1,000,000 rows. Aggregation cost must scale with the number of incidents, which stays small, rather than with the number of affected devices, which does not.

---

> - IC4 stretch — cost and blast radius
>   - Collector cost is proportional to fleet size
>   - History retention is the dominant storage cost, so raw heartbeat retention is a cost control.
>   - A cell failure strands one cell's fleet in `UNKNOWN`, not the whole region.
>   - Reconnect storms after an outage are the largest transient cost
>   - Jittered reconnect is a capacity control, not a nicety.

**What this says in plain words**
Four cost observations.

1. **Collector cost scales with the fleet.** Unavoidable; every device's heartbeat must be received by something.
2. **History retention dominates storage.**
   - Worked example: raw heartbeats at 576 gigabytes per day cost far more than transitions at a few megabytes per day. Retention length is therefore the single largest storage lever, which is why the retention window is measured in hours and stated as a decision.
3. **A cell failure is contained.** One cell's fleet in `UNKNOWN` rather than a region-wide blind spot.
4. **Reconnect storms are the largest transient cost, and jitter is a capacity control.**
   - The last four words are the point. Jitter looks like a small implementation detail, and it is actually what determines the peak the ingress fleet must be sized for.
   - Worked example: without reconnect jitter, a healed partition produces a peak perhaps ten times steady state, so the fleet must be sized for 666,667 heartbeats per second to survive a recovery. With jitter spreading reconnects over 60 seconds, the peak is a small multiple of steady state. Jitter is directly a hardware budget decision.

---

> - Weakest assumption
>   - Heartbeat delivery latency stays well under the `45 s` timeout, so silence reflects the device rather than network jitter.
>   - Mitigation: measure interarrival distribution per device class and site
>     - Move noisy classes to adaptive thresholds before lowering the fixed timeout.

**What this says in plain words**
The whole design assumes that when a device is healthy, its heartbeats arrive comfortably within 45 seconds.

- Worked example of the assumption failing: a site on a congested link sees delivery delays of 30 to 50 seconds during business hours. Its devices are marked suspect every afternoon and recover every evening. Nothing is wrong with any device; the threshold does not fit the path.
- The mitigation has an explicit order: measure first, then move noisy classes to adaptive thresholds, and only then consider changing the fixed timeout.
- The ordering is the signal. The instinctive fix is to raise the timeout, which slows detection for the entire fleet to accommodate a few bad paths. Measuring first localizes the problem to the classes that have it.

---

> - Over-engineering warning
>   - Do not claim a failure detector can distinguish a crash from an arbitrary partition with certainty.
>   - Do not build a timing wheel or per-device leases before the heap and version compare are shown to fall short.

**What this says in plain words**
Two warnings, of different kinds.

1. **Do not claim certainty.**
   - This is not an engineering-effort warning, it is a correctness one. In an asynchronous network the distinction cannot be made, because both cases present as a message that has not arrived.
   - Worked example of the claim being made accidentally: describing a state as `DEAD`, or saying "after two failed probes we know it is down". Both assert knowledge the system cannot have, and an interviewer who knows the theory will stop you there.
2. **Do not build the advanced structures prematurely.**
   - The heap costs 0.1% of a core, and the version compare is a few lines. Both are demonstrably sufficient at the stated scale.
   - Worked example of the cost of ignoring this: a candidate who opens the deep dive with per-device leases and a hierarchical timing wheel has spent the highest-value minutes of the interview on machinery the numbers say is unnecessary, and has demonstrated that they do not use their own estimates.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Liveness ping | Evidence that a process is running, with no claim about whether it is working correctly. | What a heartbeat actually proves. |
| Health check | A report that depends on the application actually functioning, not just the agent running. | What a heartbeat must become to close the agent-alive-app-dead gap. |
| Probe storm | The load created by a scheduler initiating probes for a whole fleet. | The central-pull weakness at scale. |
| Interarrival distribution | The spread of time gaps between consecutive heartbeats from one device. | What an adaptive threshold is derived from, and what the weakest assumption is about. |
| Adaptive suspicion | Setting the timeout per device from its measured behavior rather than globally. | Handles device classes a fixed threshold cannot fit. |
| Operational cell | An independent, complete copy of the system serving a subset of the fleet. | Contains a failure to one subset. |
| Cell summary | An aggregate a global dashboard reads instead of querying raw per-cell state. | Stops the dashboard recoupling the cells. |
| Fenced remediation | Replacement that first takes an authoritative, exclusive ownership claim. | Stops a false positive creating two active owners. |
| Expected schedule | A per-class statement of how often a device is supposed to report. | Lets sleeping devices coexist with a fast detection target. |
| Timing wheel | An array of time buckets giving constant-time scheduling. | Justified at 10× load, not before. |
| Incident-bounded aggregation | Aggregation whose cost scales with incidents rather than affected devices. | Keeps a global dashboard usable during a million-device event. |
| Retention window | How long data is kept before deletion. | The dominant storage lever, so raw retention is a cost control. |
| Reconnect storm | The surge when many devices reconnect after an outage heals. | The largest transient load, and what sizes the ingress fleet. |
| Capacity control | A mechanism whose real purpose is to bound peak load. | What jitter is, despite looking like a detail. |
| Asynchronous network | A network with no bound on message delay. | Why crash and partition cannot be distinguished with certainty. |

## Level signal — IC3 baseline vs IC4 stretch

One row per interview phase: what must land, what raises it, and the observable that separates the two.

---

> | Phase | IC3 must land | IC4 adds | Separating signal |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Requirements | Registry-defined expectations, NAT constraint, `45 s` suspect target | Names remediation and metrics as non-goals; device-class schedules | Separates suspicion from certainty before drawing anything |
> | Estimates | Ingest rate, hot state, history row count | Deadline-work arithmetic that keeps the heap and rejects per-heartbeat DB writes | A number decides the hot-path storage |
> | Architecture | Shards by device id, one owner, watchdog separate from collectors | Control plane versus data plane; cached assignments survive registry outage | States static stability without being asked |
> | Deep dive | Version-checked expiry, incarnation plus sequence ordering | Owner epochs, monotonic versus wall-clock boundary, conservative rebuild after failover | Reasons about the same race across restarts and hosts |
> | Operations | Failure table, coverage watchdog, grouped alerts | Reconnect-storm control, outbox lag, cell blast radius | Volunteers monitoring-of-the-monitor |
> | Wrap | Recap and push-versus-pull tradeoff | Names delivery-latency assumption and adaptive thresholds first | Identifies the flaw before the interviewer does |

**What this says in plain words**
A self-assessment grid. The middle column is a checklist; the fourth column is what actually distinguishes a stronger answer.

**Why the fourth column is the one to score**
The difference is rarely more content. It is a different kind of claim about the same content.

- **Row: Requirements.**
  - Must land: the registry defines what is expected, NAT rules out probing, and suspicion begins at 45 seconds.
  - Adds: naming remediation and metrics as non-goals, and recognizing that device classes may need different schedules.
  - Separating signal: separating suspicion from certainty *before drawing anything*.
  - Worked example of the difference: "I will detect when a device is down" versus "I can detect silence; silence has at least three causes and I will design a vocabulary that says which one I can actually distinguish". The second frames every later decision.
- **Row: Estimates.**
  - Must land: 66,667 heartbeats per second, 128 megabytes of hot state, 5.76 billion rows per day if you stored everything.
  - Adds: the deadline-work arithmetic, which does two jobs at once, keeping the heap and rejecting per-heartbeat database writes.
  - Separating signal: "a number decides the hot-path storage".
  - Worked example of failing it: reciting "66,667 heartbeats per second" and then drawing a database. The number was stated and ignored. Passing it: "66,667 writes per second exceeds what one table sustains by an order of magnitude, so the hot path is memory and the database is a checkpoint".
- **Row: Architecture.**
  - Must land: sharding by device identity, one owner per shard, and a watchdog outside the collectors.
  - Adds: the control-plane and data-plane split, with cached assignments surviving a registry outage.
  - Separating signal: stating static stability without being asked.
- **Row: Deep dive.**
  - Must land: version-checked expiry, and ordering by incarnation plus sequence.
  - Adds: owner epochs, the monotonic versus wall-clock boundary, and conservative rebuilding after failover.
  - Separating signal: reasoning about the same race across restarts and hosts.
  - Worked example: the version compare handles a stale timer inside one process. The epoch handles a stale owner across processes. The skew allowance handles reconstruction across machines. Recognizing these as one problem at three scales, rather than three unrelated mechanisms, is the signal.
- **Row: Operations.**
  - Must land: the failure table, the coverage watchdog, and grouped alerts.
  - Adds: reconnect-storm control, outbox lag, and cell blast radius.
  - Separating signal: volunteering monitoring-of-the-monitor.
  - Worked example of why it reads as experience: anyone who has been on call has asked "is the service down or is the dashboard broken?" A candidate who raises it unprompted has been in that position.
- **Row: Wrap.**
  - Must land: the recap and the push-versus-pull tradeoff.
  - Adds: naming the delivery-latency assumption and adaptive thresholds first.
  - Separating signal: finding the flaw before the interviewer does.

**How to use this table in preparation**
Rehearse the whole answer, then score only the fourth column. Each row is a yes-or-no observable rather than an impression.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Suspicion versus certainty | The difference between evidence of silence and knowledge of failure. | Separating them up front is the requirements-phase signal. |
| Non-goal | Something explicitly excluded from the design, stated up front. | Remediation and metrics, each for its own reason. |
| Device class | A group of devices with a shared reporting pattern and constraints. | Allows different detection targets under one system. |
| Hot path | The code and storage touched on every request. | The estimate decides it must be memory, not a database. |
| Static stability | Continuing to behave correctly without a dependency, because everything needed is already local. | The architecture-phase addition. |
| Owner epoch | A number increased on each ownership change, checked before any write. | Extends fencing from inside a process to across processes. |
| Monotonic versus wall-clock | Elapsed-time measurement that is process-local, versus calendar time that is comparable but jumpy. | The boundary that makes recovery after failover subtle. |
| Conservative rebuild | Erring toward waiting longer when reconstructing deadlines from uncertain inputs. | Prevents mass false expiry after a failover. |
| Outbox lag | How far behind the transition consumer is running. | Determines real alert latency. |
| Cell blast radius | How much of the fleet one cell's failure affects. | The containment property cells buy. |
| Monitoring-of-the-monitor | Independent checking of whether the observation system itself works. | The operations-phase separating signal. |
| Separating signal | A specific observable that distinguishes a stronger answer from a complete one. | The fourth column, and the only one worth scoring. |

## Interview pushback and validation

Eight challenges, each with a baseline and a stretch answer, then nine things to test.

---

> - Q1: Does push reduce total fleet work to `O(1)`?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Each device does constant work, but the service receives `O(N)` reports.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Hierarchical summaries reduce global traffic while regional collection remains proportional to fleet size.

**What is really being asked**
Whether you understand what push actually buys, or whether you have absorbed "push scales better" as a slogan.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Per-device work is constant. Total server work is not.

- Worked example: each device sends one message every 15 seconds regardless of fleet size, so a device's cost is O(1). The service receives 66,667 messages per second at a million devices and 666,667 at ten million, so the centre is O(N).
- The real benefits of push are different and worth stating: it works through NAT, and the sending schedule is distributed across a million independent devices instead of driven by one scheduler.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
You can reduce *global* traffic without reducing regional traffic.

- Worked example: each region's collectors report a summary upward, such as "region us-east: 99,997 of 100,000 up, 3 suspect, here are their identifiers". The global view receives one message per region per interval instead of a million.
- The honest limit: regional collection is still O(N). Hierarchy moves the O(N) to the edge rather than removing it. Saying that is what distinguishes the answer from the same slogan repeated one level up.

---

> - Q2: A heartbeat arrives while its timer expires.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Compare observation version and deadline under one atomic boundary.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Reject transitions from a prior shard owner as well.

**What is really being asked**
The central race, asked directly.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Two comparisons, both under one atomic boundary.

- Worked example, as a step table:

| Time | Event | Version | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 14:00:00 | Heartbeat | 7 | deadline 14:00:45 |
| 14:00:44 | Heartbeat | 8 | deadline 14:01:29 |
| 14:00:45 | Timer created at version 7 fires | 8 | 7 ≠ 8, discarded |

- "under one atomic boundary" is the phrase that matters: checking and then acting as separate steps leaves a window where a heartbeat lands between them, which is the same race one level down.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
The same wrong write can come from a different source: an old owner that still believes it owns the shard.

- The version compare cannot catch it, because the old owner has its own consistent view of versions.
- The epoch does catch it: every publish carries its epoch, and only the current one is accepted.
- The signal is recognizing that a correct answer needs both, because they defend against different failures.

---

> - Q3: Can two failed probes confirm a dead machine?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - They confirm failed observations, not the physical cause.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Require independent evidence and a fenced remediation protocol.

**What is really being asked**
Whether you will claim certainty when offered an easy opportunity to.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Two failed probes confirm exactly one thing: two probes failed.

- Worked example of all the things that produce identical evidence: the device is powered off; the site's link is down; a firewall rule changed this morning; the probe's own credentials expired. All four look the same from the server.
- The correct statement is that evidence accumulated, not that a cause was established.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
If something must act on this, two requirements.

1. **Independent evidence**, meaning a check whose failure mode is genuinely different from the heartbeat path, such as a local gateway at the site.
2. **A fenced remediation protocol**, meaning any replacement first takes an exclusive, authoritative claim, so a wrong conclusion cannot produce two active owners.
- The framing worth saying: you never remove the uncertainty, you build a protocol that stays correct despite it.

---

> - Q4: Devices cannot run an agent.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Use pull where reachable or an on-site observer.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Renegotiate detection coverage and freshness where neither path exists.

**What is really being asked**
The premise of the whole design is an agent that sends heartbeats. What if there is none?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Two fallbacks, in order of preference.

1. **Pull where the device is reachable.** Works for directly addressable devices, which is the subset NAT does not block.
2. **An on-site observer.** A small component at the customer's site that can see devices locally and reports on their behalf.
   - Worked example: 200 devices at a site, none individually reachable. One gateway observes all 200 locally and sends one heartbeat covering the set. The heartbeat model is preserved, at a different granularity.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
If neither exists, the honest response is to renegotiate the requirement, not to pretend.

- Worked example: a device with no agent, no reachability, and no local observer. You cannot detect its failure in 60 seconds by any mechanism. What you can offer is coverage of a different kind, such as detection when it next interacts with another system, at a freshness of hours rather than seconds.
- Stating that a requirement cannot be met, and offering what can, is a stronger answer than a design that quietly does not work.

---

> - Q5: What happens when collectors restart?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Rebuild state and expose a warm-up unknown interval.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Move ownership with an epoch and reconcile transition history before paging.

**What is really being asked**
The in-memory hot state is the design's main tradeoff. What does a restart cost?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Rebuild from the checkpoint, and report `UNKNOWN` during the warm-up.

- Worked example of skipping the warm-up: the collector was down for 3 minutes, so every restored deadline is already past. On the first tick, all 100,000 devices expire at once and page, even though most were healthy throughout.
- The warm-up says: for one interval, report that we do not know, and let fresh heartbeats re-establish reality.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Two additions.

1. **Move ownership with a new epoch**, so the restarted process and any lingering old process cannot both publish.
2. **Reconcile transition history before paging.**
   - Worked example: during the restart, a device genuinely failed. On recovery the collector sees it silent and would page. But a transition may already have been recorded before the restart. Reconciling first prevents a duplicate page for one event, which matters because duplicate pages during a recovery are exactly when operators start ignoring the channel.

---

> - Q6: Why not persist every heartbeat?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - The daily row count is billions
>     - Transitions meet the history requirement.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Retain sampled raw evidence only for a bounded forensic window.

**What is really being asked**
Whether you can justify discarding data, which always feels risky.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Two parts, and the second is the one that makes it safe.

1. **5.76 billion rows per day**, about 576 gigabytes daily at 100 bytes per row.
2. **Transitions already satisfy every real query.** Nobody asks whether a device was up at a particular second. They ask when it went down and what went down with it, which is a transition query.
- Worked example of the ratio: roughly 2,000,000 transitions per month against 172 billion heartbeats.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Keep a small raw sample for a bounded window, for the cases transitions cannot answer.

- Worked example: a device is flapping between `UP` and `SUSPECT`. The transitions tell you it flapped. The raw heartbeats tell you the interarrival gaps were 44, 46, 43, 47 seconds, sitting exactly on the threshold, which identifies a latency problem rather than a device problem.
- "bounded forensic window" means hours, and it means the retention is a deliberate cost decision rather than an accident.

---

> - Q7 (premise reversal): Why not write `last_seen` to the database and scan for stale rows?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - That is `66,667` row writes/s plus a `1 million`-row scan every tick
>     - The database becomes the hot path and the scan bounds detection latency.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - The database is a checkpoint for recovery
>     - The deadline index is in-memory per shard owner and fenced by epoch.

**What is really being asked**
The premise reversal, and the most valuable question here. The whole design exists to avoid the simple approach. Was that justified?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Two independent reasons, and both are needed.

1. **The write rate.** 66,667 row updates per second, continuously, on one table. Worked example: a well-tuned relational instance sustains a few thousand. You are an order of magnitude over with no quiet period, and the updates contend with the scan.
2. **The scan bounds detection latency.** This is the subtler reason and the better one to lead with.
   - Worked example: to publish suspicion within 60 seconds you must scan at least that often. A million-row scan every 30 seconds is 33,000 rows examined per second purely to find the few that expired, and it runs against a table already absorbing 66,667 writes per second. Making detection faster means scanning more often, which makes the load worse. The approach becomes less viable exactly as the requirement tightens.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
The database is not removed, it is repositioned.

- It becomes a checkpoint for recovery, written at a bounded rate, rather than the serving path.
- The deadline index moves to memory, per shard owner, fenced by epoch.
- The closing line worth saying: the database was never the wrong technology, it was the wrong place in the flow.

---

> - Q8: Can the device's `sentAt` drive the deadline?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - No
>     - Device clocks are unordered across the fleet, so collector receive time drives timeouts.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Keep `sentAt` only to measure agent-side delay and detect clock drift as a diagnostic.

**What is really being asked**
Using the device's own timestamp seems more accurate, since it knows when it actually sent. Why reject it?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Device clocks across a million field devices cannot be trusted or compared.

- Worked example of the direct exploit or accident: a device with a clock one hour fast sends `sentAt` in the future. Its deadline is an hour away, so it can fail and never be marked suspect. One misconfigured clock disables monitoring for that device permanently, and nothing logs an error.
- Worked example of the milder case: a device 30 seconds slow appears already two-thirds of the way to its timeout on every heartbeat, so it is marked suspect constantly.
- The collector's clock is one clock for all devices in a shard, which makes the comparisons meaningful.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Keep the field, but only as a diagnostic.

- `receive_time − sentAt` estimates the delivery delay, which is exactly the measurement the weakest assumption needs.
- A value that is negative, or wildly large, identifies clock drift on that device, which is worth knowing for other reasons.
- The principle: untrusted input can be valuable data while remaining unusable as a control signal.

---

> - Validation scenarios
>   - Expiry racing with refresh.
>   - Timer tick rounding.
>   - Old incarnation replay after reboot.
>   - Clock regression on collector host.
>   - Collector outage causing mass silence.
>   - Verifier unavailable behind NAT.
>   - Ownership transfer with delayed old-owner writes.
>   - Registry outage during steady-state heartbeats.
>   - Reconnect storm after a regional partition heals.

**What this says in plain words**
Nine tests, each mapping to a mechanism discussed earlier.

1. **Expiry racing with refresh.** The direct test of the version compare. Fire a timer at the exact moment a heartbeat lands and assert the device stays `UP`.
2. **Timer tick rounding.** Assert a wheel bucket never fires before the true deadline, and that the exact check catches it when it does.
3. **Old incarnation replay after reboot.** Replay a pre-reboot heartbeat after the new incarnation is active and assert it is ignored.
4. **Clock regression on collector host.** Step the host clock backwards and assert deadlines do not lengthen and nothing mints extra time.
5. **Collector outage causing mass silence.** Kill a collector and assert its devices become `UNKNOWN` with one grouped alarm, not 100,000 `SUSPECT` pages.
6. **Verifier unavailable behind NAT.** Assert a device with no reachable path stays `SUSPECT` rather than being wrongly promoted to `UNREACHABLE`.
7. **Ownership transfer with delayed old-owner writes.** The direct epoch test: transfer ownership, then have the old owner attempt a write, and assert it is rejected.
8. **Registry outage during steady-state heartbeats.** The static-stability test: stop the registry and assert heartbeat processing continues from cached assignments.
9. **Reconnect storm after a regional partition heals.** Assert ingress survives the burst and that recovery transitions are grouped rather than replayed as 100,000 individual alerts.

**Why it matters**
Each item names a mechanism that could be wrong and the observation that would reveal it, which is what turns a design into something testable.

## Recall card

Five one-liners and a four-sentence closing script, memorized rather than improvised.

---

> - “The registry defines expected devices; heartbeats establish recent observations.”

**What this says in plain words**
Two different kinds of truth in one sentence. The registry says what should be there. Heartbeats say what has been seen.

**Why it matters**
This split is what makes absence detectable at all.

- Worked example: a device that has never enrolled goes silent forever, and nothing happens, correctly, because nothing expected it. A registered device goes silent for 45 seconds and becomes suspect. The same silence means different things depending on the registry.
- It also protects the hot path: the registry is edited rarely by humans, while observations change 66,667 times per second. Keeping them in separate places keeps the configuration path away from the write-heavy path.

---

> - “A versioned deadline prevents stale expiry from overwriting fresh evidence.”

**What this says in plain words**
Every observation carries a version. A timer captures the version at the moment it was created. When it fires, it compares, and a mismatch means it is out of date.

**Why it matters**
It is the central correctness mechanism in nine words.

- Worked example: a timer holding version 7 fires on a device now at version 8, and it is discarded. Without the compare, the device is marked suspect 45 seconds after a heartbeat that arrived 5 seconds ago, thousands of times a minute across the fleet.

---

> - “Silence produces suspicion, while monitoring outages produce visible uncertainty.”

**What this says in plain words**
Two different failures produce two different states. A silent device becomes `SUSPECT`. A broken collector produces `UNKNOWN`.

**Why it matters**
It is the sentence that separates this design from a naive one.

- Worked example: a collector dies. The naive system reports 100,000 devices down and pages accordingly. This design reports "monitoring is blind for 100,000 devices", which is a different incident with a different first action.
- "visible" is the operative word: the uncertainty is surfaced rather than hidden behind a stale `UP`.

---

> - “Independent verification and grouped alerts improve diagnosis without pretending to prove a crash.”

**What this says in plain words**
Two mechanisms improve the quality of what an operator sees, and neither converts evidence into proof.

**Why it matters**
The last clause is the discipline.

- Verification adds evidence. Grouping adds context. Neither establishes that a device is physically dead, because that is not obtainable.
- Worked example of a design that forgets this: after two failed probes it marks a device `DEAD` and triggers replacement. Every network blip becomes a replacement, and the system's confidence is unearned.

---

> - “Collectors keep serving on cached assignments when the registry is down; that is the static-stability line.”

**What this says in plain words**
A control-plane outage does not stop heartbeat processing, because each collector already holds what it needs.

**Why it matters**
It names the property, which makes it something you can point at rather than a happy accident.

- Worked example: one hour of registry downtime, 240,000,000 heartbeats processed normally, and the only visible effect is that enrollment and maintenance edits fail.
- "that is the static-stability line" is a deliberate act of naming the boundary: on one side, things that keep working from local state; on the other, things that genuinely require the control plane.

---

> - Wrap-up script
>   - “The design is a registry of expected devices, heartbeats sharded by device id to one owner, an in-memory deadline index, and a separate coverage watchdog.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence one: the architecture in four components.

**Why it matters**
The recap must stand alone, because the interviewer may write their notes from these four sentences. Each component named here is one the rest of the interview depended on.

---

> -   - “The key invariant is that expiry is version-checked and epoch-fenced, so a stale timer or an old owner never overwrites fresh evidence.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence two: the correctness property, with both mechanisms and both failures they prevent.

**Why it matters**
Two mechanisms for two distinct races, stated in one breath. A candidate who names only the version compare has covered the in-process race and missed the cross-process one.

---

> -   - “The main tradeoff is push for NAT-friendliness and distributed scheduling versus pull for observer control; I use push with bounded verification.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence three: what you gave up and what you bought, ending in the choice.

- Given up: the observer's control over when checks happen.
- Bought: the ability to reach devices behind NAT, and a sending schedule distributed across a million devices.
- The resolution: push as primary, pull as a bounded verifier for the suspect cohort only.

**Why it matters**
It shows the choice was made rather than inherited, and "bounded" signals that you know pull does not scale to an incident.

---

> -   - “My weakest assumption is heartbeat delivery latency well under the timeout; I would measure interarrival jitter per device class before trusting the `45 s` threshold.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence four: the flaw, and the specific measurement you would take first.

**Why it matters**
"per device class" is what makes it a real plan rather than a gesture.

- A fleet-wide average hides the problem entirely: 99% of devices at 15 seconds and 1% at 50 seconds averages to about 15, while that 1% is 10,000 devices generating constant false suspicions.
- Naming the measurement, the granularity, and the decision it would inform is the difference between acknowledging a weakness and owning it.

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Registry | The authoritative list of which devices are expected to report. | Makes absence meaningful. |
| Observation | Evidence gathered from heartbeats, as opposed to configuration. | Kept separate from the registry. |
| Versioned deadline | A deadline whose associated observation version is captured by the timer. | The central correctness mechanism. |
