---

> - Why this is hard
>   - Naive: a counter per tenant in each gateway's memory, reset every second.

**What this says in plain words**
The obvious first design is to keep a small counter inside each gateway process.
One counter per tenant. Add one on each request. Set it back to zero every second.

**Why it matters**
This is the design everyone reaches for, so the interviewer is waiting to see whether you know why it fails.
Naming the naive design first is a signal in itself: it shows you are choosing complexity deliberately rather than out of habit.

- Worked example of the naive design working fine
  - One gateway, one tenant, limit of 1,000 requests per second.
  - The counter goes 1, 2, 3 … 1,000, then the 1,001st request is rejected.
  - At one second the counter resets to 0.
  - With exactly one gateway this is correct and costs nothing.

---

> -   - First break: `N` gateways each hold a full budget, so a tenant gets `N×` the quota, and a restart wipes the count.

**What this says in plain words**
This one line carries two separate failures. Split them.

1. **Multiplication across gateways.**
   - Each gateway keeps its own private counter.
   - Each one independently believes the tenant may send 1,000 per second.
   - Worked example: 8 gateways × 1,000 = 8,000 requests per second actually admitted, against a policy of 1,000.
   - The backend was sized for 1,000, so it now receives 8× its designed load and starts timing out.
2. **A restart erases the memory.**
   - The counter only exists in process memory.
   - Worked example: a tenant has spent 950 of its 1,000 at the 0.6-second mark. The gateway is redeployed. The new process starts at 0. The tenant immediately spends another 1,000 in the same second, for 1,950 total.
   - Deploys, crashes, and autoscaling all produce this, and none of them are rare.

**Why it matters**
Both failures are silent.
Nothing logs an error. The limiter reports that it is working. The backend simply falls over, and the graph shows a traffic spike with no cause.

---

> -   - Second break: the shared owner times out after debiting, or fails over with a lagging replica, and nobody knows whether the tokens were spent.

**What this says in plain words**
Suppose you fix the first break by putting the counter in one shared place. A new problem appears, again in two parts.

1. **The timeout is ambiguous.**
   - The gateway asks the shared counter "may I spend 1 unit?" and waits.
   - No answer comes back within the deadline.
   - The gateway cannot distinguish "the request never arrived" from "the counter was decremented and the reply was lost".
   - Worked example: budget 1,000, the counter is actually at 640 spent, the gateway times out, retries, and the counter goes to 642 for a tenant that made one request. Repeated at scale, a tenant is rejected while well under its real quota.
2. **Failover loses recent writes.**
   - The shared counter has a backup copy that is normally a fraction of a second behind.
   - If the main copy dies, the backup is promoted, and it is missing the last few hundred milliseconds of debits.
   - Worked example: the tenant had spent 900 of 1,000. The backup thinks 700 were spent. The tenant gets 300 extra requests for free.

**Why it matters**
This is the level the question is actually testing.
Anyone can write the counter arithmetic. The interview signal is whether you know that a shared counter converts a correctness problem into an ownership-and-failure problem.

---

> -   - Minimum primitives
> -     - One atomic refill-and-debit per bucket
> -     - A stable bucket key
> -     - A versioned policy snapshot
> -     - An explicit degradation rule per policy.

**What this says in plain words**
These are the four parts you cannot drop. If any one is missing, the design has a hole you can name.

1. **One atomic refill-and-debit per bucket.**
   - "Atomic" means the add-tokens-back step and the spend-a-token step happen as one indivisible action that no other request can interleave with.
   - Without it, two requests both read "1 token left" and both spend it, leaving −1.
2. **A stable bucket key.**
   - The name under which the counter is stored must not change when a policy is edited.
   - Worked example: if the key includes the policy version, then editing the limit at 14:00 creates a brand-new, completely full bucket at 14:00, handing the tenant a free extra burst on every edit.
3. **A versioned policy snapshot.**
   - Every gateway carries a numbered copy of the current limits.
   - The number lets you say which gateways are on the old rules and for how long.
4. **An explicit degradation rule per policy.**
   - Written down in advance, per rule: when the counter cannot be reached, does this rule allow or reject?
   - Without it, the decision gets made accidentally by whatever the code does on exception, usually allow, usually during an outage, which is the worst possible time.

**Why it matters**
In the room, these four give you a recovery path.
If you get lost, you can say "let me check my four primitives" and walk them, and each one leads to a concrete piece of the design.

> - 🗣 “The algorithm is ten lines; the design is who owns the bucket and what happens when that owner cannot answer in time.”

**What this says in plain words**
A quotable one-liner. The 🗣 marker means: say this sentence out loud, roughly verbatim.
It claims that the token-bucket math is the easy part, and the real design work is ownership and failure behavior.

**Why it matters**
It reframes the question in your favor within ten seconds.

- Most candidates spend their time on the algorithm, because the algorithm is the part that feels like an exam question.
- This sentence tells the interviewer you already know the algorithm is not the hard part, which buys you permission to spend your deep-dive minutes on ownership and timeouts.
- 🗣 sentences are memorized, not improvised. Under interview pressure your sentence quality drops; a pre-written line does not.

---

> - Opening script
>   - “I will design a regional per-tenant token bucket with one atomic owner per bucket, then make its timeout and failover behavior explicit.”

**What this says in plain words**
The first sentence out of your mouth. It announces four decisions at once.

1. *Regional* — the limit is enforced within one region, not globally across the planet.
2. *Per-tenant token bucket* — the algorithm and what it is keyed by.
3. *One atomic owner per bucket* — exactly one place makes the decision for a given counter.
4. *Then make its timeout and failover behavior explicit* — a promise about what you will cover later.

**Why it matters**
The interviewer now has a map of your answer before you have drawn anything.

- If they disagree with any of the four, they will interrupt in the first 30 seconds, which is the cheapest possible moment to be corrected.
- Without an opening like this, a disagreement about global versus regional can surface at minute 35, after you have built everything on the wrong assumption.

---

> -   - “First I confirm whether bursts are allowed, whether the limit is regional or global, and whether it protects capacity or a security boundary.”

**What this says in plain words**
Announces the three questions you will ask before designing. Each one genuinely changes the design.

1. **Bursts allowed?**
   - A burst is a short spike above the steady rate that you still accept.
   - Worked example: a limit of 10 per second with a burst of 20 means a client that has been quiet can fire 20 immediately, then settle to 10 per second.
   - If bursts are forbidden, a token bucket is the wrong algorithm and you need a different structure.
2. **Regional or global?**
   - Regional means each region enforces its own budget independently, with no cross-region conversation on the request path.
   - Global means the regions must agree, which adds roughly 100 ms of round trip to every decision on a 2 ms latency budget.
3. **Capacity protection or security boundary?**
   - Capacity protection keeps a backend from being overwhelmed. If the limiter breaks, letting some traffic through is often better than rejecting everything.
   - A security boundary stops password guessing or credential stuffing. If the limiter breaks, you must reject, because allowing is exactly the attacker's goal.

**Why it matters**
The third question alone flips the failure behavior from allow to reject. Getting it wrong turns your outage-handling story into a security incident.

---

> -   - “Then I size bucket state and decision traffic, and define an internal check API plus an admin policy API.”

**What this says in plain words**
Second phase: estimate how much memory the counters need and how many decisions per second arrive, then write down two interfaces.
One interface is used by gateways on every request. The other is used by administrators to change limits.

**Why it matters**
Separating the two interfaces is a real design decision, not bookkeeping.

- The check interface runs 100,000 times per second and must answer in about 2 milliseconds.
- The policy interface runs a few times per day and can take 200 milliseconds.
- Worked example of merging them wrongly: if a policy edit takes a lock that the check path also needs, one administrator saving a form stalls 100,000 decisions per second for the duration of the write.

---

> -   - “The deep dive is the atomic refill-and-consume script, its clock handling, and the ambiguous-timeout race.”

**What this says in plain words**
Names the three things you will go deep on, in advance.

1. The small program that runs on the counter's owner and does refill-then-spend as one action.
2. How time is read, because refill depends on elapsed time and clocks misbehave.
3. What to do when a decision request times out and you do not know whether it took effect.

**Why it matters**
Interviewers frequently ask "what would you like to go deeper on?" and a candidate who has not pre-chosen wanders.
Pre-announcing also means the interviewer can redirect you early if they want a different deep dive, instead of at minute 40.

---

> -   - “I will close with the failure policy per rule, the hot-tenant limit, and what changes under a global or exact budget.”

**What this says in plain words**
Announces the ending: the per-rule allow-or-reject decision, the ceiling created by one very large tenant, and how the design would change under stronger requirements.

**Why it matters**
Naming the hot-tenant limit yourself is the single highest-value move in the whole script.

- It is the weakest point of the design.
- Stating it before you are asked reads as ownership.
- Being caught by it at minute 50 reads as a blind spot.

## Requirements — IC3 baseline

This section turns a one-sentence prompt into a specification.
It has five parts: a warning about the numbers, the clarifying-question table, the functional scope, the numeric target table, and the invariants plus non-goals.

---

> - Numerical targets are interview assumptions unless an adjacent primary source states otherwise.

**What this says in plain words**
Every number in this file was chosen by the author of the file to make the design concrete.
None of them came from Oracle, unless a link next to the number says so.

**Why it matters**
It protects you from a specific trap.

- If you say "the target is 100,000 checks per second" as though it were a fact, the interviewer may ask where that came from, and you have no answer.
- If you say "I will assume 100,000 checks per second; tell me if the real number is different", you have given yourself a number to design against and invited a correction.
- Worked example of the difference: at 100,000 per second you need about 4 shards. At 1,000 per second you need one, and the whole sharding discussion is wasted interview time.

---

> | Question | Assumed answer | Why it changes the design |
> |---|---|---|
> | Are bursts allowed? | Yes, up to a configured burst `B` | Selects token bucket over a sliding log or fixed window |
> | Regional or global limit? | Regional; global is a stretch | One regional owner per bucket avoids cross-region coordination on every request |
> | Capacity protection or security boundary? | Both exist; each rule declares which | Decides fail-open with a bound versus fail-closed on owner loss |
> | What identifies the quota? | Authenticated tenant plus normalized route class | Attacker-controlled raw URLs cannot mint new buckets |
> | Is an exact rolling-window count required? | No; a burst envelope is acceptable | Exactness would force a sliding log and stronger state ownership |
> | Who can change limits? | Tenant administrators through a versioned policy API | Policy becomes a control-plane resource with audit and rollout |

**What this says in plain words**
Six questions to ask the interviewer, the answer you will assume if they leave it to you, and what each answer decides.

**Why it matters**
The third column is the whole point.
A clarifying question that does not change the design is filler, and interviewers notice filler. Every row here earns its place.

- **Row: Are bursts allowed?**
  - Plain meaning: may a quiet client fire a short spike that exceeds the steady rate?
  - Assumed yes, up to a configured ceiling `B`.
  - Why it decides the algorithm: a token bucket *saves up* unspent allowance, which is exactly what a burst is. A fixed window does not save anything up. A sliding log tracks exact timestamps and permits no burst beyond the strict count.
  - Worked example: rate 10 per second, burst 20. A client silent for 5 seconds has accumulated the full 20 tokens and can send 20 at once, then falls back to 10 per second.
- **Row: Regional or global limit?**
  - Plain meaning: is the tenant's 1,000 per second counted per region, or once for the whole world?
  - Assumed regional.
  - Why it decides the topology: a global count requires the regions to agree on every single request.
  - Worked example: a cross-region round trip is on the order of 100 milliseconds. The latency target in this design is 2 milliseconds. One global check would blow the budget by 50×, so global correctness has to be bought some other way, which is the IC4 stretch.
- **Row: Capacity protection or security boundary?**
  - Plain meaning: is this limit protecting a server from overload, or protecting an account from attack?
  - Assumed both exist, and each individual rule says which kind it is.
  - Why it decides failure behavior:
    - Capacity rule, limiter unreachable: allowing some traffic is better than a total outage. This is fail-open, and it must be bounded.
    - Security rule, limiter unreachable: allowing traffic is the attacker's win. This is fail-closed, reject.
  - Worked example: the login endpoint has a 5-attempts-per-minute rule. If the counter is unreachable and you fail open, an attacker gets unlimited password guesses precisely during your outage.
- **Row: What identifies the quota?**
  - Plain meaning: what exact string do you use as the bucket's name?
  - Assumed the authenticated tenant identity plus a normalized route class, not the raw URL.
  - Why it decides safety: if the raw URL were part of the key, each distinct URL would create its own fresh, full bucket.
  - Worked example of the attack: an attacker calls `/v1/items?x=1`, `/v1/items?x=2`, and so on. With raw URLs as keys, 10,000 distinct query strings mint 10,000 separate full buckets and the limit is bypassed entirely. Normalizing to the route template `/v1/items` collapses all of them into one bucket.
- **Row: Is an exact rolling-window count required?**
  - Plain meaning: must you be able to guarantee "never more than 1,000 in any 60-second window, measured from any instant"?
  - Assumed no. A burst envelope is enough.
  - Why it decides state size: exactness means remembering every individual admitted request's timestamp so you can age them out precisely.
  - Worked example: at 100,000 requests per second, an exact 60-second window means holding 6,000,000 timestamps in memory, continuously pruned. The token bucket holds two numbers per tenant instead.
- **Row: Who can change limits?**
  - Plain meaning: is a limit a code constant or a piece of live configuration with an owner?
  - Assumed live configuration, edited by tenant administrators through an API, with versions.
  - Why it decides structure: it makes policy a control-plane resource, which brings along audit trails, authorization, staged rollout, and rollback.
  - Worked example of the alternative: if limits are constants in code, raising one customer's limit requires a deploy, which takes 40 minutes and cannot be rolled back per tenant.

---

> - Functional scope
>   - Decide allow or reject for `(tenant, route class, cost)` before expensive backend work.
>   - Permit a configured burst and refill at a configured rate.
>   - Serialize each bucket's decisions at one regional owner.
>   - Distribute versioned policies to gateways without a restart.
>   - Return `429` with a retry hint
>   - Report degraded admission separately from normal success.

**What this says in plain words**
Six things the system must do. Taken one at a time:

1. **Decide allow or reject early.**
   - The decision happens before the backend does real work.
   - Worked example: rejecting at the gateway costs about 1 millisecond of CPU. Rejecting after a database query costs 50 milliseconds plus a database connection, so a rejected flood still takes the database down.
2. **Permit a burst, refill at a rate.**
   - Two knobs per rule: how big the saved-up allowance can get, and how fast it comes back.
3. **Serialize each bucket at one owner.**
   - *Serialize* means the decisions for one bucket happen strictly one after another, never simultaneously.
   - Different buckets proceed in parallel. Only one bucket's own decisions are ordered.
4. **Distribute versioned policies without a restart.**
   - A limit change reaches the gateways while they keep serving.
   - Worked example: without this, changing one tenant's limit means a rolling restart of the entire gateway fleet.
5. **Return `429` with a retry hint.**
   - `429` is the HTTP status code for "too many requests".
   - The retry hint tells the client how many seconds until it is worth trying again.
   - Worked example: without a hint, a client retries immediately in a tight loop and turns a rate-limit rejection into a second denial-of-service attack against your gateway.
6. **Report degraded admission separately from normal success.**
   - When the limiter could not check properly and let the request through anyway, that outcome is recorded with its own distinct code.
   - Worked example: if degraded allows are recorded as ordinary allows, you can be fail-open for six hours and every dashboard looks perfectly healthy.

---

> | Requirement | Target | Enforced by |
> |---|---|---|
> | Regional decision throughput | `100,000` checks/s | Sharded bucket owners; one atomic operation per check |
> | Decision latency | p99 < `2 ms` in-region | Single round trip to a colocated owner; no cross-region call on the request path |
> | Decision timeout | Stop waiting at `5 ms` | Gateway deadline; then the rule's explicit degradation policy |
> | Decision-service availability | `99.95%` | Replicated owner per shard; gateways keep a last-known policy snapshot |
> | Policy propagation | Normal changes visible within `10 s` | Versioned snapshot push plus periodic pull |
> | Bucket state bound | Tokens within `[0, B]` at all times | Clamp inside the owner script; clamp again on capacity edits |
> | Degraded-admission visibility | `100%` of emergency admissions tagged; alarm above `1%` of decisions | Distinct outcome code on the decision and a dedicated metric |

**What this says in plain words**
The non-functional requirements. Each row is a promise with a number attached and a named mechanism that keeps the promise.

**Why it matters**
A requirement without a number cannot be violated, so it cannot be designed against.
"Low latency" is unfalsifiable. "p99 under 2 milliseconds" tells you that a cross-region call is forbidden.

- **Row: Regional decision throughput, 100,000 checks per second.**
  - Every admitted or rejected request costs exactly one operation at an owner.
  - Consequence: you need enough owners to sustain 100,000 operations per second, which is computed in the next section.
- **Row: Decision latency, p99 under 2 milliseconds.**
  - p99 means the 99th percentile: 99 out of 100 decisions finish faster than this.
  - Worked example of why p99 and not average: at 100,000 per second, the slowest 1% is 1,000 requests every second. An average of 1 millisecond with a p99 of 200 milliseconds is a bad system that looks fine on the average.
  - Consequence: the owner must be in the same region and ideally the same data centre as the gateway.
- **Row: Decision timeout, stop waiting at 5 milliseconds.**
  - A hard deadline. At 5 milliseconds the gateway gives up and applies the degradation policy.
  - Worked example of omitting it: if a gateway holds 2,000 concurrent connections and each waits 30 seconds for a dead owner, the gateway itself runs out of workers and stops serving healthy tenants too. The limiter's outage becomes a total outage.
- **Row: Decision-service availability, 99.95%.**
  - 99.95% of a 30-day month leaves about 21.6 minutes of allowed downtime.
  - Enforced two ways: each shard has a replica, and gateways keep working from their last policy snapshot.
- **Row: Policy propagation, visible within 10 seconds.**
  - A normal limit change reaches every gateway within 10 seconds.
  - Worked example of what the number buys you: an administrator who lowers a limit at 14:00:00 knows that by 14:00:10 the whole fleet enforces it. Without a stated bound, "eventually" could be an hour and nobody could say it was wrong.
- **Row: Bucket state bound, tokens within `[0, B]`.**
  - The token count is never negative and never above the burst ceiling.
  - Enforced in two places, and both are needed: inside the atomic script on every operation, and again when an administrator lowers the capacity.
  - Worked example of the second clamp: capacity is 1,000, the bucket is full at 1,000, an administrator lowers capacity to 100. Without a clamp on edit, the bucket sits at 1,000 tokens under a 100 ceiling and the tenant gets a 10× burst once.
- **Row: Degraded-admission visibility, 100% tagged, alarm above 1%.**
  - Every emergency admission carries a distinguishable outcome code, and an alarm fires if more than 1% of all decisions are degraded.
  - Worked example: at 100,000 decisions per second, 1% is 1,000 degraded admissions per second. That is a real signal, well above noise, and worth waking someone for.

---

> - Invariants
>   - Tokens stay between `0` and capacity `B`.
>   - Each atomic execution at the current bucket owner applies one debit.
>   - Retried logical decisions need an atomic replay record to avoid a second debit.
>   - With stable configuration and clock, admitted cost in an interval of duration `t` is at most `B + r × t`.
>   - This is a burst envelope, not a guarantee of exactly `r` requests in every one-second window.
>   - Numerical targets above are interview assumptions unless a reference explicitly supplies them.

**What this says in plain words**
An invariant is a statement that is true at every instant, no matter what else is happening. These are the five the design promises.

1. **Tokens stay in `[0, B]`.**
   - Never negative, never above the ceiling.
2. **Each atomic execution applies one debit.**
   - One run of the script spends tokens exactly once.
   - Note the careful wording: *at the current bucket owner*. After a failover the owner changed, and the promise does not reach across that boundary.
3. **A retried decision needs a replay record to avoid a second debit.**
   - If the gateway retries after an ambiguous timeout, something must remember "request 7f3a was already charged".
   - Without it, one logical request is charged twice.
4. **Admitted cost over duration `t` is at most `B + r × t`.**
   - This is the actual mathematical guarantee, and it is worth memorizing.
   - Worked example: `B = 20`, `r = 10` per second, `t = 3` seconds. The most that can get through is `20 + 10 × 3 = 50` requests. Not 30.
   - The `B` term is the saved-up burst. The `r × t` term is what refills during the window.
   - The qualifier "with stable configuration and clock" is the defeater: edit the policy mid-window, or let the clock jump, and the bound does not hold.
5. **It is an envelope, not a per-second guarantee.**
   - The design does *not* promise "at most 10 in every one-second window".
   - Worked example of the difference: with `B = 20` and `r = 10`, a client can send 20 in the first 100 milliseconds. That single second saw 20 requests against a rate of 10, and the system is behaving exactly as specified.
   - Say this out loud in the interview. It is the difference between describing your guarantee accurately and overselling it.

**Why it matters**
Invariants are what you check a design against when someone proposes a change.
"Can we shard one tenant's bucket eight ways?" is answered instantly by checking invariant 4: eight independent buckets give `8B + 8rt`, which is eight times the promise.

---

> - Non-goals
>   - Billing-grade metering.
>   - An exact rolling-window count across regions.
>   - Protecting the backend from long-running requests that have already been admitted.

**What this says in plain words**
Three things this system deliberately does not do.

1. **Billing-grade metering.**
   - The counts here are not accurate enough to invoice a customer from.
   - Worked example: a failover that loses 300 debits is an acceptable capacity outcome and an unacceptable billing error.
2. **An exact rolling-window count across regions.**
   - No promise of "at most N worldwide in any window".
3. **Protecting the backend from long-running requests already admitted.**
   - The limiter counts admissions, not work in progress.
   - Worked example: 100 requests are admitted, each running a 30-second report. The limiter says the tenant is within its rate; the backend has 100 concurrent 30-second queries and is dying. Only a concurrency limit or queue limit in the backend fixes this.

**Why it matters**
Non-goals are a defensive move.

- They stop the interviewer's follow-up from looking like a gap you missed.
- The third one in particular sets up a strong answer later: "rate limiting and concurrency limiting are different admission controls, and you need both."

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|

## Capacity and consequence — IC3 baseline

The rule for this section in the source is strict: every estimate ends with the decision it forces.
An estimate that changes nothing is deleted. There are three estimates here.

---

> - Bucket state
>   - `10,000 tenants × 5 route classes = 50,000 buckets`.
>   - At an assumed `200 B` of logical state each, the raw state is `10 MB`.
>   - Allocator overhead and replication increase memory requirements.
>   - Decision: memory is not the first constraint, so choose shard count from operations per second, not from state size.

**What this says in plain words**
Work out how much memory all the counters need.

1. **How many buckets exist.**
   - One bucket per (tenant, route class) pair.
   - 10,000 tenants, each with 5 kinds of endpoint, gives 50,000 buckets.
2. **How big one bucket is.**
   - Each bucket holds a token count, a last-refill timestamp, and the policy version it applied.
   - Assume about 200 bytes each once you include the key string and the encoding overhead.
3. **Total raw state.**
   - 50,000 × 200 bytes = 10,000,000 bytes, about 10 megabytes.
4. **The real number is larger.**
   - Memory allocators round every allocation up, and per-key bookkeeping adds more.
   - Every replica holds a full copy, so two copies double it.
   - Even at 5× the raw estimate, you are at 50 megabytes.

**Why it matters — the decision**
10 megabytes, or even 50, is nothing. A single modern server has hundreds of gigabytes.
So memory does not decide anything here, and the number of shards must be chosen on a different basis: operations per second.

- Worked example of getting this backwards: if you sized the fleet by memory you would conclude "one small node is plenty", then discover in production that one node cannot process 100,000 operations per second and every decision is timing out.
- This is exactly why the source requires each estimate to end in a decision. The estimate's job here is to *rule memory out*, which is a real result.

---

> - Decision traffic
>   - One owner operation per request means `100,000 operations/s`.
>   - If a measured shard sustains `25,000 operations/s` at the target latency, use at least `4` active shards before failure headroom.
>   - This is a sizing example, not a Redis performance guarantee.
>   - Decision: place replicas of each shard across fault domains and reserve enough failover capacity for the largest domain loss.
>   - Adding an unrelated shard does not recover another shard's state or ownership.

**What this says in plain words**

1. **Every request costs exactly one operation.**
   - No batching, no caching of decisions. 100,000 requests per second means 100,000 owner operations per second.
2. **One shard handles about 25,000 per second at the latency target.**
   - The qualifier *at the target latency* is doing all the work. A shard can accept far more operations per second if you do not care that the 99th percentile climbs to 50 milliseconds.
3. **So you need at least 4 shards.**
   - 100,000 ÷ 25,000 = 4.
   - "At least" and "before failure headroom" mean 4 is the floor, not the answer.
4. **The 25,000 figure is illustrative.**
   - It is not a published Redis benchmark. In real life you measure it on your own hardware with your own script.

**Why it matters — the decision**
Two things follow, and they are separate.

- **Spread replicas across fault domains.**
  - A fault domain is a group of machines that can fail together: one rack, one power feed, one availability zone.
  - Worked example: 4 shards, each with a primary and a replica. If a shard's primary and replica sit in the same rack and that rack loses power, that shard is simply gone, and 25% of all tenants get no decisions at all.
- **Reserve failover capacity for the largest domain loss.**
  - Worked example: 4 shards across 2 fault domains, so losing one domain costs you 2 shards' worth of capacity. The surviving 2 shards must absorb 100,000 operations per second against a measured ceiling of 50,000. They cannot, so you need 6 or 8 shards, not 4.
- **The last line is the subtle one.**
  - "Adding an unrelated shard does not recover another shard's state or ownership."
  - Sharding buys throughput by splitting the work. It does not buy redundancy.
  - Worked example: buckets are assigned to shards by hashing the key. Tenant A's bucket lives on shard 2 and nowhere else. If shard 2 dies, adding shard 5 does nothing for tenant A, because shard 5 has never held that bucket. Redundancy comes from replication within a shard, not from having more shards.

---

> - Hot tenant
>   - A dominant tenant does not benefit from ordinary sharding.
>   - Its bucket still has one serial owner.
>   - Decision: monitor the hottest key and cap per-bucket rate before adding cluster nodes
>     - The fix is disjoint grants, not more shards.

**What this says in plain words**
Sharding spreads *different* buckets across machines. It cannot spread *one* bucket.

1. **One very large tenant gets no help from sharding.**
   - Its bucket is one key, and one key lives on exactly one shard.
2. **That bucket's decisions are serial.**
   - They happen one at a time, because that is what guarantees correctness.
3. **So the tenant's ceiling is one shard's ceiling.**
   - Worked example: your fleet is 8 shards at 25,000 operations per second each, a fleet total of 200,000. One tenant sends 40,000 requests per second, all to the same route class. Every one of those lands on the single shard holding its bucket. That shard is asked for 40,000 against a ceiling of 25,000. Its latency climbs past the 5-millisecond timeout, and now every tenant that happens to share that shard is also being degraded.
4. **The right response is not more shards.**
   - Adding a 9th shard does not move the one hot key anywhere useful.
   - The response is to cap that bucket's rate so the damage stays inside one tenant, and, if the tenant genuinely needs that throughput, to change the algorithm to disjoint grants.

**Why it matters — the decision**
Two actions, in order:

- **Monitor the hottest key.**
  - You need a metric showing operations per second for the single busiest bucket, not just the fleet average.
  - Worked example of why the average hides it: 8 shards, 200,000 total capacity, 100,000 total load. The fleet is at 50% and looks healthy. One shard is at 100% and timing out. Only a per-key or per-shard metric shows this.
- **Cap the per-bucket rate before scaling out.**
  - A cap makes the hot tenant absorb its own consequences instead of exporting them to co-located tenants.

**What a disjoint grant is** — the term appears here and is fully designed in the stretch section, so the short version:

→ Instead of asking the owner on every request, a gateway asks once for a block of 100 tokens.  
→ The owner subtracts 100 from the bucket immediately and hands the block over.  
⇒ That gateway now serves 100 requests locally with no owner traffic at all.  
⇒ Central operations drop from 100,000 per second toward 1,000 per second.  
→ The cost is that a gateway which crashes holding 60 unused tokens strands them until they expire.

- The word *disjoint* means the grants do not overlap: the same token is handed to exactly one gateway, so the total across all gateways still respects the bucket's budget.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Bucket state | The few fields stored per bucket: token count, last refill time, applied policy version. | What the memory estimate is counting. |
| Shard | One of several independent servers, each owning a disjoint subset of the buckets. | How throughput is scaled. |
| Sharding by key hash | Deciding which shard owns a key by hashing the key name. | Explains why one key lives on exactly one shard and cannot be spread. |
| Fault domain | A set of machines that can plausibly fail together, such as a rack, a power feed, or an availability zone. | Replicas must be placed in different ones or replication buys nothing. |
| Failover headroom | Spare capacity kept so the survivors can absorb the load of whatever failed. | The reason 4 shards is a floor rather than an answer. |
| Serial owner | The single process that handles one bucket's decisions strictly one at a time. | The source of the hot-tenant ceiling. |
| Hot key | One key receiving a disproportionate share of all traffic. | The failure mode that sharding cannot fix. |
| Disjoint grant | Handing a gateway an exclusive block of tokens in advance, so it can decide locally without contacting the owner. | The actual fix for a hot tenant, introduced here and designed in the stretch section. |
| Stranded tokens | Tokens inside a grant held by a gateway that died before spending them, unusable until the grant expires. | The cost paid for the grant model. |
| Replication | Keeping a second live copy of a shard's data so the shard survives losing one machine. | The only source of redundancy; sharding alone provides none. |

## Architecture and flow — IC3 baseline

The order in this section is deliberate: the diagram comes first, then who owns what, then one request traced end to end.
Drawing before explaining is a rule in the source's own quality checklist.

---

> ```text
> Data plane (per request):
> Client -> edge abuse filter -> authentication -> gateway limiter -> backend
>                                                    |
>                                                    v
>                                        bucket owner / Redis shard
>                                        (primary + replica, different fault domains)
>
> Control plane (per policy change):
> Policy API -> durable policy DB -> versioned snapshots -> gateways
> Decision outcomes -> bounded telemetry -> alarms and tenant reports
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
Two independent paths. The top one runs on every single request. The bottom one runs only when configuration changes.

**Reading the data plane, box by box**

1. **Client**
   - The caller. Could be a customer's application or a script.
2. **Edge abuse filter**
   - Sits before authentication and drops obvious garbage: known-bad addresses, malformed requests, floods from a single source.
   - Why it is before authentication: verifying a credential costs real work, so an attacker who can force you to authenticate 100,000 forged requests per second has already won something.
3. **Authentication**
   - Establishes *who* is calling, producing a trustworthy tenant identity.
   - This must come before the limiter, because the limiter's key is the authenticated tenant. A tenant identity taken from an unverified header can be forged, and then an attacker simply claims to be a different tenant on every request and never runs out of budget.
4. **Gateway limiter**
   - Builds the bucket key, looks up the local policy snapshot, and asks the owner for a decision.
5. **Backend**
   - The service doing the real work, reached only if the decision was allow.
6. **Bucket owner / Redis shard (the box hanging below the gateway)**
   - Holds the actual counter and runs the atomic script.
   - Drawn to the side rather than in the chain because it is a lookup, not a hop the request travels through. The request never passes *through* Redis.
   - "primary + replica, different fault domains" is the redundancy note from the capacity section.

**Reading the control plane**

1. **Policy API → durable policy DB**
   - An administrator's edit is written to a real database that survives restarts.
2. **durable policy DB → versioned snapshots**
   - The current set of limits is packaged into a numbered bundle.
3. **versioned snapshots → gateways**
   - Each gateway receives the bundle and keeps it locally.
4. **Decision outcomes → bounded telemetry → alarms and tenant reports**
   - Every decision emits an outcome. *Bounded* means the volume of telemetry is capped so that a traffic flood does not turn into a telemetry flood that takes out the metrics system as well.

**Why it matters**
The split is the design's main structural claim: the control plane can be entirely down and the data plane keeps deciding correctly, because each gateway already holds the policy it needs.

- Worked example: the policy database is offline for one hour. Administrators get an error when they try to save a change. Meanwhile 360,000,000 requests are decided correctly from snapshots. Zero customer impact on the request path.
- Without the split, a policy-database outage becomes a total outage, because every request would need a policy lookup.

---

> - Gateway
>   - Derives tenant identity from authentication.
>   - Resolves a route template rather than an attacker-controlled raw URL.
>   - Keeps a last-known validated policy snapshot, so the data plane keeps deciding when the control plane is down.
>   - Cost: snapshot lag means gateways can apply different policy versions for a bounded window.

**What this says in plain words**
The gateway has three jobs and one price.

1. **Derive tenant identity from authentication.**
   - The identity comes from the verified credential, never from a request header the caller controls.
2. **Resolve a route template.**
   - Turn `/v1/items/98432?fields=name` into the template `/v1/items/{id}` before using it in the key.
   - Worked example of skipping this: 1,000,000 distinct item IDs produce 1,000,000 distinct buckets, each full, and the limit is meaningless.
3. **Keep a last-known validated policy snapshot.**
   - *Validated* is load-bearing: the gateway checks the snapshot is well-formed before adopting it, so a corrupt push does not replace a working policy set with garbage.
4. **The cost: snapshot lag.**
   - Different gateways can be on different policy versions for a bounded window.
   - Worked example: the propagation target is 10 seconds. An administrator lowers a tenant's limit from 1,000 to 100 at 14:00:00. Gateway A adopts it at 14:00:02, gateway B at 14:00:09. For seven seconds the tenant's effective limit depends on which gateway it reaches.
   - This is acceptable because it is *bounded* and *stated*. A limit change is not a security boundary that must take effect instantly.

**Why it matters**
Naming the cost next to the benefit is the pattern the whole source file follows.
Every component says both what it buys and what it costs, because "we cache the policy locally" with no mention of staleness is the kind of line an interviewer probes.

---

> - Owner
>   - Uses a short atomic operation for refill and consume.
>   - Redis scripting provides local execution atomicity.
>   - Script execution occupies the shard, so avoid unbounded loops.
>   - Cost: one serial owner per bucket caps the hottest tenant at one shard's throughput.

**What this says in plain words**

1. **A short atomic operation does refill and consume together.**
   - One indivisible step. Nothing can run between the refill and the spend.
2. **Redis scripting provides that atomicity locally.**
   - Redis runs a submitted script to completion before handling any other command. That is what makes the compound update safe.
   - The word *local* is the defeater: the guarantee covers one server executing one script. It does not extend across a failover or to another shard.
3. **A running script occupies the shard.**
   - Because Redis runs one thing at a time, a script that loops for 50 milliseconds blocks every other tenant's decision on that shard for 50 milliseconds.
   - Worked example: a shard serving 25,000 operations per second is blocked for 50 milliseconds. That is roughly 1,250 decisions queued behind one bad script, and the 5-millisecond deadline means most of them will time out and take their degradation path.
   - The rule that follows: the script must be a fixed, short sequence of arithmetic. No loops over collections, no scans.
4. **The cost: the hot-tenant ceiling.**
   - Restated from the capacity section, because it belongs next to the component that causes it.

---

> - Policy API
>   - A control-plane resource with `If-Match` versioning and an audit trail.
>   - Cost: propagation is asynchronous, so an edit is not fleet-wide until every gateway acknowledges the version.

**What this says in plain words**

1. **`If-Match` versioning.**
   - When an administrator saves a change, they send the version number they were looking at. The server applies the change only if that is still the current version.
   - Worked example of the problem it solves: two administrators open the same limit at 14:00. One raises the rate to 2,000 and saves. The other, still looking at the old page, lowers the burst and saves at 14:01. Without version checking, the second save silently overwrites the first, and the rate is back to 1,000 with nobody aware. With `If-Match`, the second save is rejected with status `412`, forcing a reload.
2. **An audit trail.**
   - Every change records who did it, when, and what the version went from and to.
   - Worked example: a tenant's limit was raised 50× at 03:00 and the backend fell over at 03:05. The audit trail is how you find out whether that was an operator, an automated system, or a compromised credential.
3. **The cost: propagation is asynchronous.**
   - The API returning `200 OK` means the change is durably stored, not that it is being enforced everywhere.
   - The honest statement is "saved, and in force fleet-wide within the propagation bound", which is why the requirement table carries a 10-second number.

---

> - Backend
>   - Enforces its own concurrency and queue limits.
>   - A rate limiter alone cannot contain long-running requests.
>   - Cost: two admission points must agree on which one owns overload rejection.

**What this says in plain words**

1. **The backend has its own limits.**
   - A cap on how many requests can be in flight simultaneously, and a cap on how deep its queue gets.
2. **The rate limiter cannot do this job.**
   - The limiter counts starts, not duration.
   - Worked example: the limit is 10 per second and each request runs a 60-second report. After 60 seconds there are up to 600 reports running concurrently. Every single one was within the rate limit. The backend is dead.
3. **The cost: two admission points need a clear division.**
   - Now two components can reject a request, and the client sees different errors depending on which one fired.
   - Worked example of the confusion: the limiter returns `429` meaning "you are over your quota, slow down". The backend returns `503` meaning "I am overloaded, this is my problem not yours". If the backend also returns `429`, the client's dashboard shows a quota violation that never happened, and the customer opens a support ticket about a quota they never exceeded.
   - The rule: rate limiting owns per-tenant fairness, backend admission owns total overload, and they use different status codes.

---

> → Authenticate and derive `(tenant, route class)`.  
> → Read the active policy version from the local snapshot.  
> → Route the decision to the bucket owner.  
> → Atomically refill and conditionally consume.  
> → Allow the request or return `429` with a retry hint.  
> → On an owner timeout, apply the rule's explicit degradation policy.

**What this says in plain words**
One request traced end to end. Six steps. Walked with a concrete request:

A customer's application sends `GET /v1/items/98432` with a valid credential for tenant `acme`.

1. **Authenticate and derive the key.**
   - The credential verifies as tenant `acme`.
   - The URL normalizes to route class `items-read`.
   - The bucket key is `bucket:acme-items-read`.
2. **Read the active policy version from the local snapshot.**
   - The gateway looks in its own memory, not over the network.
   - It finds rate 1,000 per second, burst 2,000, failure policy fail-open-bounded, policy version 47.
   - Cost so far: microseconds, because nothing left the process.
3. **Route the decision to the bucket owner.**
   - Hash `bucket:acme-items-read` to find its shard, say shard 2.
   - Send the atomic operation to shard 2 with a 5-millisecond deadline.
4. **Atomically refill and conditionally consume.**
   - The shard computes how much time has passed since the last refill, adds that many tokens up to the ceiling, and then spends 1 if at least 1 is available.
   - Worked example: 40 milliseconds elapsed at 1,000 tokens per second adds 40 tokens. The bucket had 12, so it now has 52, spends 1, and stores 51.
5. **Allow, or return `429` with a retry hint.**
   - Allowed: the request continues to the backend.
   - Denied: the gateway returns `429` and a `Retry-After` computed from how long it takes to refill enough tokens.
   - Worked example of the hint: 0 tokens, cost 1, rate 1,000 per second, so the wait is 1 millisecond, and a hint of 1 second is the practical floor most clients honor.
6. **On an owner timeout, apply the degradation policy.**
   - No answer within 5 milliseconds.
   - The policy for this rule says fail-open-bounded, so the request is allowed, tagged as a degraded admission, and counted toward the 1% alarm.
   - Had the rule been a login rule marked fail-closed, the same timeout would produce a rejection.

**Why it matters**
The trace is what proves the diagram is a real system rather than a set of boxes.
It is also the safest thing to fall back on if you lose your place: walking one request forward always produces correct, relevant content.

## API and data model — IC3 baseline

Four parts: the interface table, the state table, the reference SQL, and the rules about bucket keys and expiry.

---

> | Surface | Contract | Concurrency or error behavior |
> |---|---|---|
> | `check(tenant, route, cost, request_id)` | Internal decision: allowed, remaining, retry delay, policy version | Validate `0 < cost <= B`; do not retry an ambiguous consume blindly |
> | `PUT /v1/tenants/{t}/limits/{route}` | Admin updates refill rate, burst, failure policy | `If-Match` rejects stale edits with `412` |
> | `GET /v1/tenants/{t}/limits` | Paginated policy listing | Tenant authorization on every page |
> | `POST /v1/tenants/{t}/limits/{route}:reset` | Privileged, audited quota reset | Idempotency key; a duplicate returns the first reset record |
> | Rejected application request | `429` and `Retry-After` in seconds | Remaining tokens are an observation, not a reservation |
> | Decision infrastructure unavailable | `503` for strict rules | Keep distinct from a quota rejection |

**What this says in plain words**
Six interfaces. The first is internal and runs 100,000 times per second. The next three are administrative. The last two are what the end client sees.

- **Row: `check(tenant, route, cost, request_id)`**
  - The internal decision call, made by the gateway on every request.
  - *cost* exists because not all requests are equal. A bulk export might cost 50 tokens while a single read costs 1.
  - *request_id* is the identifier that makes a retry safely detectable.
  - It returns four things: allowed or not, how many tokens remain, how long to wait if denied, and which policy version was applied.
  - Returning the policy version is what lets you debug "why was this rejected" three hours later.
  - **Validate `0 < cost <= B`.** Two separate checks:
    - `cost > 0` stops a zero or negative cost, which would spend nothing or *add* tokens.
    - `cost <= B` stops a request that can never succeed. Worked example: burst is 2,000 and a request costs 5,000. No amount of waiting will ever make that pass, so rejecting immediately with a clear error beats a retry hint that would be a lie.
  - **Do not retry an ambiguous consume blindly.** Covered in depth in the algorithm section.
- **Row: `PUT /v1/tenants/{t}/limits/{route}`**
  - Creates or replaces one rule. `PUT` means "make it look exactly like this", which is naturally repeatable.
  - Guarded by `If-Match` so a stale edit is rejected with `412` instead of silently clobbering a newer one.
- **Row: `GET /v1/tenants/{t}/limits`**
  - Lists a tenant's rules, in pages.
  - "Tenant authorization on every page" is the important part. Checking authorization only on the first page and then trusting an opaque cursor is a real and common cross-tenant data leak.
  - Worked example of the bug: page 1 is authorized for tenant `acme` and returns a cursor. An attacker edits the cursor to point into tenant `globex` rows. If page 2 does not re-check, it returns another tenant's configuration.
- **Row: `POST .../limits/{route}:reset`**
  - Refills a tenant's bucket immediately. Used when support agrees a customer was wrongly throttled.
  - Privileged and audited, because it is an override of the protection the system exists to provide.
  - **Idempotency key**: the caller supplies a unique string. If the same key arrives twice, the second call returns the first call's result rather than performing a second reset.
  - Worked example: a support tool times out and the agent clicks reset again. Without an idempotency key the bucket is refilled twice, which lets the tenant burst twice. With one, the second click returns the original record.
- **Row: Rejected application request**
  - The client gets `429` plus `Retry-After` in seconds.
  - **"Remaining tokens are an observation, not a reservation."** This is the subtle line.
    - The response says 40 tokens remain. That was true at the instant of the decision.
    - It is not a promise that 40 are still yours.
    - Worked example: the tenant runs 20 client processes. Each receives "40 remaining" within the same millisecond. If each concluded it could safely send 40, they would collectively send 800 against 40 available. The number is a hint for backoff, never a ticket to spend.
- **Row: Decision infrastructure unavailable**
  - `503` for rules marked strict, kept deliberately distinct from `429`.
  - Worked example of merging them: a client that sees `429` believes it exceeded its quota and backs off, possibly alerting its own operators about a quota problem that does not exist. `503` says "my side is broken, retry", which is the true statement and produces the right client behavior.

---

> | State | Key | Fields and invariant |
> |---|---|---|
> | Durable policy | `(tenant_id, route_class)` | `rate, capacity, failure_policy, version`; positive rate and capacity |
> | Ephemeral bucket | `bucket:{tenant-route}` | `tokens, last_refill, applied_version`; owner updates together |
> | Optional decision replay | `bucket:{tenant-route}:req:{request_id}` | Prior decision; TTL equals the documented retry window |
> | Audit event | `event_id` | Actor, old/new policy versions, timestamp; no credentials |

**What this says in plain words**
Four kinds of state, with different durability requirements. That difference is the design decision.

- **Row: Durable policy.**
  - The configured limits. Must survive everything; losing it means losing every customer's configuration.
  - Lives in a relational database.
- **Row: Ephemeral bucket.**
  - The live counter. Losing it is survivable.
  - Worked example of why: if a bucket is lost, it is recreated full. The tenant gets one extra burst of at most `B`. That is a bounded, acceptable cost, and paying for durable storage on a value that changes 100,000 times per second would be enormously more expensive.
  - "owner updates together" means tokens, last refill time, and applied version are written as one atomic action. Writing tokens without the timestamp would cause the next refill to compute elapsed time from a stale point and mint free tokens.
- **Row: Optional decision replay.**
  - A short-lived record of "request 7f3a was already decided, and the answer was allow".
  - *Optional* because it costs an extra write on every request and is only needed where a retry contract demands exactly-once debiting.
  - **TTL equals the documented retry window.** TTL is how long the record lives before it is deleted automatically.
  - Worked example of getting the TTL wrong: the record expires after 10 seconds, but the client library retries for up to 30 seconds. A retry at 25 seconds finds no record and is charged a second time. The TTL must cover the longest retry the contract supports.
- **Row: Audit event.**
  - Who changed what, when. Keyed by its own event identifier.
  - **"no credentials"** is a rule, not an observation. Audit logs are read widely, retained for years, and shipped to analysis systems. A credential written into one has to be treated as compromised.

---

> - Reference DDL
>   - Keys and uniqueness match the state table above and the [companion schema](../../1sde/1.backend/2.rest102.md#infra-1).
>   - Bucket counters and decision replay entries live in Redis; only policy, reset, and audit rows are relational.

**What this says in plain words**
The SQL below covers only the three durable, relational pieces.
The two Redis pieces are named explicitly so the reader does not think they were forgotten.

**Why it matters**
Saying out loud which state is *not* in the database is itself a design statement. It shows the split between durable configuration and ephemeral counters was a choice.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE limit_policies (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, route_class text NOT NULL,
>   rate numeric NOT NULL CHECK (rate > 0),                      -- tokens per second
>   capacity numeric NOT NULL CHECK (capacity > 0),              -- burst B; check() validates 0 < cost <= B
>   failure_policy text NOT NULL
>     CHECK (failure_policy IN ('FAIL_OPEN_BOUNDED','FAIL_CLOSED')),
>   fail_open_allowance numeric CHECK (fail_open_allowance >= 0), -- local allowance while the owner is unreachable
>   version bigint NOT NULL CHECK (version > 0),                 -- If-Match validator; copied to the bucket as applied_version
>   updated_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, route_class),
>   CHECK ((failure_policy = 'FAIL_CLOSED') = (fail_open_allowance IS NULL))
> );
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
One row per rule. One rule per tenant per route class.

- **`PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, route_class)`**
  - The tenant comes first in the key, which is what makes every access naturally tenant-scoped.
  - It also enforces that a tenant cannot have two conflicting rules for the same route class.
- **`CHECK (rate > 0)` and `CHECK (capacity > 0)`**
  - A rate of 0 means the bucket never refills, so the tenant is permanently blocked once drained. If you want to block a tenant, that should be an explicit disable, not a zero rate that looks like a typo.
  - A capacity of 0 means no request can ever pass, since every cost exceeds the ceiling.
- **`failure_policy` restricted to two values**
  - The database itself refuses any other string. A typo like `FAIL_OPEN` is rejected at write time rather than becoming an unknown value that the gateway has to guess about at 3 a.m. during an outage.
- **`fail_open_allowance`**
  - How much traffic a gateway may admit locally while the owner is unreachable. This is what makes fail-open *bounded* rather than unlimited.
- **`version bigint CHECK (version > 0)`**
  - The number used by `If-Match`, and the same number copied into the bucket as `applied_version`.
  - That copy is what lets you answer "which policy version actually made this decision?".
- **The final `CHECK ((failure_policy = 'FAIL_CLOSED') = (fail_open_allowance IS NULL))`**
  - This one is worth reading slowly. It says the two sides must agree.
  - If the policy is `FAIL_CLOSED`, the allowance must be empty, because a closed rule admits nothing and an allowance would be meaningless or, worse, quietly honored.
  - If the policy is `FAIL_OPEN_BOUNDED`, the allowance must be present, because fail-open without a bound is unlimited fail-open, which the requirements forbid.
  - Worked example of what it prevents: a row with `FAIL_OPEN_BOUNDED` and a null allowance. The gateway reads null, treats it as no limit, and admits unlimited traffic during an owner outage. The constraint makes that row impossible to write.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE limit_resets (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, route_class text NOT NULL, idempotency_key text NOT NULL,
>   reset_id text NOT NULL, actor_id text NOT NULL, reason text NOT NULL,
>   policy_version bigint NOT NULL, created_at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, route_class, idempotency_key),       -- a duplicate returns the first reset record
>   UNIQUE (tenant_id, reset_id)
> );                                                             -- no FK: reset history outlives a deleted policy
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
One row per quota reset performed.

- **`PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, route_class, idempotency_key)`**
  - This is the mechanism that makes the reset endpoint safe to call twice.
  - The second insert with the same key violates the primary key, the service catches that, looks up the existing row, and returns it.
  - The important property: the uniqueness is enforced by the database, not by application logic. An application-level "check then insert" has a window between the check and the insert where a concurrent duplicate slips through.
- **`UNIQUE (tenant_id, reset_id)`**
  - A separate, caller-facing identifier for the reset, unique within the tenant, so it can be quoted in a support ticket.
- **`reason text NOT NULL`**
  - A reset cannot be recorded without a written justification. Making it `NOT NULL` forces the tool that calls the endpoint to collect one.
- **`no FK: reset history outlives a deleted policy`**
  - There is deliberately no foreign key to `limit_policies`.
  - Worked example: a tenant's rule is deleted in March. In June an auditor asks who reset that tenant's quota in February. With a foreign key and cascading delete, the evidence is gone. Without one, the history survives the configuration it refers to.

---

> ```sql
> CREATE TABLE limit_audit_events (
>   tenant_id text NOT NULL, event_id text NOT NULL, route_class text NOT NULL,
>   actor_id text NOT NULL,
>   action text NOT NULL CHECK (action IN ('POLICY_PUT','POLICY_DELETE','RESET')),
>   old_version bigint, new_version bigint,                      -- versions only; no credentials or request bodies
>   at timestamptz NOT NULL,
>   PRIMARY KEY (tenant_id, event_id)
> );
> CREATE INDEX limit_audit_by_route ON limit_audit_events (tenant_id, route_class, at, event_id);
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
One row per administrative action.

- **`action` restricted to three values.**
  - Any future action type requires a schema change, which is a feature: it means the set of auditable operations is explicit and reviewed.
- **`old_version` and `new_version` are nullable.**
  - A creation has no old version. A deletion has no new version. Both are legitimately null.
- **`-- versions only; no credentials or request bodies`**
  - Storing the full request body would mean storing whatever the administrator submitted, which can include secrets. Version numbers answer "what changed" without that risk.
- **`CREATE INDEX limit_audit_by_route ON (tenant_id, route_class, at, event_id)`**
  - This index exists to serve one specific question: "show me everything that happened to this tenant's rule for this route, newest first".
  - The column order matters and is not arbitrary.
    - `tenant_id` first narrows to one tenant.
    - `route_class` next narrows to one rule.
    - `at` orders by time within that rule, so the database can walk the index in order instead of sorting.
    - `event_id` last makes the index entry unique, giving a stable tiebreak for two events in the same instant and a usable pagination cursor.
  - Worked example of omitting it: with 50,000,000 audit rows and no index, answering that question scans the whole table. The support tool times out, and someone concludes the audit log is broken.

---

> - Not captured by the DDL
>   - `bucket:{tenant-route}` holds `tokens, last_refill, applied_version` in Redis under one owner operation; the SQL row is the source `version` it applies.
>   - `bucket:{tenant-route}:req:{request_id}` is a Redis replay entry with the retry-window TTL; it never reaches SQL.
>   - Paginated `GET /limits` walks the `(tenant_id, route_class)` primary key as its cursor; no extra index is needed.

**What this says in plain words**
Three clarifications about what is deliberately absent.

1. The live counter is in Redis, and the database row supplies the version number it enforces.
2. The replay record is in Redis with an expiry, and never reaches SQL. It would be an enormous write volume for data that is worthless after 30 seconds.
3. Listing a tenant's limits needs no additional index, because the primary key `(tenant_id, route_class)` is already sorted the right way and can be walked as a cursor.
   - Worked example: page 1 returns rules up to `route_class = 'items-read'`. Page 2 asks for `tenant_id = 'acme' AND route_class > 'items-read'`, which the primary key serves directly.

---

> - Deviation from the companion
>   - Companion `scope` is `route_class`, `rate_per_second` is `rate`, `burst` is `capacity`.
>   - Companion `mode ENFORCE/SHADOW` is replaced by `failure_policy`, which is the knob this file's requirements name; shadow mode is an IC4 rollout option, not a baseline column.
>   - `limit_resets` and `limit_audit_events` are added so the reset endpoint's idempotency and the audit row in the state table have a home.

**What this says in plain words**
There is a shared schema file elsewhere in the repository. This file's schema differs from it, and the differences are listed rather than hidden.

1. Three columns are simply renamed.
2. The shared schema has a `mode` column for enforce-versus-shadow. This file drops it, because shadow mode is a rollout technique discussed in the stretch section, not something the baseline requirements need.
3. Two tables are added so that the idempotency and audit rows promised in the state table actually exist somewhere.

**Why it matters**
An interviewer rarely sees this, but the discipline matters: when two documents disagree, say which one wins and why, rather than leaving a future reader to discover the conflict.

---

> - Use a stable bucket key across policy edits.
>   - A new version must not silently create a second full bucket.
>   - On update, clamp existing tokens to the new capacity under the owner operation.
>   - Refill up to the change boundary using the old rate when that boundary is available.
>   - Document bounded transition behavior if gateways observe updates at different times.

**What this says in plain words**
Four rules about what happens to a live bucket when its policy is edited.

1. **The key must not change when the policy changes.**
   - Worked example of the bug: the key is `bucket:acme-items-read:v47`. An edit makes it version 48, so the key becomes `bucket:acme-items-read:v48`, which does not exist yet and is therefore created full. A tenant that had drained its bucket gets a complete free burst on every edit, and an administrator who saves the form ten times hands out ten bursts.
2. **Clamp tokens to the new capacity, inside the atomic operation.**
   - Worked example: 1,000 tokens held, capacity lowered to 100. Clamp to 100 immediately. Doing this outside the atomic operation opens a window where a concurrent request sees 1,000 under a ceiling of 100.
3. **Refill up to the change boundary using the old rate.**
   - The tokens earned before the edit were earned under the old rate, and should be computed at that rate.
   - Worked example: the rate goes from 10 to 100 per second at 14:00:00, and the bucket was last refilled at 13:59:58. The honest computation gives 2 seconds × 10 = 20 tokens for the pre-change period, then the new rate afterwards. Applying the new rate to the whole gap would grant 200 tokens for time the tenant was only entitled to 20.
   - "when that boundary is available" is the hedge: the bucket may not know exactly when the edit landed, in which case you accept a small bounded error and say so.
4. **Document the bounded transition.**
   - Because gateways adopt the new version at different moments, there is a window with mixed enforcement. Write the bound down instead of pretending it does not exist.

---

> - Idle expiration
>   - Evict only after enough idle time for a full refill.
>   - Recreating a bucket full is then consistent with its prior idle state.
>   - Do not expire depleted state earlier just to save memory.

**What this says in plain words**
When you delete an unused bucket to reclaim memory, the timing must be chosen so that deletion is invisible.

1. **Wait at least long enough for a full refill.**
   - Worked example: capacity 2,000, rate 1,000 per second. A full refill from empty takes 2 seconds. So after 2 seconds of silence the bucket would be full anyway.
2. **Then recreating it full is correct, not a gift.**
   - Deleting it and recreating it full produces the same state the tenant would have had. The eviction is a pure memory optimization with no behavioral effect.
3. **Never expire a depleted bucket early.**
   - Worked example of the bug: capacity 2,000, and buckets are evicted after 100 milliseconds of idleness to save memory. A tenant drains its bucket to 0, pauses for 150 milliseconds, and the bucket is deleted. Its next request recreates a full bucket with 2,000 tokens. Repeating that pause turns a 1,000-per-second limit into an unlimited one, and the memory saved was about 200 bytes.

**Why it matters**
This is the pattern to notice: a small operational optimization silently destroys the correctness property the entire system exists to provide. The capacity section already established that memory is not the constraint, which is exactly why this trade is a bad one.

## Atomic algorithm and races — IC3 baseline

This is the deep dive, the 15-minute block and the section that produces the level signal.
Six parts: the pseudocode, where the decision actually happens, what atomicity does not cover, clocks, the last-token race, the ambiguous timeout, and multi-bucket debits.

---

> ```text
> atomic CHECK(bucket, policy, now, cost):
>     require 0 < cost <= capacity and rate > 0
>     reject invalid or permanently unsatisfiable requests without a retry estimate
>     load tokens and last_refill, or initialize a full bucket
>     elapsed = max(0, now - last_refill)
>     tokens = min(capacity, tokens + elapsed * rate)
>     last_refill = max(last_refill, now)
>     if tokens < cost:
>         store refreshed state
>         return DENY, ceil((cost - tokens) / rate)
>     tokens = tokens - cost
>     store refreshed state
>     return ALLOW, tokens
> ```

**What this says in plain words**
The whole algorithm. It is about ten lines, which is exactly the point the 🗣 one-liner made. Line by line:

1. **`require 0 < cost <= capacity and rate > 0`**
   - Reject nonsense before touching state. A cost of 0 or below, a cost larger than the ceiling, or a non-positive rate.
2. **`reject invalid or permanently unsatisfiable requests without a retry estimate`**
   - "Permanently unsatisfiable" means it can never succeed, no matter how long you wait.
   - Worked example: capacity 2,000, cost 5,000. The bucket maxes out at 2,000, so waiting is pointless. Returning a `Retry-After` here would be a lie that makes the client retry forever.
3. **`load tokens and last_refill, or initialize a full bucket`**
   - Read the two stored numbers. If the bucket does not exist, create it full.
   - Starting full rather than empty is the right default: a new tenant, or one whose bucket was evicted after a long idle period, should be entitled to its full burst.
4. **`elapsed = max(0, now - last_refill)`**
   - Time since the last refill, floored at zero.
   - The `max(0, …)` is not decoration. If the clock moved backwards, `now - last_refill` is negative, and a negative elapsed time would *subtract* tokens, corrupting the count in a way nothing else would catch.
5. **`tokens = min(capacity, tokens + elapsed * rate)`**
   - Add the earned tokens, then clamp to the ceiling.
   - The clamp is what makes a burst finite. Without it, a tenant idle for an hour at 1,000 per second would accumulate 3,600,000 tokens and could obliterate the backend in one second.
   - Worked example: tokens 12, elapsed 0.04 seconds, rate 1,000 per second, capacity 2,000. 12 + 40 = 52, which is under 2,000, so tokens become 52.
6. **`last_refill = max(last_refill, now)`**
   - Move the refill timestamp forward, but never backwards.
   - Worked example of why: the clock jumps back 5 seconds. If you stored the earlier time, the *next* request would compute 5 seconds of elapsed time and mint 5,000 free tokens. Clamping stores the later value, so the backward jump costs a little refill rather than granting a flood.
7. **`if tokens < cost:` → store, return DENY and a wait estimate**
   - Note the state is stored even on denial. The refill that just happened is real and must be persisted, otherwise it is recomputed from the same stale timestamp on the next call.
   - `ceil((cost - tokens) / rate)` is how long until enough tokens exist.
   - Worked example: cost 1, tokens 0.3, rate 1,000 per second. (1 − 0.3) / 1000 = 0.0007 seconds, rounded up.
8. **`tokens = tokens - cost` → store, return ALLOW**
   - Spend and persist.

**Why it matters**
Every line except the last two is defensive.
The actual budget logic is two lines. The other eight exist because clocks lie, buckets vanish, and callers send nonsense. That ratio is the real lesson of the section.

---

> - Linearization point
>   - The owner's conditional debit determines the decision.
>   - `GET` followed by client-side `SET` loses updates under concurrency.

**What this says in plain words**
A linearization point is the single instant at which an operation takes effect, so that all concurrent operations can be placed in one clean order.

1. **Here it is the conditional debit inside the script.**
   - The moment the owner checks `tokens >= cost` and subtracts is the moment the decision becomes true. Everything before is preparation; everything after is reporting.
2. **Read-then-write from the client is broken.**
   - Worked example of the lost update, step by step, with the bucket holding 1 token:
     - Gateway A reads tokens = 1.
     - Gateway B reads tokens = 1.
     - Gateway A decides 1 ≥ 1, allows, writes tokens = 0.
     - Gateway B decides 1 ≥ 1, allows, writes tokens = 0.
   - Two requests were admitted against one token. The stored value is 0, so nothing looks wrong afterwards. There is no error and no log line.
   - At 100,000 requests per second this is not a rare race; it is the normal case whenever a bucket is near empty, which is precisely when the limit matters.

**Why it matters**
This is the crispest possible answer to "why not just read the counter and write it back?".
Having the phrase *linearization point* and a four-line interleaving ready is the difference between asserting the design is correct and demonstrating it.

---

> - Consistency boundary
>   - Atomicity holds within one owner's script execution.
>   - It does not extend across shards, across a failover, or across regions.

**What this says in plain words**
The atomicity guarantee has an edge, and the edge is drawn at one server executing one script.

Three things it does not cover:

1. **Across shards.**
   - A script on shard 2 and a script on shard 5 are unrelated. There is no combined all-or-nothing behavior.
2. **Across a failover.**
   - The replica may not have received the last few writes. The guarantee is about the current owner, and the owner just changed.
3. **Across regions.**
   - Each region has its own owners and its own counters. Nothing coordinates them.

**Why it matters**
Almost every wrong answer to this question comes from assuming atomic means globally correct.

- Worked example: "Redis scripts are atomic, so the limit is exactly enforced." A failover occurs, the replica was 200 milliseconds behind at 1,000 debits per second, and 200 debits vanish. The limit was not exactly enforced, and no script ever behaved incorrectly.
- Stating the boundary yourself, before being asked, is the single strongest signal in this section.

---

> - Clock
>   - Use a monotonic clock for an in-process bucket.
>   - Use an owner-side time source for distributed decisions.
>   - Clamp backward jumps without moving the stored time backward.
>   - A forward jump can grant an early burst up to capacity.
>   - Strict clock guarantees need an explicit clock and failover design.

**What this says in plain words**
Refill depends entirely on elapsed time, so the design has to state which clock it reads and what happens when that clock misbehaves.

1. **Monotonic clock for an in-process bucket.**
   - A monotonic clock only ever moves forward, at a steady rate, and is unaffected by time-synchronization corrections or an administrator setting the date.
   - It cannot tell you the wall-clock time, which is fine, because refill needs *elapsed* time, not the date.
   - Worked example of using wall-clock time instead: a time-synchronization daemon steps the clock back 2 seconds. `now - last_refill` goes negative and the arithmetic breaks.
2. **Owner-side time source for distributed decisions.**
   - The owner reads its own clock. Gateways never send their timestamp.
   - Worked example of trusting the caller: a gateway with a clock 60 seconds fast sends `now = T+60`. The owner computes 60 seconds of elapsed time and mints 60,000 tokens. One misconfigured machine disables the limiter for everyone whose buckets it touches.
   - It also means all decisions for one bucket are ordered by one clock, which is the only way the ordering is meaningful.
3. **Clamp backward jumps without moving the stored time backwards.**
   - Both halves of the rule are in the pseudocode: `max(0, …)` for elapsed and `max(last_refill, now)` for storage.
4. **A forward jump grants an early burst, bounded by capacity.**
   - Worked example: the clock jumps forward 1 hour. Elapsed is 3,600 seconds, which at 1,000 per second is 3,600,000 tokens, immediately clamped to the capacity of 2,000.
   - So the damage from a forward jump is at most one full burst per bucket, not unlimited traffic. The clamp in line 5 is what bounds it.
   - This is a good thing to volunteer: it shows you traced a failure through the code and found where it is contained.
5. **Strict clock guarantees need an explicit design.**
   - If someone truly needs "never more than `r` per second regardless of clock behavior", you need bounded clock error and a failover story, which is a different and much more expensive system.

---

> - Same bucket example
>   - `B=20`, `r=10/s`, and `tokens=0`.
>   - After `0.5 s`, five unit-cost requests may pass.
>   - A sixth concurrent request must observe the prior debits and fail.

**What this says in plain words**
A worked example supplied by the source itself.

- Capacity 20, rate 10 per second, currently empty.
- After 0.5 seconds, the refill is 0.5 × 10 = 5 tokens.
- Five requests costing 1 each drain those 5.
- A sixth request arriving in the same instant must see 0 and be denied.

**Why it matters**
The word *concurrent* is the entire content of the example.
All six can arrive within the same microsecond, from six different gateways. The sixth must still observe the effect of the first five.

- That is only true if the six are serialized at the owner, which is what the whole architecture exists to provide.
- Under the read-then-write design, all six would read 5 and all six would pass, which is 6 admitted against 5 earned.
- This example is worth memorizing verbatim. It is small enough to draw on a whiteboard in fifteen seconds and it demonstrates the correctness property exactly.

---

> - Ambiguous timeout
>   - At `5 ms` the gateway stops waiting
>   - The script may or may not have run.
>   - A blind retry can debit twice
>   - A blind allow can over-admit.
>   - Baseline: apply the declared degraded policy after an ambiguous timeout.
>   - Optional replay protection stores request ID, payload hash, debit, and result atomically with the bucket update.
>   - Its TTL must cover the supported retry horizon and its durability follows the same failover limits.

**What this says in plain words**
The hardest case in the design, and the one interviewers push on.

1. **At 5 milliseconds the gateway gives up.**
   - It has no answer.
2. **It cannot know what happened.**
   - Three possibilities, indistinguishable from outside:
     - The request never reached the owner. No debit occurred.
     - The request arrived, the script ran, the reply was lost. A debit occurred.
     - The request arrived and the script is still queued behind a slow shard. A debit is about to occur.
3. **A blind retry can double-charge.**
   - Worked example: the script ran and spent 1 token. The gateway retries. Another token is spent. One user request consumed 2 tokens. At scale a tenant is throttled at 50% of its actual quota and files a support ticket that nobody can reproduce.
4. **A blind allow can over-admit.**
   - Worked example: the gateway gives up and allows anyway. During a 30-second owner outage at 100,000 requests per second, that is 3,000,000 unchecked admissions against a backend sized for far less. The limiter's outage becomes the backend's outage.
5. **Baseline answer: apply the declared degradation policy.**
   - Do not invent a decision at the moment of failure. The policy row already said what to do for this rule: bounded fail-open, or fail-closed.
   - This is why `failure_policy` is a column in the schema rather than a code branch.
6. **Optional replay protection.**
   - Store, atomically with the bucket update, a record of: the request identifier, a hash of the payload, the debit applied, and the result.
   - The word *atomically* is the crux. If the replay record were written separately from the debit, a crash between the two writes recreates exactly the ambiguity you were trying to remove.
   - The payload hash guards against a client reusing an identifier for a different request. Worked example: the same request ID arrives with a different body; the hash differs, so this is a new request, not a retry, and must not receive the cached answer.
7. **TTL and durability caveats.**
   - The record must outlive the longest retry the contract supports, as covered in the state table.
   - Its durability is no better than the bucket's: a failover can lose the replay record too, and then the retry is charged again. Exactly-once here is best-effort within one owner's lifetime, and saying so is more valuable than claiming exactly-once.

**Why it matters**
This is where an IC4 signal is available for free.
Most candidates answer "retry it". The distinguishing answer is "I cannot tell whether the debit happened, so the behavior must be declared in advance per rule, and if a caller needs exactly-once I add an atomic replay record whose TTL covers the retry horizon, while being clear it does not survive failover."

---

> - Multiple constraints
>   - One script can atomically check and debit colocated route and tenant buckets.
>   - Redis Cluster multi-key operations require a shared hash slot.
>   - All script-accessed keys must be declared as keys to the script.
>   - Independent shards cannot provide an all-or-nothing multi-bucket debit without coordination.
>   - Rejecting after one conservative debit wastes budget
>   - It does not inherently over-admit.

**What this says in plain words**
Real systems apply several limits to one request: a per-tenant limit *and* a per-route limit, for example. Both must pass, and the request must not consume budget from one if the other rejects.

1. **One script can do several buckets, if they live together.**
   - Atomicity covers everything inside one script execution on one server, so two buckets on the same server can be checked and debited all-or-nothing.
2. **Redis Cluster requires a shared hash slot.**
   - Redis Cluster assigns each key to one of 16,384 slots by hashing the key name, and a multi-key operation is only allowed when every key is in the same slot.
   - The standard technique is a hash tag: only the part of the key inside braces is hashed. Keys `bucket:{acme}:items-read` and `bucket:{acme}:orders-write` both hash on `acme` and therefore land in the same slot, on the same node.
   - This also explains the isolation rule from the failure section: keeping one tenant's buckets together means a hot tenant's blast radius is one shard.
3. **All keys must be declared to the script.**
   - Redis requires the script to state up front which keys it will touch, so the cluster can verify they are all on the node being asked.
   - A script that computes a key name internally and reads it may work on a single node and fail in a cluster. Worked example: it passes every test in a single-node development environment and fails on the first production deploy.
4. **Independent shards cannot do all-or-nothing.**
   - If the tenant bucket is on shard 2 and the route bucket on shard 5, there is no way to debit both or neither without a coordination protocol such as two-phase commit, which costs more latency than the entire 2-millisecond budget.
5. **The fallback: debit conservatively, one at a time, and accept waste.**
   - Worked example: debit the tenant bucket (succeeds), then check the route bucket (fails). You reject the request, but the tenant bucket already lost a token.
   - The cost is wasted budget: the tenant is charged for a request that never ran.
   - The critical property is stated in the last line: this **does not over-admit**. Wasting budget makes the limiter slightly stricter than intended, which is the safe direction to err. Over-admitting would be the unsafe direction.
   - Order the checks cheapest-and-most-likely-to-fail first to minimize the waste.

**Why it matters**
The final line is the reason this is an acceptable design rather than a bug.
When you must break a guarantee, break it toward being too strict. "It wastes budget but never over-admits" is a sentence that ends the discussion.

## Failure, operations, and security

A nine-row failure table, then five topics: the durability caveat, bounded emergency mode, what to measure, retry discipline, tenant isolation, and rollout safety.

---

> | Failure | Blast radius | Detection | Degraded behavior | Recovery |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Owner times out after consuming | One uncertain decision | Timeout counter | Strict rule returns `503`; no blind debit retry | Optional short-lived decision-ID dedup for a required retry contract |
> | Redis primary loses recent debits on failover | Buckets on that shard | Failover and admission metrics | Temporary extra budget is possible | Accept explicitly or use a stronger state owner |
> | Owner shard unavailable | That shard's tenants | Error/latency alarms | Capacity rule uses bounded emergency budget; security rule rejects | Warm and validate state before normal admission |
> | Policy DB or policy API unavailable | New policy edits only | Snapshot version lag | Gateways keep deciding on the last-known snapshot | Resume ordered snapshot delivery; reconcile versions |
> | Gateway restarts with an empty snapshot | One gateway's traffic | Missing-policy counter | Serve a bundled default policy; never fail open by omission | Pull the current snapshot before joining the pool |
> | Bad policy rollout | Targeted tenants | Shadow reject-rate delta | Keep last-known valid snapshot | Roll back policy and clamp bucket state |
> | Owner clock jumps forward | One shard's buckets | Refill-rate anomaly | Early burst up to `B` per bucket, then normal | Clamp time source; alert on drift beyond the bound |
> | Hot tenant saturates one shard | Co-located tenants on that shard | Hottest-key load and p99 by shard | Per-bucket rate cap sheds the hot tenant first | Move the hot key or issue disjoint grants |
> | Backend concurrency saturation | One service | In-flight count and queue age | Reject admission even with available tokens | Reduce offered load and shed expensive operations |

**What this says in plain words**
Nine ways the system breaks. For each: how far the damage spreads, how you find out, what the system does meanwhile, and how you get back to normal.

**Why the five columns matter**
"Blast radius" and "Detection" are the two columns candidates skip, and they carry the ownership signal.
Knowing a failure exists is table stakes. Knowing that it affects one shard's tenants rather than the fleet, and naming the metric that reveals it, is what an on-call owner knows.

- **Row: Owner times out after consuming.**
  - Radius: exactly one decision is uncertain. Not a shard, not a tenant.
  - Detection: a counter of timed-out checks.
  - Behavior: a strict rule returns `503`, and under no circumstances is the debit retried blindly.
  - Recovery: add the replay record only where a retry contract genuinely requires it.
- **Row: Redis primary loses recent debits on failover.**
  - Radius: every bucket on that shard, because they all failed over together.
  - Worked example: replication lag 200 milliseconds, shard handling 25,000 debits per second, so about 5,000 debits are lost and handed back as free budget spread across that shard's tenants.
  - Recovery: there is no clever fix. Either accept it explicitly and write it down, or replace the storage with a consensus-backed system that pays latency for durability.
- **Row: Owner shard unavailable.**
  - Radius: the tenants whose buckets hash to that shard, roughly 1/N of the fleet.
  - Behavior splits by rule type, which is exactly why `failure_policy` is per rule.
  - Recovery: "warm and validate state before normal admission" means do not put a shard back into rotation the instant it responds. A shard that is up but holds empty buckets hands every tenant a full burst simultaneously.
- **Row: Policy DB or policy API unavailable.**
  - Radius: only new edits. This is the payoff of the control-plane split.
  - Detection: snapshot version lag, meaning the age of the newest snapshot each gateway holds.
  - Recovery: "reconcile versions" means confirm the fleet converged on one version before letting edits resume, so an edit is not applied on top of divergent state.
- **Row: Gateway restarts with an empty snapshot.**
  - Behavior: serve a bundled default policy, and **never fail open by omission**.
  - This is the most important line in the table. Failing open *by policy* is a decision. Failing open because a lookup returned nothing and the code treated missing as unlimited is an accident that looks identical in production and is invisible in tests.
  - Worked example: a fresh gateway starts with no snapshot, finds no rule for tenant `acme`, and allows everything. It joins the pool and silently becomes an unlimited entry point.
  - Recovery: pull the current snapshot *before* accepting traffic, which is a readiness check, not a liveness check.
- **Row: Bad policy rollout.**
  - Detection: the shadow reject-rate delta, meaning how different the proposed policy's decisions would be from the current one.
  - Worked example: a proposed change would reject 40% of a tenant's traffic where the current rule rejects 0.5%. That delta is visible before enforcing anything.
  - Recovery: roll back the policy *and* clamp bucket state, because a bad capacity value may have left tokens above the corrected ceiling.
- **Row: Owner clock jumps forward.**
  - Detection: a refill-rate anomaly, meaning tokens appearing faster than `rate` allows.
  - Behavior: bounded at one full burst per bucket by the `min(capacity, …)` clamp.
- **Row: Hot tenant saturates one shard.**
  - Radius: the innocent tenants co-located on that shard. This is the noisy-neighbor problem.
  - Detection: hottest-key load, and p99 latency broken out per shard rather than averaged.
  - Behavior: the per-bucket cap sheds the hot tenant's traffic first, so the noisy neighbor absorbs its own consequences.
- **Row: Backend concurrency saturation.**
  - Behavior: reject even when tokens are available.
  - This is the third non-goal made operational. Tokens describe arrival rate; they say nothing about how much work is currently in flight.

---

> - Atomic execution is not durable global correctness.
>   - Redis replication is normally asynchronous.
>   - `WAIT` does not turn the deployment into a strongly consistent CP system.
>   - See [Redis replication](https://redis.io/docs/latest/operate/oss_and_stack/management/replication/).

**What this says in plain words**

1. **Atomic and durable are different properties.**
   - Atomic: the update happened as one indivisible step.
   - Durable: the update survives the machine that made it.
2. **Redis replication is asynchronous by default.**
   - The primary confirms the write to you first and sends it to the replica afterwards. The gap is where debits are lost on failover.
3. **`WAIT` does not fix this.**
   - `WAIT` is a Redis command that blocks until a given number of replicas acknowledge. It narrows the window; it does not close it, because there is no consensus protocol deciding which replica may be promoted.
   - CP here refers to the consistency-and-partition-tolerance side of the CAP theorem: a system that refuses to answer rather than return a stale or divergent result.
   - Worked example of the remaining hole: `WAIT 1 100` returns after one replica acknowledges. If the primary and that replica both fail, or a different, lagging replica is promoted, the acknowledged write is still gone.

**Why it matters**
It is the second half of the consistency-boundary point, aimed at a specific wrong answer an interviewer may offer: "could you use `WAIT` to make it strongly consistent?" The correct reply is that `WAIT` buys a narrower window, not a different guarantee.

---

> - Bounded emergency mode
>   - A fixed gateway membership can divide a separate emergency allowance.
>   - Autoscaling and duplicate membership can violate that sum.
>   - If membership is uncertain, use admission leases or reject strict traffic.
>   - Never describe unrestricted fail-open as backend protection.

**What this says in plain words**
How to fail open without it being unlimited.

1. **Divide an emergency allowance across a known set of gateways.**
   - Worked example: the emergency allowance is 1,000 requests per second and there are 10 gateways, so each may admit 100 per second locally while the owner is unreachable. The total stays at 1,000.
2. **Autoscaling breaks the arithmetic.**
   - Worked example: the outage causes errors, errors trigger autoscaling, the fleet grows from 10 gateways to 40. Each still admits 100 per second, so the total is 4,000 against an allowance of 1,000. The emergency mode amplifies the incident it was supposed to contain.
   - "Duplicate membership" is the same failure from a different cause: a gateway that is being replaced can briefly be counted twice.
3. **If you cannot count the fleet, use leases or reject.**
   - An admission lease is a grant with an expiry that must be obtained centrally, so the total is enforced by issuance rather than by arithmetic over an assumed fleet size.
   - If neither is possible, strict rules reject. That is the honest option.
4. **Never call unrestricted fail-open "protection".**
   - It is the opposite. An unbounded fail-open limiter provides no protection exactly when the backend is least able to cope.

**Why it matters**
This is a premise-checking move. The obvious answer to "what if the limiter is down?" is "let traffic through". This paragraph shows that the obvious answer needs a bound, that the bound depends on knowing the fleet size, and that the fleet size is exactly what becomes unreliable during an incident.

---

> - Observe
>   - Decision latency by shard.
>   - Allowed and rejected cost.
>   - Emergency-mode admissions.
>   - Hottest bucket load.
>   - Policy version lag.
>   - Page on sustained decision failures or backend overload
>   - A high reject count alone may indicate correct enforcement.

**What this says in plain words**
Five metrics, then a rule about which of them wakes someone up.

1. **Decision latency by shard**, not averaged. An average across 8 shards hides one shard at 100%.
2. **Allowed and rejected cost**, measured in tokens rather than request counts, because costs differ.
3. **Emergency-mode admissions**, the metric behind the 1% alarm.
4. **Hottest bucket load**, the early warning for the weakest assumption in the design.
5. **Policy version lag**, the age of the oldest version still in use across the fleet.

**The paging rule, and why it is the interesting part**

- Page on sustained decision *failures*, or on backend overload.
- Do **not** page on a high rejection count by itself.
- Worked example: rejections jump from 100 per second to 50,000 per second. That could be a bug, or it could be one tenant deploying a broken retry loop and the limiter correctly containing it. The second case is the system working perfectly, and paging a human at 3 a.m. for it trains the team to ignore the alert.
- What distinguishes them: rejections concentrated in one tenant with the backend healthy is correct enforcement. Rejections spread across all tenants, or accompanied by rising backend latency, is an incident.

---

> - Retry discipline
>   - Retry a timed-out check at most once, and only with a `request_id` the owner can deduplicate.
>   - Otherwise classify the decision as degraded and apply the rule's policy.
>   - Clients honor `Retry-After` with jitter
>   - A synchronized retry storm is itself an overload.

**What this says in plain words**

1. **At most one retry, and only when deduplication exists.**
   - Two conditions, both required. Without the identifier, a retry is a blind retry and double-charges.
2. **Otherwise call it degraded and apply the policy.**
   - Do not keep trying. Take the declared path and record it.
3. **Honor `Retry-After` with jitter.**
   - Jitter means adding a small random offset so that clients do not all return at the same instant.
   - Worked example without jitter: 10,000 clients are rejected at 14:00:00 and all told to retry after 1 second. At 14:00:01 exactly, 10,000 requests arrive simultaneously. They are rejected again and synchronize harder. The system oscillates between idle and overloaded forever. This is called a thundering herd.
   - With jitter, each client waits 1 second plus a random 0 to 1 second, and the same 10,000 retries spread across a second.

---

> - Tenant isolation
>   - Bucket keys and policies are scoped by tenant
>   - Route-class cardinality is bounded per tenant.
>   - Authorize tenant administration
>   - Audit resets and quota increases.
>   - Keep one tenant's buckets on one shard so a hot tenant's blast radius is that shard, not the fleet.

**What this says in plain words**
Five isolation rules.

1. **Keys and policies are tenant-scoped.** The tenant identifier is part of every key, so cross-tenant access is structurally impossible rather than prevented by a check someone might forget.
2. **Route-class cardinality is bounded per tenant.**
   - Cardinality means how many distinct values exist. There is a cap on how many route classes one tenant can have.
   - Worked example of the attack this stops: a tenant creates 10,000,000 distinct route classes, each minting a bucket. The memory estimate of 10 megabytes becomes 2 gigabytes for one tenant, and the shard is exhausted. Bounding the count converts an unbounded resource into a bounded one.
3. **Authorize tenant administration.** Being authenticated as some tenant does not permit editing another tenant's limits.
4. **Audit resets and increases.** Both are ways to obtain more capacity, so both need a record of who and why.
5. **Keep one tenant's buckets on one shard.**
   - Counter-intuitive at first: spreading them would balance load better.
   - But spreading them means a hot tenant touches every shard, so its blast radius becomes the entire fleet.
   - Worked example: a tenant sending 40,000 requests per second spread across 8 shards degrades all 8 and therefore every other tenant. Concentrated on one shard, it degrades 1/8 of the fleet.
   - This is the bulkhead principle: contain damage within a compartment rather than distributing it evenly.
   - It is implemented with the hash tag from the algorithm section, so the same mechanism serves both multi-key atomicity and blast-radius containment.

---

> - Rollout and rollback safety
>   - Roll out policy through shadow evaluation: compare proposed decisions without consuming the live bucket twice.
>   - Canary a small tenant cohort
>   - Roll back when legitimate rejection or backend saturation breaches the agreed threshold.
>   - A new script version must read the previous bucket layout
>   - Change layout under a new key prefix, not in place.

**What this says in plain words**
Five rules for changing the limiter without causing an incident.

1. **Shadow evaluation.**
   - Run the proposed policy alongside the live one and record what it *would* have decided.
   - "without consuming the live bucket twice" is the trap: a naive implementation runs both rules against the same bucket, so every request is charged twice and the shadow evaluation itself throttles the tenant.
   - The correct implementation evaluates the proposed rule against a separate shadow bucket, or computes the hypothetical decision without writing.
2. **Canary a small tenant cohort.**
   - Apply the change to a few tenants first. Worked example: 50 tenants out of 10,000 is 0.5% of the blast radius while still being enough traffic to see the effect.
3. **Roll back on an agreed threshold.**
   - Two triggers: legitimate requests being rejected, or the backend saturating.
   - "Agreed" means the number is decided before the rollout, not argued about during it.
4. **A new script version must read the old bucket layout.**
   - During a deploy, both versions run at once and touch the same buckets.
   - Worked example of forgetting: the new script expects a field the old one never wrote. It reads null, treats it as zero, and every bucket it touches behaves as empty. Every tenant on that shard is rejected until the deploy completes.
5. **Change layout under a new key prefix.**
   - If the structure genuinely must change, write the new structure under new key names.
   - The old and new coexist, and the old keys expire naturally. This is the standard expand-and-contract migration, and it makes rollback a configuration change instead of a data repair.

| Blast radius | How much of the system one failure affects. | A column in the failure table, and the reason a tenant's buckets are kept on one shard. |
| Asynchronous replication | The primary confirms a write before the replica has it. | The reason a failover loses recent debits. |
| `WAIT` | A Redis command that blocks until N replicas acknowledge. | Narrows the failover loss window but does not close it. |

| Readiness check | A check that decides whether an instance may receive traffic, distinct from whether it is alive. | A gateway must not accept traffic before it has a policy snapshot. |
| Bounded emergency mode | A fail-open path with an explicit total allowance divided across a known fleet. | The only defensible form of fail-open. |
| Admission lease | A centrally issued, expiring permit to admit a bounded amount of traffic. | Replaces fleet-size arithmetic when membership is uncertain. |
| Noisy neighbor | One tenant whose load degrades other tenants sharing the same resource. | What the hot-tenant row and the per-bucket cap address. |
| Thundering herd | Many clients retrying at the same instant and re-creating the overload. | Why `Retry-After` must be honored with jitter. |
| Jitter | A small random offset added to a wait time to desynchronize clients. | Spreads retries across an interval. |
| Cardinality | The number of distinct values a field can take. | Bounded per tenant so bucket creation cannot exhaust memory. |
| Bulkhead | Containing damage inside one compartment instead of spreading it evenly. | Why one tenant's buckets stay on one shard. |
| Shadow evaluation | Computing what a proposed policy would decide, recording it, and enforcing nothing. | How a policy change is validated before it can hurt anyone. |
| Canary | Applying a change to a small subset first and watching it. | Limits the blast radius of a bad rollout. |
| Expand and contract | Adding a new structure alongside the old, migrating, then removing the old. | Why a layout change goes under a new key prefix. |
| Refill-rate anomaly | Tokens appearing faster than the configured rate permits. | The detection signal for a clock jump. |
| Policy version lag | The age or version gap of the oldest snapshot still in use across the fleet. | Detects a stalled control plane. |

## Alternatives and IC4 stretch

The option table first, then the base choice, then four stretch items, then the weakest assumption and the over-engineering warning.

---

> | Option | What it is | Strength | Weakness | Choose when |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Token bucket | Refilled budget | Explicit burst envelope | Not an exact rolling-window count | Default burst-tolerant API protection |
> | Fixed window | Counter per interval | Small state | Boundary permits concentrated traffic | Coarse limits tolerate boundary bursts |
> | Sliding log | Timestamp per admitted event | Exact window membership | State and pruning grow with traffic | Small strict attempt limits |
> | Sliding counter | Weighted adjacent windows | Constant state | Error depends on traffic distribution | Approximation is accepted and measured |
> | Leaky-bucket queue | Scheduled release | Smooth downstream work | Queueing increases latency | Work may wait rather than be rejected |

**What this says in plain words**
The five rate-limiting algorithms, each with the situation it is right for.

- **Row: Token bucket.**
  - Mechanism: a budget that refills continuously up to a ceiling, and each request spends from it.
  - State: two numbers per bucket.
  - Strength: bursts are explicit and bounded. You can state the exact guarantee, `B + r × t`.
  - Weakness: it is not an exact count per window, as established in the invariants.
- **Row: Fixed window.**
  - Mechanism: a counter per calendar interval, reset at the boundary.
  - Strength: one integer, trivially cheap.
  - Weakness, worked in full: the limit is 100 per minute. A client sends 100 at 14:00:59 and another 100 at 14:01:00. Both minutes are within the limit. But a 2-second span saw 200 requests, which is 2× the intended rate at the worst possible moment. This is the boundary burst, and it is why fixed windows are only acceptable for coarse limits.
- **Row: Sliding log.**
  - Mechanism: store the timestamp of every admitted request, count the ones inside the window, discard the rest.
  - Strength: exactly correct. "At most 100 in any 60 seconds measured from any instant" is genuinely true.
  - Weakness: state grows with traffic, and pruning is continuous work.
  - Worked example: at 100,000 per second with a 60-second window, that is 6,000,000 timestamps held and pruned continuously. At 1 login attempt per minute per user, it is 5 timestamps. Same algorithm, completely different verdict.
  - Right choice: small, strict attempt limits such as failed logins.
- **Row: Sliding counter.**
  - Mechanism: keep counters for the current and previous window, and weight the previous one by how much of it still overlaps the current window.
  - Worked example: limit 100 per minute. It is 14:01:15, so 75% of the window lies in 14:01 and 25% in 14:00. The previous minute counted 80, the current has 30. Estimate = 80 × 0.25 + 30 = 50.
  - Strength: constant state, two integers, and no boundary burst.
  - Weakness: the estimate assumes traffic was spread evenly across the previous window. If all 80 arrived in the first second of 14:00, the weighting is wrong.
  - Right choice: when approximation is acceptable *and you measure the error*.
- **Row: Leaky-bucket queue.**
  - Mechanism: admit requests into a queue and release them downstream at a fixed rate.
  - Strength: the downstream service sees perfectly smooth traffic.
  - Weakness: requests wait instead of being rejected, so latency rises with queue depth.
  - Worked example: release rate 10 per second, 50 requests queued. The last one waits 5 seconds. If the client's timeout is 2 seconds, it gave up 3 seconds ago and the work is done for nobody.
  - Right choice: asynchronous work where waiting is genuinely better than rejection, such as a batch job submission.

---

> - Base choice: token bucket.
>   - The deciding variable is whether bursts are part of the contract.

**What this says in plain words**
Pick the token bucket. The single question that decides it is whether the product promises customers they may burst.

**Why it matters**
The source's own rules forbid a neutral survey: a comparison must end in a recommendation and name the deciding variable.

- Worked example of the decision flipping: if the contract is "exactly 5 login attempts per minute, no exceptions", bursts are not part of the contract, the deciding variable points the other way, and a sliding log is correct, precisely because the volume is tiny.

---

> - IC4 stretch — hot tenant
>   - Allocate disjoint token grants to gateways.
>   - Debit a grant centrally before any local spending.
>   - Crashed gateways strand unused tokens.
>   - Reclaim only after expiry plus the stated clock/lease safety bound.
>   - Fresh grant delivery must not reset a still-valid prior grant.

**What this says in plain words**
The real fix for the design's weakest point. Five parts:

1. **Disjoint grants.**
   - A gateway requests a block of tokens, say 100, instead of asking per request.
   - Worked example: the tenant sends 40,000 requests per second across 10 gateways. With grants of 100, central traffic falls from 40,000 operations per second to 400. The single-owner ceiling stops being the constraint.
2. **Debit the grant centrally first.**
   - The owner subtracts 100 from the bucket at the moment it issues the grant, before any of it is spent.
   - This is what makes the grants disjoint: the tokens are gone from the central bucket, so no other gateway can be given the same ones. The total across all gateways still respects `B + r × t`.
3. **Crashed gateways strand tokens.**
   - Worked example: a gateway holding 60 unspent tokens crashes. Those 60 were already subtracted centrally, so they are lost until the grant expires. The tenant is briefly entitled to slightly less than its quota.
   - This is the cost of the model: throughput is bought with utilization.
4. **Reclaim only after expiry plus a safety bound.**
   - You cannot reclaim the moment the grant expires, because the gateway's clock may differ from the owner's.
   - Worked example: the grant expires at T. The owner reclaims at T and reissues. The gateway's clock is 2 seconds slow, so it believes the grant is valid until T+2 and keeps spending. For 2 seconds the same tokens exist twice, and the limit is exceeded.
   - Adding the maximum clock error plus a margin before reclaiming closes that window. This is the same reasoning as a lease in a distributed lock.
5. **A fresh grant must not reset a still-valid prior grant.**
   - Worked example: a gateway holds a grant with 40 tokens left and receives a new grant of 100. If the new one overwrites the old, the gateway now has 100 instead of 140, which is merely wasteful. If instead the delivery *extends* the old grant's expiry without the owner knowing, the gateway can spend past the window the owner accounted for. Grants must compose by explicit rule, not by whichever message arrived last.

---

> - IC4 stretch — exact global budget
>   - Split budget into non-overlapping regional allocations.
>   - Rebalance asynchronously while conserving the total allocation.
>   - A disconnected region can spend only its remaining allocation.
>   - Strict shared bursts require coordination and may sacrifice availability.

**What this says in plain words**
How to give a global guarantee without a global round trip on every request.

1. **Split the budget into non-overlapping regional allocations.**
   - Worked example: a global limit of 1,000 per second across 4 regions becomes 250 per second each. The sum is exactly 1,000, and no request ever leaves its region to be decided.
2. **Rebalance asynchronously, conserving the total.**
   - Worked example: one region is using 240 of 250 while another uses 10 of 250. A background process moves 100 from the idle region to the busy one, giving 350 and 150. The sum is still 1,000. This happens off the request path, so no request pays for it.
3. **A disconnected region can spend only what it holds.**
   - Worked example: the network partitions. The busy region has 350 per second and cannot ask for more. It enforces 350 and rejects the rest.
   - The system stayed correct and stayed available, at the cost of utilization: the idle region's 150 sits unused while the busy region rejects traffic.
4. **Strict shared bursts force a choice.**
   - If the requirement is "any region may use the entire 1,000 at any instant", the regions must agree in real time. During a partition they cannot agree, so you must either stop serving or violate the limit.
   - This is the CAP theorem applied concretely, and it is the reason allocation is the standard answer.

---

> - IC4 stretch — 10× load
>   - At `1M checks/s`, grants of `100` can reduce central allocation traffic toward `10,000/s` under full utilization.
>   - Measure wasted grants and skew before claiming a `100×` improvement.

**What this says in plain words**
At ten times the load, grants change the arithmetic.

1. **The headline number.**
   - 1,000,000 checks per second ÷ 100 tokens per grant = 10,000 grant requests per second centrally.
   - That fits comfortably on a single shard, so the central bottleneck disappears.
2. **The caveat, which is the actual signal.**
   - The 100× reduction assumes every grant is fully spent.
   - Worked example of it not holding: grants are 100 but a gateway averages only 30 before the grant expires. The real reduction is about 3.3×, not 100×, and 70% of the budget is being stranded and reissued.
   - "Skew" means uneven distribution across gateways: if routing is uneven, some gateways burn their grant in 10 milliseconds while others hold an untouched grant for its full life.
   - So the honest claim is "up to 100× under full utilization, and I would measure actual grant utilization and per-gateway skew before relying on it".

---

> - IC4 stretch — cost and blast radius
>   - The owner fleet is the dominant cost
>   - Grants trade owner throughput for stranded budget on gateway loss.
>   - Cell the owner fleet per tenant cohort so one shard's failover degrades a cohort, not the region.

**What this says in plain words**

1. **The owner fleet is where the money goes.**
   - Gateways exist anyway. The Redis fleet is the marginal cost created by adding rate limiting, so that is the number to reduce.
2. **Grants trade one resource for another.**
   - You spend less owner throughput and pay in stranded budget.
3. **Cell the fleet per tenant cohort.**
   - Celling means partitioning the fleet into independent groups, each serving a fixed set of tenants, with no shared components.
   - Worked example: 10,000 tenants across 10 cells of 1,000 tenants each. A cell failing degrades 10% of tenants completely, instead of every tenant being degraded slightly.
   - That trade is usually correct: one tenant cohort with a real incident is easier to detect, communicate, and recover than a fleet-wide brownout that every customer notices a little.

---

> - Weakest assumption
>   - No single tenant exceeds one shard's measured ceiling.
>   - Mitigation: alert on hottest-key load early and have the grant path ready before that tenant arrives.

**What this says in plain words**
If you say only one thing from this section, say this.
The entire baseline design rests on no tenant needing more decision throughput than one shard provides. The mitigation has two halves: an early alert, and a prepared alternative.

**Why it matters**
Naming your own weakest assumption is the highest-value 20 seconds in the interview.

- It demonstrates you know where the design breaks rather than believing it is uniformly sound.
- "Have the grant path ready before that tenant arrives" is the ownership signal: the response is prepared in advance, not designed during the incident.
- Worked example of the timeline it prevents: a large customer onboards on Monday, saturates a shard on Tuesday, and the team spends Wednesday designing grants under pressure while the customer is degraded.

---

> - Over-engineering warning
>   - Do not introduce cross-region consensus for a regional approximate quota.

**What this says in plain words**
Do not reach for a global agreement protocol when the requirement was a regional approximation.

**Why it matters**
The failure it prevents is a specific and common interview mistake: demonstrating knowledge of consensus algorithms by applying one where it is not needed.

- Worked example of the cost: adding cross-region consensus to every decision turns a 2-millisecond check into a 200-millisecond one, makes the limiter unavailable during any network partition, and delivers exactness that the requirements explicitly declared a non-goal.
- The signal an interviewer takes from it is that the candidate optimizes for appearing sophisticated rather than for the stated requirements, which is the opposite of what the level is being judged on.

### Terms in this section

| Term | Plain meaning | Why it appears here |
|---|---|---|
| Boundary burst | Traffic concentrated either side of a fixed window's reset, producing up to 2× the intended rate across the boundary. | The defining weakness of fixed windows. |
| Sliding log | Storing every admitted request's timestamp and counting those inside the window. | Exact but grows with traffic; right only for tiny limits. |
| Sliding counter | Weighting the previous window's count by how much of it still overlaps the current one. | Constant state, with error depending on how traffic was distributed. |
| Leaky bucket | Queueing admitted work and releasing it at a fixed rate. | Smooths downstream load at the cost of waiting. |
| Deciding variable | The single question whose answer picks between options. | Required by the source's comparison rule; here it is whether bursts are contractual. |
| Disjoint grant | An exclusive block of tokens debited centrally and handed to one gateway to spend locally. | The fix for the hot tenant and the single-owner ceiling. |
| Stranded tokens | Granted tokens held by a crashed gateway, unusable until expiry. | The cost the grant model pays for throughput. |
| Lease safety bound | The extra wait before reclaiming an expired grant, covering the maximum clock disagreement. | Prevents the same tokens existing in two places at once. |
| Grant utilization | The fraction of a granted block actually spent before it expires. | Determines whether the claimed traffic reduction is real. |
| Skew | Uneven distribution of load across gateways or shards. | Makes some grants burn instantly and others expire untouched. |

## Level signal — IC3 baseline vs IC4 stretch

One row per interview phase. What must land, what raises it, and the specific observable that separates the two.

---

> | Phase | IC3 must land | IC4 adds | Separating signal |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Requirements | Burst allowed, regional scope, tenant-plus-route key, envelope invariant | Capacity rules versus security rules, each with a failure policy | Names the degradation contract before being asked |
> | Estimates | Bucket state, operations per second, shard count | Memory is not the constraint; the hot key picks the scaling path | A number changes a decision |
> | Architecture | Gateway, owner, policy plane with one decision trace | Snapshot lag and single-owner ceiling as the price of each split | Every component has a mechanism and a price |
> | Deep dive | Atomic script, clock clamp, last-token race | Ambiguous timeout and the multi-bucket hash-slot constraint | Reasons about what atomicity does not cover |
> | Operations | Failure table, emergency budget, shadow rollout | Gateways stay static-stable under policy-plane loss; cohort cells | Volunteers control-plane outage behavior |
> | Wrap | Recap and burst-versus-exactness tradeoff | Single-owner ceiling named as the weakest assumption | Identifies the flaw before the interviewer does |

**What this says in plain words**
A self-assessment grid. Read the middle column as a checklist and the right column as what actually distinguishes a stronger answer.

**Why the fourth column is the useful one**
The difference between the levels is almost never *more content*. It is a different kind of statement.

- **Row: Requirements.**
  - Must land: bursts are allowed, the scope is regional, the key is tenant plus route class, and the guarantee is an envelope not a per-second count.
  - Adds: rules are of two kinds, and each declares its own failure policy.
  - Separating signal: you say what happens when the limiter cannot decide *before* anyone asks. That converts an obvious follow-up into something you already own.
- **Row: Estimates.**
  - Must land: the three numbers.
  - Adds: interpreting them, namely that memory rules itself out and the hot key determines the scaling path.
  - Separating signal: "a number changes a decision". Worked example of failing it: reciting "50,000 buckets, 10 megabytes" and then moving on. Nothing was decided, so the estimate was decoration.
- **Row: Architecture.**
  - Must land: three components and one traced request.
  - Adds: the price of each split, named next to the split.
  - Separating signal: every component states both the mechanism it provides and the cost it introduces. Worked example of failing it: "gateways cache the policy locally so they are fast", with no mention of snapshot lag.
- **Row: Deep dive.**
  - Must land: the script, the clock clamp, and the last-token race.
  - Adds: the ambiguous timeout, and the cluster constraint on multi-bucket debits.
  - Separating signal: reasoning about what atomicity does *not* cover. Worked example of the difference: "the script is atomic so it is correct" versus "the script is atomic within one owner's execution, which does not survive a failover, so a failover can hand back roughly 200 milliseconds of debits".
- **Row: Operations.**
  - Must land: the failure table, the bounded emergency budget, and shadow rollout.
  - Adds: static stability, and cohort cells.
  - Static stability means the system keeps behaving correctly when its control plane disappears, because it is already holding everything it needs and does not have to fetch anything to keep working.
  - Separating signal: volunteering what happens when the control plane is down, rather than answering it when challenged.
- **Row: Wrap.**
  - Must land: a recap and the central tradeoff.
  - Adds: the weakest assumption named explicitly.
  - Separating signal: you find the flaw before the interviewer does. Worked example of the alternative: the interviewer asks at minute 50 "what if one tenant sends 40,000 requests per second?", and the answer becomes a discovery rather than a prepared position.

**How to use this table in preparation**
Rehearse the answer, then score yourself only on the fourth column. Every row is a yes-or-no observable, not a matter of impression.

## Interview pushback and validation

Eight challenges an interviewer can raise, each with a baseline and a stretch answer, then seven things to test.
Read each one as: what is really being asked, then why the given answer works.

---

> - Q1: Why is a concurrent map insufficient?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Refill and debit are a compound update.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Local atomicity still does not cover failover or independent regions.

**What is really being asked**
Why not just use a thread-safe map inside the gateway process? Java's `ConcurrentHashMap`, or the equivalent.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
A concurrent map makes each individual operation safe, but refill-and-debit is several operations that must be indivisible together.

- Worked example of the failure with a thread-safe map:
  - Thread A reads tokens = 1.
  - Thread B reads tokens = 1.
  - Thread A computes refill, subtracts 1, writes 0.
  - Thread B computes refill, subtracts 1, writes 0.
  - Both requests were admitted against a single token. Every individual map operation was correctly synchronized.
- The fix within one process is an atomic compute-and-replace, which the map does offer. But that only solves the single-process case, which was never the problem.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Even with perfect in-process atomicity, the map is in *one process*. Eight gateways hold eight independent maps, which is the original `N×` multiplication.
And the same limit applies to the shared version: atomicity at one owner does not survive a failover or extend to another region.

---

> - Q2: Can retries exceed the quota?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - An ambiguous debit retry can consume twice and reject legitimate work.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - A lost replicated debit or duplicate token grant can instead over-admit.

**What is really being asked**
Do retries break the guarantee, and in which direction?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
A retried ambiguous debit charges twice. The error is in the *strict* direction: the tenant is throttled below its real quota.

- Worked example: quota 1,000 per second. 5% of checks time out and are retried. Roughly 50 extra tokens per second are consumed, so the tenant is effectively limited to about 950 and its engineers see rejections they cannot explain.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
The interesting part is that the error can also go the *other* way, from a different cause.

- A failover loses recent debits, handing back budget.
- A duplicate token grant hands the same tokens to two gateways.
- Worked example combining both: retries over-charge by 5% while a failover under-charges by 200 milliseconds of debits. These do not cancel out predictably; they are two independent error sources in opposite directions.
- The strong version of this answer names the direction of each error and says which one is acceptable: over-charging is annoying, over-admitting is dangerous.

---

> - Q3: What happens when the entire limiter fails?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Select strict rejection or bounded emergency admission per policy.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Reserve capacity for recovery operations without bypassing tenant authorization.

**What is really being asked**
Does the design have a stated answer for total failure, or was fail-open assumed?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Per rule, declared in advance. Security rules reject, capacity rules admit a bounded emergency allowance.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
During an outage, some traffic matters more than normal traffic.

- Worked example: the recovery runbook requires calling the configuration API to roll back a bad policy. If emergency mode throttles everything uniformly, the rollback call is throttled and you cannot recover.
- The fix is a reserved allowance for a small set of identified recovery operations.
- The second half of the sentence is the guard: "without bypassing tenant authorization". The reserved path is a capacity reservation, not an authentication bypass. A reserved path that skips authorization is a backdoor that exists permanently and is reachable by claiming to be a recovery operation.

---

> - Q4: The requirement changes to exactly 100 attempts in any minute.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Use a sliding event log for this bounded attempt rate.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Coordinate the authoritative history across regions or explicitly reduce availability.

**What is really being asked**
Can you notice when the requirement change invalidates your algorithm, rather than defending the design you already built?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
"Exactly 100 in any minute" is precisely the guarantee a token bucket does not give. Do not try to tune the bucket into it.

- The token bucket permits `B + r × t`, so with any burst at all it will exceed 100 in some minute.
- A sliding log gives the exact answer, and at 100 attempts per minute the state is 100 timestamps, which is trivial.
- Worked example of why the volume changes the verdict: the same sliding log at 100,000 requests per second would be 6,000,000 timestamps. The algorithm did not become correct or incorrect; the volume decided whether it is affordable.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
"Exactly" plus "globally" forces a choice.

- Either the regions coordinate on an authoritative history, which costs a cross-region round trip per attempt.
- Or you accept reduced availability: a region that cannot reach the authority refuses attempts rather than guessing.
- For a login-attempt limit, refusing during a partition is usually the correct product decision, and saying that out loud is the strong version of the answer.

---

> - Q5: Can we shard one tenant's key eight ways?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Independent full budgets multiply the allowance.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Disjoint allocations preserve a sum but trade utilization for availability.

**What is really being asked**
This is bait. Sharding the hot key is the obvious response to the hot-tenant problem, and it is wrong in the obvious form.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Eight independent buckets each with capacity `B` and rate `r` give `8B + 8rt`, which is eight times the promise.

- Worked example: the limit is 1,000 per second. Split into 8 buckets of 1,000 each and the tenant can send 8,000. You did not shard the limit; you multiplied it.
- The instant check is the invariant from the requirements section, which is exactly why it is worth memorizing.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
There is a correct version: split the *budget*, not the bucket.

- Worked example: 8 buckets of 125 per second each. The sum is 1,000, so the guarantee holds.
- The cost is utilization. If the tenant's traffic hashes unevenly, one sub-bucket exhausts its 125 while another sits idle, and the tenant is rejected while 875 per second of its own budget is unused.
- That is the general shape of the tradeoff: splitting a budget preserves correctness and loses utilization, and the loss grows with skew.

---

> - Q6: Why not a queue?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Rejection and delayed execution have different client contracts.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Queue bounds must include wait-time deadlines and cancellation cost.

**What is really being asked**
Why reject work instead of holding it until capacity exists?

**The baseline answer, expanded**
They are different promises to the caller.

- Rejection: "not now, try later, and here is when". The client keeps control and can decide to give up, degrade, or retry.
- Queueing: "accepted, it will happen eventually". The client is now waiting on you with no ability to act.
- Worked example: a synchronous read with a 2-second client timeout. Queued behind 50 requests at 10 per second, it is released after 5 seconds, by which time the client gave up 3 seconds earlier. The work was done, the resources were spent, and nobody received a result. A rejection at time zero would have been strictly better for everyone.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
If you do queue, the bound cannot be depth alone.

- A wait-time deadline: drop anything that has already waited longer than the client's timeout, because completing it produces nothing.
- Cancellation cost: when a client disconnects, the queued work should be discarded, which requires actually detecting the disconnect.
- Worked example of getting this wrong: a queue bounded only at 10,000 entries, draining at 10 per second. An entry at the back waits 1,000 seconds. Every client timed out long ago, and the system spends 100% of its capacity producing results nobody is waiting for. The queue is full, so new work is rejected too, and the service is effectively down while looking busy.

---

> - Q7 (premise reversal): Why a shared owner at all?
>   - Put the limit in each gateway and divide the quota by fleet size.
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - The sum is only correct with fixed membership
>     - Autoscaling, uneven routing, and restarts break it silently.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - That is the grant model, and it is only sound when grants are leased, expire, and are reclaimed centrally.

**What is really being asked**
This is the premise-reversal question, and it is the most valuable one in the set.
The interviewer attacks the foundation: maybe the shared owner, the source of all the complexity, is unnecessary. Divide 1,000 by 10 gateways and give each 100 locally.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
The arithmetic is correct only if the fleet size is exactly what you assumed, at every instant. Three ways that fails, and all three are silent:

1. **Autoscaling.**
   - The fleet scales from 10 to 40 during a traffic spike. Each still enforces 100, so the total is 4,000 against a limit of 1,000. The limiter loosens exactly when load is highest.
2. **Uneven routing.**
   - Load balancing is never perfectly even. If 60% of a tenant's traffic lands on 2 of 10 gateways, those two reject at 100 each while the other eight sit at 20% utilization. The tenant is rejected at roughly 400 per second against a quota of 1,000.
3. **Restarts.**
   - A gateway that restarts loses its counter and starts fresh, the original in-memory failure.

- The word *silently* is the point. None of these log an error. The limit is simply wrong, in a different direction each time, and nothing reports it.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Rather than dismissing it, concede that the idea is right and the naive form is what fails.

- Local decisions are exactly what the grant model does, and it is the recommended answer for a hot tenant.
- It becomes sound when three properties are added:
  - **Leased**: the allowance is issued by a central authority, not assumed from a headcount.
  - **Expiring**: an allowance held by a crashed gateway becomes reclaimable.
  - **Centrally reclaimed**: the authority tracks what is outstanding, so the total is enforced by issuance.
- The strong closing line: the difference between the broken version and the correct one is not the local decision, it is whether the fleet's total allowance is *asserted* or *issued*.

---

> - Q8: The policy database is down for an hour.
>   - What do tenants see?
>   - **IC3 baseline**
>     - Decisions continue from the last-known snapshot
>     - Only edits return `503`.
>   - **IC4 stretch**
>     - Alarm on snapshot age and reconcile versions across the fleet before edits resume.

**What is really being asked**
Does a control-plane outage become a data-plane outage? This tests whether the split you drew is real.

**The baseline answer, expanded**
Tenants see nothing at all on the request path.

- Worked example: at 100,000 decisions per second for one hour, that is 360,000,000 decisions served correctly from snapshots held in gateway memory.
- Only administrators are affected: attempting to save a limit change returns `503`.
- This is the entire payoff of the control-plane split, and it is worth stating in exactly these terms.

**The stretch answer, expanded**
Recovery is where the hidden risk lives.

1. **Alarm on snapshot age.**
   - Without it, a control plane that has been silently dead for three days is discovered only when someone tries to make a change.
   - Worked example: the snapshot age metric crosses 15 minutes and pages someone, long before anyone notices by accident.
2. **Reconcile versions before edits resume.**
   - After an hour of failure, gateways may hold different versions, because some restarted and pulled a bundled default while others kept version 47.
   - Worked example of skipping reconciliation: an administrator edits based on version 47, but a third of the fleet is on version 45. The edit is applied on top of state that some gateways never had, producing an outcome nobody intended.
   - Confirm the fleet has converged first, then reopen edits.

> - Validation scenarios
>   - Inject time to test refill boundaries and clock regression.
>   - Race requests against the last token.
>   - Change capacity while a bucket is depleted.
>   - Lose a response after the owner debit.
>   - Fail over with a lagging replica.
>   - Restart a gateway holding a partially spent grant.
>   - Stop the policy API and confirm decisions continue on the stale snapshot.

**What this says in plain words**
Seven tests. Each one maps to a specific failure discussed earlier, which is what makes the list credible rather than generic.

1. **Inject time.**
   - The clock must be a parameter the test can control, not a direct call to the system clock. If the code reads the clock directly, refill behavior cannot be tested at all.
   - Test both directions: forward jumps granting a bounded burst, backward jumps not corrupting state.
2. **Race requests against the last token.**
   - Worked example: set tokens to 1, fire 100 concurrent requests of cost 1, assert that exactly 1 is allowed and 99 are denied. This is the direct test of the linearization point.
3. **Change capacity while a bucket is depleted.**
   - Tests the clamp on edit, and confirms the bucket key stayed stable so the edit did not mint a fresh full bucket.
4. **Lose a response after the owner debit.**
   - Simulates the ambiguous timeout, and verifies that the declared degradation policy is applied rather than a blind retry.
5. **Fail over with a lagging replica.**
   - Confirms the loss is bounded and observable, and that the system does not claim exactness it does not have.
6. **Restart a gateway holding a partially spent grant.**
   - Tests that stranded tokens are reclaimed only after expiry plus the safety bound, and that reclamation does not happen early.
7. **Stop the policy API.**
   - The direct test of the Q8 claim. Decisions must continue, and snapshot age must start rising visibly.

**Why it matters**
A validation list is where a design stops being a drawing. Each item names a mechanism that could be wrong and the observation that would reveal it.

## Recall card

Five one-liners and a four-sentence closing script. These are memorized verbatim, not improvised.

---

> - “I use an atomic token bucket keyed by authenticated tenant and route.”

**What this says in plain words**
The design in one sentence. Four decisions compressed: token bucket, atomic, keyed by tenant, keyed by route class.

**Why it matters**
Every word is load-bearing.

- *Atomic* excludes read-then-write.
- *Authenticated* excludes a caller-supplied identity.
- *Route* excludes a raw URL.
- Drop any one word and you have described a design with a known hole.

---

> - “The normal invariant is a burst-plus-refill envelope.”

**What this says in plain words**
States the guarantee accurately: at most `B + r × t` over any window of duration `t`.

**Why it matters**
It is a deliberate act of *not* overclaiming.

- Worked example: `B = 20`, `r = 10` per second. In one particular second the system may admit 30. That is correct behavior, and this sentence is what makes it correct rather than a bug you have to explain later.
- Claiming "at most 10 per second" and then being shown a second with 30 in it costs you the credibility of everything else you said.

---

> - “Owner failure invokes an explicit strict or bounded-emergency policy.”

**What this says in plain words**
When the counter is unreachable, behavior is not improvised. Each rule already declared reject, or admit up to a bounded allowance.

**Why it matters**
The words *explicit* and *bounded* are both defences.

- *Explicit* rules out fail-open by omission.
- *Bounded* rules out unlimited fail-open, which is not protection at all.

---

> - “The policy plane can die; gateways keep deciding on the last snapshot.”

**What this says in plain words**
A control-plane outage does not become a request-path outage.

**Why it matters**
It is the static-stability claim in nine words.

- Worked example: one hour of policy-database downtime, 360,000,000 decisions served correctly, and the only visible effect is that administrators cannot save changes.

---

> - “The first scaling issue is a hot bucket; disjoint grants change central traffic and recovery semantics.”

**What this says in plain words**
Names the weakest point and the fix in one sentence, including the fact that the fix changes more than throughput.

**Why it matters**
The last three words are what make it an ownership statement rather than a slogan.

- "Changes recovery semantics" acknowledges that grants introduce stranded tokens and a reclamation rule with a clock-safety bound.
- Presenting the fix together with its new costs is the difference between knowing the technique and having thought about operating it.

---

> - Wrap-up script
>   - “The design is a regional token bucket per tenant and route, with one atomic owner per bucket and versioned policy pushed to gateways.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence one: the architecture. Algorithm, key, ownership model, and configuration distribution.

**Why it matters**
The recap has to stand alone, because the interviewer may be writing their notes from these four sentences alone.

---

> -   - “The healthy owner enforces burst plus rate times elapsed time, while failover and timeout policies declare any additional admission budget.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence two: the guarantee, and the honest caveat attached to it.

- Normal operation gives `B + r × t`.
- Failover and timeouts can admit more, and the amount is declared rather than accidental.

**Why it matters**
Stating the guarantee and its exception in one breath is the pattern the whole source file follows: every claim carries its defeater.

---

> -   - “The main tradeoff is a burst envelope instead of an exact window, which buys one round trip per decision and no cross-region coordination.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence three: what you gave up and what you bought.

- Given up: exactness.
- Bought: a single local round trip per decision, and no cross-region traffic at all.

**Why it matters**
It shows the approximation was chosen, not conceded.

- Worked example of the alternative's price: exactness across regions costs about 100 milliseconds per decision against a 2-millisecond budget, plus unavailability during a partition.

---

> -   - “My weakest assumption is that no tenant exceeds one shard's ceiling; I would validate the hottest key first and keep the grant path ready.”

**What this says in plain words**
Sentence four: the flaw, the first thing you would measure, and the prepared response.

**Why it matters**
Ending on your own weakest assumption is counter-intuitive and correct.

- It is the last thing the interviewer hears, and it demonstrates that you evaluated your design rather than only building it.
- "Validate the hottest key first" gives a concrete next action, which is what distinguishes an owner from an author.
