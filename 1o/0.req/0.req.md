### B.3 Behavioral guidance — PAR / STAR

- What it is about
  - How you present your experience and the value you bring.
  - Past success and behavior are what matter.
  - They look for things you have already done to indicate how you will perform once hired.
- Question shape
  - Behavioral-based questions, often expressed as "what if" or "tell me about a time" scenarios.
  - Draw from actual experience even when they propose a "what if."
- Answer format
  - **PAR** — Problem, Action, Result
  - **STAR** — Situation/Task, Action, Result
  - Here was the situation, task, or problem (explain the situation and the need).
  - Here was the action I took to correct or fix, and why I decided to pursue this course.
  - Here was the end result — learning, success, growth; demonstrate value.
- Delivery
  - You are telling a story, so be engaging and excited about it.
  - Pick specific experiences, projects, and events that demonstrate the most value.
  - Include challenges (challenging people included), obstacles, failures, wins, learning experiences.
  - Refer to the OCI Core Values for context and ideas; they will ask questions directly related to these values.
  - **Practice saying it** in PAR or STAR format.

### B.4 Tech guidance — Control Plane prep

- Links and resources
  - Coding practice
    - https://www.hackerrank.com/dashboard (listed in the most recent OCI Tech Interview Prep)
    - https://edabit.com/challenges (pick language and difficulty on the side)
    - https://leetcode.com/explore/ (coding, data structures/algorithms, system design, company prep for Amazon, MS, Google, FB; no Oracle/OCI)

  - Books
    - Cracking the Coding Interview
    - Elements of Programming Interviews
  - System design
    - Highscalability.com — highly informative and extremely useful
- Common topics to focus on
  - **Algorithms and data structures**
    - Searching and sorting algorithms with time and space complexity — average, best, and worst case.
    - Hash tables.
    - Binary tree / n-ary tree / tries.
    - Heaps.
    - Linked lists.
    - Stacks / queues.
    - Complexity analysis for lookup / insertion / deletion into various data structures.
    - Recursion vs iterative programming.
  - **Operating systems and container basics**
    - Threads.
    - Memory organization / management.
    - Program execution basics.
    - Container basics; running in VMs vs running in containers.
    - Linux fundamentals; basic commands like grep, awk, uniq.
  - **System design**
    - Horizontal vs vertical scaling; pros and cons.
    - Availability, scalability, caching, load balancers, exponential backoff, two-phase commits.
    - Read up on how popular services like Twitter, Facebook Live, Instagram are designed; focus on the challenges they faced and how they overcame them.
    - Be prepared to **define SLAs for your service**
      - Latency

      - Security

      - Vector clock
      - Eventual consistency

<a id="req-b5"></a>

### B.5 Coding-round approach and scoring

- Approach if it is a coding problem
  1. They will begin with some type of technical discussion, more than likely; topic not fixed.
  2. They will present technical problems to solve.
     - Example: what algorithm would you use to sort this table? Follow-up questions about that algorithm.
  3. **Ask questions and qualify everything before you start to code.**
     - Points are awarded per question; you will not get all the points without qualifying questions.
  4. If you do not know something, say so and describe the resources you use in that situation.
  5. If you would reach out to a mentor or senior engineer, treat the interviewer that way.
     - They will provide guidance to see if you can follow instruction and figure it out.
  6. Problems may be perceived as out of your experience level; they want to help you.
     - Ask questions, clarify before you begin, think aloud for feedback as you go.
  7. Clearly and meticulously discuss your solution before coding, including time/space complexity.
  8. Use the same approach for the test cases you run on your code.
- You are getting points for
  1. Qualifying questions prior (quality of your questions)
  2. Your solution
  3. Your test cases

<a id="req-c3"></a>

### C.3 Behavioral prep — STAR method

- Purpose
  - Questions prompt a real-life example of how you handled a kind of situation in the past.
  - They reveal how you cope with change, approach learning, and overcome challenges and obstacles.
- STAR
  - **S — Situation.** What situation were you in, or what task did you need to accomplish?
  - **T — Task.** What goal were you working toward?
  - **A — Action.** What specific steps did you take to address the situation?
  - **R — Result.** What happened because of your action? What did you learn?
- Preparation
  - Arrive with examples of challenging situations, with details that highlight your experience.
  - Show ways you have succeeded, failed, taken risks, and grown.
  - Align examples with the OCI Core Values ([C.5](#req-c5)).
- Example questions
  - Tell me about a time you had to prioritize your customers' needs. What was the situation? What did you do and how did it go? What would you do differently knowing what you know now?
  - Describe a work situation where you faced a big obstacle. How did you overcome it?
  - There is more than one way to solve a problem. Give an example from your recent work experience that illustrates this.
  - What do you do when priorities change quickly? Give one example of when this happened.
- Why STAR
  - A simple checklist framework for both candidates and interviewers.
  - Helps candidates walk through stories in a logical, clear manner.
- Interviewer probes per STAR section

| Section | What the interviewer looks for | Probe questions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Situation | The challenge or situation the candidate was in | What happened? How did this come about? Who was involved? What was the main issue? |
| Task | What the candidate was trying to achieve | What responsibility did you take on? Did your manager assign it? Did you take it on your own? Did others have tasks as well? |
| Action | What the candidate did, why, and the alternatives | What did you do first? How did the person/situation respond? What did you do next? |
| Result | The outcome of the candidate's actions | What was the end result? Was your manager satisfied? Did you continue to handle this issue? Were you given new responsibilities because of this? |

<a id="req-c4"></a>

### C.4 Technical prep — three steps

- Framing
  - OCI tests coding skills and problem-solving ability, plus culture fit.
  - They want to see how you think, how you solve a problem, and how you embody the values.
  - Resources are optional; they do not expect days of preparation.
- **Step 1: Refresh core CS concepts**
  - Data structures: lists, stacks, queues, trees, hash tables, sets, tries, graphs.
  - Algorithms: sorting, recursion, searching, graph and tree algorithms, dynamic programming.
  - Time and space efficiency (big-O analysis).
  - Reading list
    - Cracking the Coding Interview (most detailed guide).
    - Introduction to Algorithms (CLRS), augmented by The Algorithm Design Manual (Skiena), Algorithms 4th ed. (Sedgewick and Wayne), Algorithms (Dasgupta, Papadimitriou, Vazirani), or Jeff Erickson's algorithms notes.
    - Programming Pearls (Bentley); The Art of Computer Programming (Knuth) for depth.
    - Open Data Structures textbook.
- **Step 2: Practice problems**
  - Format: you are presented a problem, asked to present a solution in code, and, time permitting, create a test case.
  - Any language you are comfortable with.
  - Practice sites: https://hackerrank.com, http://www.spoj.com, https://onlinejudge.org.
  - Example problems from OCI engineers
    1. FizzBuzz: print 1 to 100; "Fizz" for multiples of three, "Buzz" for multiples of five, "FizzBuzz" for both.
    2. Given two values n1 and n2 in a Binary Search Tree, find the Lowest Common Ancestor; both values exist in the tree.
  - Practice advice
    - Write solutions long-hand on paper to mimic interview conditions without IDE completion.
    - Review whatever you struggle to remember.
    - Rehearse how you communicate your work as you go; they want to understand how you solve problems, not just see code.
- **Step 3: Ace the interview**
  - Ask clarifying questions about requirements; speak up if the question is not 100% clear.
    - Imposing a restriction on input can dramatically simplify a problem.
  - Talk through your plan before jumping into code; communicate, think out loud, make it collaborative.
  - If you get stuck, ask a question.
  - Simple brute-force first, then iterate for time and/or space efficiency is perfectly acceptable.
    - Something working with reasonable improvement suggestions beats an incomplete perfect solution.
  - After writing, walk through a simple test case if time permits; otherwise talk about how you would test.
  - If the list feels intimidating or you are not fresh, you may request more time before continuing the process.
- Other pointers in the pack
  - Video-interview tips, common interview mistakes, and Talent Advisor tips (external links in the PDF).
  - OCI mission and goals links (external).
